* Definition and Geometric Meaning of Measurable Functions

** The Role of Measurable Functions
In topology, the natural structure-preserving mappings are *continuous functions* (preimages of open sets are open).
In measure theory and probability, the natural structure-preserving mappings are *measurable functions* (preimages of measurable sets are measurable).
In probability theory, a measurable function defined on a sample space $\Omega$ is called a *random variable* $X : \Omega \to \mathbb{R}$.

** Formal Definition
#+begin_quote
*Definition (Measurable Mapping):*
Let $A \subset \mathbb{R}^n$. A mapping $f : A \to \mathbb{R}^m$ is said to be *measurable* (with respect to $\mathrm{Leb}(\mathbb{R}^n)$) if for every open set $G \subset \mathbb{R}^m$, the preimage
$$ f^{-1}(G) = \{x \in A : f(x) \in G\} $$
is a Lebesgue measurable set, i.e., $f^{-1}(G) \in \mathrm{Leb}(\mathbb{R}^n)$.
#+end_quote

*Remarks:*
1. If $f : A \to \mathbb{R}^m$ is measurable, then the domain $A = f^{-1}(\mathbb{R}^m)$ is necessarily measurable because $\mathbb{R}^m$ is open.
2. If $f : A \to \mathbb{R}^m$ is measurable and $B \subset A$ is measurable, the restriction $f|_B : B \to \mathbb{R}^m$ is measurable.
3. For extended real-valued functions $f : A \to \dot{\mathbb{R}} = [-\infty, +\infty]$, $f$ is measurable if $f^{-1}(G) \in \mathrm{Leb}(\mathbb{R}^n)$ for all open $G \subset \mathbb{R}$ and $f^{-1}(\{+\infty\}), f^{-1}(\{-\infty\}) \in \mathrm{Leb}(\mathbb{R}^n)$.

* Equivalent Characterizations for Real-Valued Functions

** The Four Sublevel/Superlevel Criteria
For functions taking values in the extended real numbers $\dot{\mathbb{R}}$, checking preimages of all open sets is cumbersome. Fortunately, testing single rays suffices.

#+begin_quote
*Theorem 2.9 (Fundamental Characterization):*
Let $A \in \mathrm{Leb}(\mathbb{R}^n)$ and $f : A \to \dot{\mathbb{R}}$. The following conditions are equivalent:
1. $\{x \in A : f(x) > a\} \in \mathrm{Leb}(\mathbb{R}^n)$ for all $a \in \mathbb{R}$.
2. $\{x \in A : f(x) \ge a\} \in \mathrm{Leb}(\mathbb{R}^n)$ for all $a \in \mathbb{R}$.
3. $\{x \in A : f(x) < a\} \in \mathrm{Leb}(\mathbb{R}^n)$ for all $a \in \mathbb{R}$.
4. $\{x \in A : f(x) \le a\} \in \mathrm{Leb}(\mathbb{R}^n)$ for all $a \in \mathbb{R}$.
5. $f$ is measurable.
#+end_quote

*Proof of Equivalences:*
- *(1) $\implies$ (2):*
  Notice that $f(x) \ge a \iff f(x) > a - \frac{1}{k}$ for all $k \in \mathbb{N}$. Therefore:
  $$ \{x \in A : f(x) \ge a\} = \bigcap_{k=1}^\infty \left\{x \in A : f(x) > a - \frac{1}{k}\right\}. $$
  Since $\mathrm{Leb}(\mathbb{R}^n)$ is closed under countable intersections, (2) holds.
- *(2) $\implies$ (3):*
  The set $\{x \in A : f(x) < a\} = A \setminus \{x \in A : f(x) \ge a\}$.
  Since the complement of a measurable set within $A$ is measurable, (3) holds.
- *(3) $\implies$ (4):*
  Analogously, $f(x) \le a \iff f(x) < a + \frac{1}{k}$ for all $k \in \mathbb{N}$, so:
  $$ \{x \in A : f(x) \le a\} = \bigcap_{k=1}^\infty \left\{x \in A : f(x) < a + \frac{1}{k}\right\}. $$
- *(4) $\implies$ (1):*
  $\{x \in A : f(x) > a\} = A \setminus \{x \in A : f(x) \le a\}$.
- *Connecting to open sets (5):*
  Every open interval $]a, b[ = ]-\infty, b[ \cap ]a, +\infty[$, so
  $$ f^{-1}(]a, b[) = \{x : f(x) < b\} \cap \{x : f(x) > a\} \in \mathrm{Leb}(\mathbb{R}^n). $$
  Since every open set in $\mathbb{R}$ is a countable union of disjoint open intervals $G = \bigcup_k I_k$:
  $$ f^{-1}(G) = \bigcup_k f^{-1}(I_k) \in \mathrm{Leb}(\mathbb{R}^n). \quad \blacksquare $$

* Continuous Functions and Preimages of Borel Sets

** Continuous Functions are Measurable
#+begin_quote
*Theorem 2.3 & 2.4:*
1. If $A \in \mathrm{Leb}(\mathbb{R}^n)$ and $f : A \to \mathbb{R}^m$ is continuous, then $f$ is measurable.
2. If $f : A \to \mathbb{R}^m$ is measurable, then for *every Borel set* $B \in \mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^m)$:
   $$ f^{-1}(B) \in \mathrm{Leb}(\mathbb{R}^n). $$
#+end_quote

*Proof of (2):*
Consider the family of target sets whose preimage is measurable:
$$ \mathcal{H} = \{S \subset \mathbb{R}^m : f^{-1}(S) \in \mathrm{Leb}(\mathbb{R}^n)\}. $$
- $\mathcal{H}$ contains all open sets by definition of measurability.
- $f^{-1}(\mathbb{R}^m \setminus S) = A \setminus f^{-1}(S) \in \mathrm{Leb}(\mathbb{R}^n)$, so $\mathcal{H}$ is closed under complements.
- $f^{-1}\left(\bigcup_k S_k\right) = \bigcup_k f^{-1}(S_k) \in \mathrm{Leb}(\mathbb{R}^n)$, so $\mathcal{H}$ is closed under countable unions.
Thus $\mathcal{H}$ is a \(\sigma\)-algebra containing all open sets. Since $\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^m)$ is the /smallest/ such \(\sigma\)-algebra, $\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^m) \subset \mathcal{H}$. $\blacksquare$

*Immediate Corollary:*
For any single point $y \in \mathbb{R}^m$, $\{y\}$ is closed (hence Borel), so the level set $f^{-1}(\{y\}) = \{x \in A : f(x) = y\}$ is always measurable.

** Composition of Functions
#+begin_quote
*Theorem 2.6 (Composition with Continuous / Borel Functions):*
Let $f : A \to \mathbb{R}^m$ be measurable ($A \subset \mathbb{R}^n$). If $g : \mathbb{R}^m \to \mathbb{R}^k$ is continuous (or Borel measurable), then the composite function
$$ h = g \circ f : A \to \mathbb{R}^k $$
is measurable.
#+end_quote
*Proof:* For any open $U \subset \mathbb{R}^k$, $g^{-1}(U)$ is open (or Borel). By Theorem 2.4, $(g \circ f)^{-1}(U) = f^{-1}(g^{-1}(U)) \in \mathrm{Leb}(\mathbb{R}^n)$. $\blacksquare$

> [!WARNING]
> *Subtlety on Composition:* The composition of two *Lebesgue* measurable functions $g \circ f$ is *not* guaranteed to be Lebesgue measurable! If $f$ is only Lebesgue measurable, $g^{-1}(G)$ might be Lebesgue measurable but not Borel, so $f^{-1}(g^{-1}(G))$ could fail to be measurable. The outer function $g$ must be Borel measurable (which holds whenever $g$ is continuous).

* Algebraic Operations and Coordinate Mappings

** Vector-Valued Mappings
#+begin_quote
*Theorem 2.7:*
A mapping $f = (f_1, \dots, f_m) : A \to \mathbb{R}^m$ is measurable if and only if each coordinate component $f_j : A \to \mathbb{R}$ is measurable for all $j \in \{1, \dots, m\}$.
#+end_quote

** Arithmetic Operations
#+begin_quote
*Theorem 2.8:*
Let $f, g : A \to \dot{\mathbb{R}}$ be measurable. Then:
1. $c f$ is measurable for any constant $c \in \mathbb{R}$.
2. $f + g$ and $f - g$ are measurable (where defined, avoiding $\infty - \infty$).
3. $f \cdot g$ is measurable.
4. $f / g$ is measurable (where $g(x) \ne 0$).
5. $|f|$ is measurable.
6. $\max(f, g)$ and $\min(f, g)$ are measurable.
#+end_quote

*Proof that $f + g$ is measurable:*
For any $a \in \mathbb{R}$, $f(x) + g(x) > a \iff f(x) > a - g(x)$.
Between any two real numbers there exists a rational number $q \in \mathbb{Q}$.
Thus $f(x) > q$ and $q > a - g(x)$, meaning $g(x) > a - q$.
Therefore:
$$ \{x \in A : f(x) + g(x) > a\} = \bigcup_{q \in \mathbb{Q}} \left( \{x \in A : f(x) > q\} \cap \{x \in A : g(x) > a - q\} \right). $$
Since $\mathbb{Q}$ is countable, this is a countable union of measurable sets, hence measurable! $\blacksquare$

** Positive and Negative Parts
Every function $f : A \to \dot{\mathbb{R}}$ decomposes into non-negative parts:
- *Positive part:* $f^+(x) = \max\{f(x), 0\} \ge 0$.
- *Negative part:* $f^-(x) = \max\{-f(x), 0\} = -\min\{f(x), 0\} \ge 0$.
Then:
$$ f(x) = f^+(x) - f^-(x) \quad \text{and} \quad |f(x)| = f^+(x) + f^-(x). $$
If $f$ is measurable, both $f^+$ and $f^-$ are non-negative measurable functions. This decomposition is the foundation of Lebesgue integration!

* Limits of Sequences of Measurable Functions

** Supremum, Infimum, $\limsup$, and $\liminf$
#+begin_quote
*Theorem 2.14 (Closure under Pointwise Operations):*
Let $f_j : A \to \dot{\mathbb{R}}$ ($j \in \mathbb{N}$) be measurable functions. Then the following functions defined pointwise on $A$ are measurable:
1. $g_1(x) = \sup_{j \in \mathbb{N}} f_j(x)$
2. $g_2(x) = \inf_{j \in \mathbb{N}} f_j(x)$
3. $g_3(x) = \limsup_{j \to \infty} f_j(x)$
4. $g_4(x) = \liminf_{j \to \infty} f_j(x)$
#+end_quote

*Proof:*
- For the supremum: $\sup_{j} f_j(x) > a \iff \exists j \in \mathbb{N}$ such that $f_j(x) > a$.
  $$ \{x \in A : \sup_j f_j(x) > a\} = \bigcup_{j=1}^\infty \{x \in A : f_j(x) > a\} \in \mathrm{Leb}(\mathbb{R}^n). $$
- For the infimum: $\inf_j f_j(x) = -\sup_j (-f_j(x))$.
- For $\limsup$ and $\liminf$:
  Recall the analytical definitions:
  $$ \limsup_{j\to\infty} f_j(x) = \inf_{k \in \mathbb{N}} \left( \sup_{j \ge k} f_j(x) \right), \qquad \liminf_{j\to\infty} f_j(x) = \sup_{k \in \mathbb{N}} \left( \inf_{j \ge k} f_j(x) \right). $$
  For each fixed $k$, $u_k(x) = \sup_{j \ge k} f_j(x)$ is measurable. Then $\inf_k u_k(x)$ is the infimum of a countable family of measurable functions, hence measurable! $\blacksquare$

** Measurability of Pointwise Limits
#+begin_quote
*Corollary:* If $f_j : A \to \dot{\mathbb{R}}$ are measurable and $f(x) = \lim_{j\to\infty} f_j(x)$ exists for all $x \in A$, then
$$ f \text{ is measurable.} $$
#+end_quote
Because when the limit exists, $f(x) = \limsup_{j\to\infty} f_j(x) = \liminf_{j\to\infty} f_j(x)$.
This is a tremendous triumph of measure theory over Riemann integration: pointwise limits of measurable functions are always measurable!

* Almost Everywhere ($a.e.$) Equality

** Definition of "Almost Everywhere"
#+begin_quote
*Definition:*
A mathematical statement holds *almost everywhere* in $A$ (abbreviated *a.e.*, or Finnish *m.k.* = /melkein kaikkialla/) if the set of points where the statement fails has Lebesgue measure zero:
$$ m(\{x \in A : \text{statement is false}\}) = 0. $$
#+end_quote

** Stability under Null Set Modifications
#+begin_quote
*Theorem 2.17 & 2.18:*
1. If $f : A \to \dot{\mathbb{R}}$ is measurable and $g = f$ a.e. in $A$, then $g$ is measurable.
2. If $f_j : A \to \dot{\mathbb{R}}$ are measurable and $f_j \to f$ a.e. in $A$, then $f$ is measurable.
#+end_quote

*Proof of (1):*
Let $N = \{x \in A : f(x) \ne g(x)\}$. By assumption, $m(N) = 0$.
For any $a \in \mathbb{R}$:
$$ \{x \in A : g(x) > a\} = \left( \{x \in A \setminus N : f(x) > a\} \right) \cup \{x \in N : g(x) > a\}. $$
The first term is $\{x \in A : f(x) > a\} \setminus N$ which is measurable.
The second term is a subset of $N$, which has measure zero and is therefore measurable by completeness (Theorem 1.12).
Their union is measurable, so $g$ is measurable. $\blacksquare$

*Practical Takeaway:* Null sets are "invisible" to measurability. We can redefine functions on sets of measure zero (e.g. at isolated points, countable sets, or boundaries) without affecting their measurability or integrals.

* Simple Functions and the Canonical Approximation Theorem

** Definition: Simple Functions
#+begin_quote
*Definition (Simple Function):*
A function $\phi : \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}$ is called a *simple function* if:
1. $\phi$ takes only finitely many distinct values $\{a_1, \dots, a_k\} \subset \mathbb{R}$.
2. The preimage sets $A_i = \phi^{-1}(\{a_i\}) = \{x \in \mathbb{R}^n : \phi(x) = a_i\}$ are Lebesgue measurable.
#+end_quote

The representation
$$ \phi(x) = \sum_{i=1}^k a_i \chi_{A_i}(x), $$
where the sets $A_1, \dots, A_k \in \mathrm{Leb}(\mathbb{R}^n)$ are pairwise disjoint and $\bigcup_{i=1}^k A_i = \mathbb{R}^n$, is called the *standard representation* of $\phi$.
Here $\chi_E$ denotes the *characteristic (indicator) function*:
$$ \chi_E(x) = \begin{cases} 1, & x \in E, \\ 0, & x \notin E. \end{cases} $$
We denote the set of non-negative simple functions by $\mathcal{Y}$ (or $\mathcal{S}^+$).

** The Fundamental Approximation Theorem (The Staircase Theorem)
#+begin_quote
*Theorem 3.9 (Approximation by Simple Functions):*
Let $f : \mathbb{R}^n \to [0, +\infty]$ be a non-negative measurable function.
Then there exists an *increasing sequence* of non-negative simple functions $(\phi_k)_{k=1}^\infty$ such that:
$$ 0 \le \phi_1(x) \le \phi_2(x) \le \dots \le \phi_k(x) \le \phi_{k+1}(x) \le \dots \le f(x) $$
and
$$ \lim_{k\to\infty} \phi_k(x) = f(x) \quad \text{for all } x \in \mathbb{R}^n. $$
Moreover, if $f$ is bounded on a set $E$, the convergence $\phi_k \to f$ is *uniform* on $E$.
#+end_quote

** The Explicit Dyadic Construction
For each $k \in \mathbb{N}$, partition the vertical interval $[0, k[$ into $k 2^k$ subintervals of width $2^{-k}$:
$$ E_{k, j} = \left\{ x \in \mathbb{R}^n : \frac{j-1}{2^k} \le f(x) < \frac{j}{2^k} \right\}, \quad j = 1, 2, \dots, k 2^k. $$
For values $f(x) \ge k$, define the "overflow" set:
$$ F_k = \{ x \in \mathbb{R}^n : f(x) \ge k \}. $$
Now define $\phi_k : \mathbb{R}^n \to [0, \infty)$ by:
$$ \phi_k(x) = \sum_{j=1}^{k 2^k} \frac{j-1}{2^k} \chi_{E_{k, j}}(x) + k \chi_{F_k}(x). $$

#+caption: Structure of the Dyadic Approximation Sequence $\phi_k$
| Level $k$ | Truncation Height | Resolution (Step Size) | Number of Levels |
|-----------+-------------------+------------------------+------------------|
| $k = 1$   | $1$               | $1/2 = 0.5$            | $2$ steps        |
| $k = 2$   | $2$               | $1/4 = 0.25$           | $8$ steps        |
| $k = 3$   | $3$               | $1/8 = 0.125$          | $24$ steps       |
| general   | $k$               | $2^{-k}$               | $k \cdot 2^k$    |

*Why $\phi_k \nearrow f$:*
1. *Monotonicity:* When passing from $k$ to $k+1$, each interval of width $2^{-k}$ splits into two subintervals of width $2^{-(k+1)}$. On the left half $\phi_{k+1} = \phi_k$, and on the right half $\phi_{k+1} = \phi_k + 2^{-(k+1)} > \phi_k$. On $F_k$, either $f(x) \ge k+1$ (so $\phi_{k+1} \ge k+1 > k = \phi_k$) or $k \le f(x) < k+1$ (so $\phi_{k+1} \ge k = \phi_k$). Thus $\phi_k(x) \le \phi_{k+1}(x)$.
2. *Convergence:* For any $x \in \mathbb{R}^n$:
   - If $f(x) < +\infty$, choose $k > f(x)$. For all such $k$, $x \notin F_k$, so $0 \le f(x) - \phi_k(x) < 2^{-k} \to 0$.
   - If $f(x) = +\infty$, then $x \in F_k$ for all $k$, so $\phi_k(x) = k \to +\infty = f(x)$. $\blacksquare$

This construction is the *engine of Lebesgue integration*: we define integrals for simple functions first, then take the limit along $\phi_k \nearrow f$ for arbitrary measurable functions!
