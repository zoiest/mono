* Overview

** What problem does this chapter solve?
In introductory calculus, we learn to compute the length of an interval $[a, b]$ as $b - a$, the area of a rectangle as $\text{width} \times \text{height}$, and the volume of a box as the product of its side lengths. 

However, advanced analysis and probability require measuring the "size" of sets far more complicated than boxes:
- What is the size of the set of rational numbers $\mathbb{Q} \cap [0, 1]$?
- What is the size of a fractal like the Cantor set?
- What is the size of the set of points where a sequence of functions converges?

Our goal is to construct a function $m$ that assigns to every subset $A \subset \mathbb{R}^n$ a generalized volume $m(A) \in [0, +\infty]$ satisfying three natural geometric axioms:
1. *Normalization:* For any $n$-interval $I = [a_1, b_1] \times \dots \times [a_n, b_n]$, $m(I) = \prod_{j=1}^n (b_j - a_j)$.
2. *Translation Invariance:* Moving a set does not change its size: $m(x + A) = m(A)$ for all $x \in \mathbb{R}^n$.
3. *Countable Additivity ($\sigma$-additivity):* If $A_1, A_2, \dots$ are pairwise disjoint sets, then
   \[
   m\left( \bigcup_{j=1}^\infty A_j \right) = \sum_{j=1}^\infty m(A_j).
   \]

*The Fundamental Obstruction:* It turns out (as proved by Vitali in 1905, see Chapter 3) that *no such function can exist on the entire power set $\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^n)$*!
To circumvent this, Henri Lebesgue introduced a two-stage strategy:
- *Stage 1 (This Chapter):* Construct an *outer measure* $m^*(A)$ defined on *all* subsets $A \subset \mathbb{R}^n$. It satisfies normalization, monotonicity, and translation invariance, but only satisfies *countable subadditivity* ($m^*(\bigcup A_j) \le \sum m^*(A_j)$).
- *Stage 2 (Chapter 2):* Identify the class of "well-behaved" sets (the *measurable sets*) on which $m^*$ becomes strictly countably additive.

* Shortcomings of the Classical Riemann Integral

** Intuition: Slicing the Domain vs Slicing the Range
The Riemann integral partitions the *domain* (the $x$-axis) into small subintervals $[x_{k-1}, x_k]$. If a function oscillates violently on every tiny interval, the upper Darboux sum and the lower Darboux sum will never meet.

Henri Lebesgue's revolutionary insight was:
#+begin_quote
"Instead of partitioning the domain of $f$, partition its *range* (the $y$-axis) into intervals $[y_{k-1}, y_k]$, and measure the size of the set of points in the domain where $f(x) \approx y_k$."
#+end_quote
To make Lebesgue's idea work, we must be able to measure the size of the preimage set $\{x : y_{k-1} \le f(x) < y_k\}$, which can be an extraordinarily jagged set.

** Three Fatal Defects of the Riemann Integral
1. *Discontinuous and Indicator Functions:*
   Consider Dirichlet's function on $[0, 1]$:
   \[
   \chi_{\mathbb{Q} \cap [0, 1]}(x) = \begin{cases} 1, & x \in \mathbb{Q} \cap [0, 1], \\ 0, & x \in [0, 1] \setminus \mathbb{Q}. \end{cases}
   \]
   In any subinterval $[x_{k-1}, x_k]$, there exist both rational and irrational numbers. Thus every upper Darboux sum is $1$ and every lower Darboux sum is $0$. The function is not Riemann integrable, even though the rationals form a tiny, countable set!
2. *Fragility of Pointwise Limits:*
   Let $(q_k)_{k=1}^\infty$ be an enumeration of $\mathbb{Q} \cap [0, 1]$. Define $f_k(x) = 1$ if $x \in \{q_1, \dots, q_k\}$ and $0$ otherwise.
   Each $f_k$ is Riemann integrable (it is non-zero at only finitely many points, so $\int_0^1 f_k(x)\,dx = 0$).
   However, the pointwise limit is Dirichlet's function:
   \[
   \lim_{k \to \infty} f_k(x) = \chi_{\mathbb{Q} \cap [0, 1]}(x),
   \]
   which is not Riemann integrable. The limit of Riemann integrable functions failed to be Riemann integrable!
3. *Incompleteness of $L^1$:*
   The normed space of Riemann integrable functions equipped with $\|f\|_1 = \int_a^b |f(x)|\,dx$ is not complete: Cauchy sequences do not necessarily converge to a Riemann integrable function.

* Geometry of $n$-Intervals and Elementary Volume

** Definition of $n$-Intervals
Let $n \in \mathbb{N}$. 
- An *open $n$-interval* in $\mathbb{R}^n$ is the Cartesian product of $n$ bounded open intervals in $\mathbb{R}$:
  \[
  I = ]a_1, b_1[ \times \dots \times ]a_n, b_n[ = \{x = (x_1, \dots, x_n) \in \mathbb{R}^n : a_j < x_j < b_j \text{ for all } j = 1, \dots, n\},
  \]
  where $-\infty < a_j < b_j < +\infty$ for each $j \in \{1, \dots, n\}$.
- A *closed $n$-interval* is $\bar{I} = [a_1, b_1] \times \dots \times [a_n, b_n]$.
- A *half-open $n$-interval* is any product of bounded intervals where each factor is of the form $[a_j, b_j[$ or $]a_j, b_j]$.
- If $a_j = b_j$ for some $j$, the interval is *degenerate* (a flat boundary with empty interior).

** Elementary Volume
#+begin_quote
*Definition 1.1 (Elementary Volume).* The *elementary volume* (or elementary measure) of an $n$-interval $I$ (open, closed, or half-open) with endpoints $a_j \le b_j$ is
\[
\ell(I) = \prod_{j=1}^n (b_j - a_j).
\]
For the empty set $\emptyset$ or any degenerate interval, $\ell(\emptyset) = 0$.
#+end_quote

Note that the volume is independent of whether the boundary faces are included:
\[
\ell(I) = \ell(\bar{I}).
\]

* Lebesgue Covers and Outer Measure

** Intuition: Wrapping an Arbitrary Set with Boxes
How do we find the volume of an arbitrary, possibly irregular set $A \subset \mathbb{R}^n$?
We wrap $A$ from the outside with a countable collection of open boxes $\{I_j\}_{j=1}^\infty$.
The sum of the elementary volumes $\sum_{j=1}^\infty \ell(I_j)$ represents an *overestimate* of the volume of $A$. 
To obtain the tightest possible estimate, we take the *infimum* over all possible countable coverings.

#+begin_quote
*Definition 1.2 (Lebesgue Cover).* Let $A \subset \mathbb{R}^n$. A countable collection $\mathcal{F} = \{I_1, I_2, \dots\}$ of open $n$-intervals is called a *Lebesgue cover* of $A$ if
\[
A \subset \bigcup_{j=1}^\infty I_j.
\]
#+end_quote

> [!NOTE]
> Every subset $A \subset \mathbb{R}^n$ has at least one Lebesgue cover. For example, $\mathbb{R}^n$ can be covered by the countable collection of open cubes $]-k, k[^n$ for $k \in \mathbb{N}$.

#+begin_quote
*Definition 1.3 (Lebesgue Outer Measure).* The *Lebesgue outer measure* of a set $A \subset \mathbb{R}^n$ is defined by
\[
m^*(A) = \inf \left\{ \sum_{j=1}^\infty \ell(I_j) : I_j \text{ is an open } n\text{-interval and } A \subset \bigcup_{j=1}^\infty I_j \right\} \in [0, +\infty].
\]
#+end_quote

* Core Properties of Outer Measure

#+begin_quote
*Theorem 1.4 (Basic Properties of $m^*$).*
1. *Null Empty Set:* $m^*(\emptyset) = 0$.
2. *Monotonicity:* If $A \subset B \subset \mathbb{R}^n$, then $m^*(A) \le m^*(B)$.
3. *Countable Subadditivity:* If $A_1, A_2, A_3, \dots \subset \mathbb{R}^n$ is any sequence of sets, then
   \[
   m^*\left( \bigcup_{j=1}^\infty A_j \right) \le \sum_{j=1}^\infty m^*(A_j).
   \]
4. *Translation Invariance:* For any $A \subset \mathbb{R}^n$ and $x \in \mathbb{R}^n$:
   \[
   m^*(x + A) = m^*(A), \quad \text{where } x + A = \{x + y : y \in A\}.
   \]
5. *Scaling Behavior:* For any $A \subset \mathbb{R}^n$ and $c \in \mathbb{R}$:
   \[
   m^*(cA) = |c|^n m^*(A), \quad \text{where } cA = \{cy : y \in A\}.
   \]
#+end_quote

#+begin_proof
1. *Empty set:* For any $\varepsilon > 0$, the open cube $I = ]-\varepsilon, \varepsilon[^n$ covers $\emptyset$, and $\ell(I) = (2\varepsilon)^n$. Letting $\varepsilon \to 0$ gives $m^*(\emptyset) = 0$.
2. *Monotonicity:* If $B \subset \bigcup_{j=1}^\infty I_j$, then also $A \subset \bigcup_{j=1}^\infty I_j$ since $A \subset B$. Thus every Lebesgue cover of $B$ is also a Lebesgue cover of $A$. Taking the infimum gives $m^*(A) \le m^*(B)$.
3. *Countable Subadditivity (The $\varepsilon/2^j$ trick):*
   If $\sum_{j=1}^\infty m^*(A_j) = +\infty$, the inequality is trivially true. 
   Assume therefore that $\sum_{j=1}^\infty m^*(A_j) < \infty$, so each $m^*(A_j) < \infty$.
   Fix an arbitrary $\varepsilon > 0$.
   By the definition of the infimum, for each $j \in \mathbb{N}$ there exists a Lebesgue cover $\{I_{j, k}\}_{k=1}^\infty$ of $A_j$ such that
   \[
   \sum_{k=1}^\infty \ell(I_{j, k}) < m^*(A_j) + \frac{\varepsilon}{2^j}.
   \]
   Now collect all intervals into a single doubly indexed family $\{I_{j, k} : j, k \in \mathbb{N}\}$.
   Since $A_j \subset \bigcup_{k=1}^\infty I_{j, k}$ for every $j$, we have
   \[
   \bigcup_{j=1}^\infty A_j \subset \bigcup_{j=1}^\infty \bigcup_{k=1}^\infty I_{j, k}.
   \]
   Thus $\{I_{j, k}\}_{j, k=1}^\infty$ is a countable Lebesgue cover of $\bigcup_{j=1}^\infty A_j$. By definition of $m^*$:
   \[
   m^*\left( \bigcup_{j=1}^\infty A_j \right) \le \sum_{j=1}^\infty \sum_{k=1}^\infty \ell(I_{j, k}) < \sum_{j=1}^\infty \left( m^*(A_j) + \frac{\varepsilon}{2^j} \right) = \sum_{j=1}^\infty m^*(A_j) + \varepsilon \sum_{j=1}^\infty 2^{-j} = \sum_{j=1}^\infty m^*(A_j) + \varepsilon.
   \]
   Since this holds for every $\varepsilon > 0$, letting $\varepsilon \to 0$ yields the result.
4. *Translation invariance:* If $\{I_j\}$ covers $A$, then $\{x + I_j\}$ covers $x + A$. The shifted interval $x + I_j$ is still an open $n$-interval, and its side lengths are unchanged: $\ell(x + I_j) = \ell(I_j)$. Thus $m^*(x + A) \le m^*(A)$. Applying this to $-x$ and $x + A$ gives the reverse inequality.
5. *Scaling:* Scaling each side of an $n$-interval by $c$ multiplies each of the $n$ side lengths by $|c|$, so $\ell(c I) = |c|^n \ell(I)$. Taking infima gives $m^*(cA) = |c|^n m^*(A)$.
#+end_proof

* Outer Measure of Intervals and Null Sets

** Outer Measure of an $n$-Interval Equals its Volume

#+begin_quote
*Theorem 1.5.* For any bounded $n$-interval $I \subset \mathbb{R}^n$ (open, closed, or half-open):
\[
m^*(I) = \ell(I).
\]
#+end_quote

#+begin_proof
1. *Upper Bound ($m^*(I) \le \ell(I)$):*
   Let $I = [a_1, b_1] \times \dots \times [a_n, b_n]$ be closed. 
   For any $\varepsilon > 0$, expand each side by $\varepsilon$:
   \[
   I_\varepsilon = ]a_1 - \varepsilon, b_1 + \varepsilon[ \times \dots \times ]a_n - \varepsilon, b_n + \varepsilon[.
   \]
   $I_\varepsilon$ is an open $n$-interval and $I \subset I_\varepsilon$. Thus $\{I_\varepsilon\}$ is a one-interval cover of $I$:
   \[
   m^*(I) \le \ell(I_\varepsilon) = \prod_{j=1}^n (b_j - a_j + 2\varepsilon).
   \]
   Letting $\varepsilon \to 0$ gives $m^*(I) \le \ell(I)$.
2. *Lower Bound ($m^*(I) \ge \ell(I)$ via Compactness):*
   Fix $\varepsilon > 0$. Let $\{I_j\}_{j=1}^\infty$ be any open cover of the closed interval $\bar{I}$.
   Since $\bar{I}$ is closed and bounded, by the *Heine--Borel Theorem* (Theorem 0.6), $\bar{I}$ is compact!
   Therefore, there exists a *finite subcover* $\{I_1, \dots, I_N\}$ such that
   \[
   \bar{I} \subset \bigcup_{j=1}^N I_j.
   \]
   By elementary geometry (partitioning the boxes into a finite grid of non-overlapping sub-boxes), the elementary volume is finitely subadditive:
   \[
   \ell(\bar{I}) \le \sum_{j=1}^N \ell(I_j) \le \sum_{j=1}^\infty \ell(I_j).
   \]
   Taking the infimum over all countable open covers $\{I_j\}$ yields $\ell(\bar{I}) \le m^*(\bar{I})$.
Since $\ell(I) = \ell(\bar{I})$ and $I \subset \bar{I}$, monotonicity and the bounds squeeze $m^*(I) = \ell(I)$.
#+end_proof

** Null Sets and Countable Sets

#+begin_quote
*Definition 1.6 (Null Set).* A subset $N \subset \mathbb{R}^n$ is called a *null set* (or set of measure zero) if $m^*(N) = 0$.
#+end_quote

#+begin_quote
*Theorem 1.7.*
1. Every singleton $\{x\} \subset \mathbb{R}^n$ has $m^*(\{x\}) = 0$.
2. Every countable subset $A \subset \mathbb{R}^n$ has $m^*(A) = 0$.
#+end_quote

#+begin_proof
1. For any $\varepsilon > 0$, the cube $I_\varepsilon = ]x_1 - \varepsilon, x_1 + \varepsilon[ \times \dots \times ]x_n - \varepsilon, x_n + \varepsilon[$ covers $\{x\}$, with $\ell(I_\varepsilon) = (2\varepsilon)^n$. Letting $\varepsilon \to 0$ shows $m^*(\{x\}) = 0$.
2. Let $A = \{a_1, a_2, a_3, \dots\}$ be countable. By countable subadditivity:
   \[
   m^*(A) = m^*\left( \bigcup_{j=1}^\infty \{a_j\} \right) \le \sum_{j=1}^\infty m^*(\{a_j\}) = \sum_{j=1}^\infty 0 = 0.
   \]
   Since $m^*(A) \ge 0$, we conclude $m^*(A) = 0$.
#+end_proof

*Example 1.8 (Rationals have measure zero):*
The set of rational numbers in $[0, 1]$ has $m^*(\mathbb{Q} \cap [0, 1]) = 0$. 
In fact, the entire set of rationals on the real line has $m^*(\mathbb{Q}) = 0$!

* Worked Exercises

** Exercise 1.1 (Fall 2026 Exam Problem 3a: Positive measure vs open sets)
Is the following statement true or false? 
"If $m^*(A) > 0$, then $A$ contains a non-empty open set."
If true, prove it; if false, provide a counterexample.

*Solution:*
*The statement is FALSE.*
*Counterexample 1:*
Let $A = [0, 1] \setminus \mathbb{Q}$ be the set of irrational numbers in $[0, 1]$.
- First, does $A$ contain any non-empty open set? 
  No. Any non-empty open set in $\mathbb{R}$ contains an open interval $]a, b[$ with $a < b$. By the density of the rational numbers, $]a, b[$ must contain at least one rational number. Hence $]a, b[ \not\subset A$. Therefore, the interior of $A$ is completely empty: $\text{int}(A) = \emptyset$.
- Second, what is the outer measure of $A$?
  Notice that $[0, 1] = A \cup (\mathbb{Q} \cap [0, 1])$. 
  By subadditivity:
  \[
  m^*([0, 1]) \le m^*(A) + m^*(\mathbb{Q} \cap [0, 1]).
  \]
  We know $m^*([0, 1]) = 1$ and $m^*(\mathbb{Q} \cap [0, 1]) = 0$ (Theorem 1.7). Thus:
  \[
  1 \le m^*(A) + 0 \implies m^*(A) \ge 1.
  \]
  (Since $A \subset [0, 1]$, $m^*(A) = 1 > 0$.)
Hence $m^*(A) = 1 > 0$, yet $A$ contains *no* non-empty open set.

** Exercise 1.2 (Fall 2026 Exam Problem 3b: Finite measure vs boundedness)
Is the following statement true or false?
"If $m^*(A) < \infty$, then $A$ is bounded, i.e., there exists $R > 0$ such that $A \subset B(0, R)$."
If true, prove it; if false, provide a counterexample.

*Solution:*
*The statement is FALSE.*
*Counterexample:*
For each integer $k \ge 1$, let $I_k$ be the open interval centered at $k$ of length $1/2^k$:
\[
I_k = \left] k - \frac{1}{2^{k+1}}, \, k + \frac{1}{2^{k+1}} \right[.
\]
Define $A = \bigcup_{k=1}^\infty I_k \subset \mathbb{R}$.
- Is $A$ bounded?
  No: for every $k \in \mathbb{N}$, the integer $k \in I_k \subset A$. Since $k \to \infty$, $A$ contains arbitrarily large numbers. For any $R > 0$, choosing $k > R$ gives $k \in A \setminus B(0, R)$. Thus $A$ is unbounded.
- Is $m^*(A)$ finite?
  By countable subadditivity:
  \[
  m^*(A) = m^*\left(\bigcup_{k=1}^\infty I_k\right) \le \sum_{k=1}^\infty \ell(I_k) = \sum_{k=1}^\infty \frac{1}{2^k} = 1 < \infty.
  \]
Thus $m^*(A) \le 1 < \infty$, but $A$ is unbounded.

** Exercise 1.3 (Homework 2, Problem 4: Graph of a continuous function)
Let $f: [0, 1] \to \mathbb{R}$ be a continuous function. The *graph* of $f$ is
\[
\Gamma(f) = \{(x, f(x)) \in \mathbb{R}^2 : x \in [0, 1]\}.
\]
Prove that $m^*(\Gamma(f)) = 0$ in $\mathbb{R}^2$.

*Solution:*
1. Since $[0, 1]$ is a compact interval and $f$ is continuous, $f$ is *uniformly continuous* on $[0, 1]$ (Heine--Cantor theorem).
2. Fix $\varepsilon > 0$. By uniform continuity, there exists $\delta > 0$ such that for all $x, y \in [0, 1]$:
   \[
   \lvert x - y \rvert < \delta \implies |f(x) - f(y)| < \varepsilon.
   \]
3. Choose $N \in \mathbb{N}$ large enough such that $1/N < \delta$. Partition $[0, 1]$ into $N$ equal subintervals:
   \[
   J_k = \left[ \frac{k-1}{N}, \frac{k}{N} \right], \quad k = 1, \dots, N.
   \]
4. On each subinterval $J_k$, the oscillation of $f$ is at most $\varepsilon$:
   \[
   M_k = \max_{x \in J_k} f(x), \quad m_k = \min_{x \in J_k} f(x) \implies M_k - m_k < \varepsilon.
   \]
5. For each $k$, the portion of the graph over $J_k$ is contained in the 2-dimensional closed rectangle:
   \[
   R_k = J_k \times [m_k, M_k] = \left[ \frac{k-1}{N}, \frac{k}{N} \right] \times [m_k, M_k].
   \]
   The elementary area of $R_k$ is
   \[
   \ell(R_k) = \frac{1}{N} (M_k - m_k) < \frac{\varepsilon}{N}.
   \]
6. Clearly $\Gamma(f) \subset \bigcup_{k=1}^N R_k$. By finite subadditivity:
   \[
   m^*(\Gamma(f)) \le \sum_{k=1}^N m^*(R_k) = \sum_{k=1}^N \ell(R_k) < \sum_{k=1}^N \frac{\varepsilon}{N} = N \cdot \frac{\varepsilon}{N} = \varepsilon.
   \]
7. Since $\varepsilon > 0$ was arbitrary, letting $\varepsilon \to 0$ gives $m^*(\Gamma(f)) = 0$.

* Summary Cheat-Sheet

| Property | Mathematical Statement | Interpretation / Pitfall |
|----------+-------------------------+--------------------------|
| *Definition* | $m^*(A) = \inf \{ \sum \ell(I_j) : A \subset \bigcup I_j \}$ | Infimum over all countable covers by open $n$-intervals. |
| *Empty set* | $m^*(\emptyset) = 0$ | Always zero. |
| *Monotonicity* | $A \subset B \implies m^*(A) \le m^*(B)$ | Bigger sets have larger or equal outer measure. |
| *Countable Subadditivity* | $m^*(\bigcup_{j=1}^\infty A_j) \le \sum_{j=1}^\infty m^*(A_j)$ | Always holds, but strict equality is NOT guaranteed for arbitrary disjoint sets! |
| *Intervals* | $m^*(I) = \ell(I) = \prod (b_j - a_j)$ | Outer measure extends classical elementary volume. |
| *Countable Sets* | $A$ countable $\implies m^*(A) = 0$ | $\mathbb{Q}$, $\mathbb{Z}$, singletons all have measure zero. |
| *Translation Invariance* | $m^*(x + A) = m^*(A)$ | Rigid shifts do not alter outer measure. |
| *Scaling* | $m^*(cA) = \lvert c\rvert^n m^*(A)$ | Volume scales with power $n$ of the dilation factor. |
| *Empty Interior $\not\Rightarrow$ Zero Measure* | $\text{int}(A) = \emptyset \centernot\implies m^*(A) = 0$ | Example: $[0, 1] \setminus \mathbb{Q}$ has empty interior but $m^* = 1$. |
| *Finite Measure $\not\Rightarrow$ Bounded* | $m^*(A) < \infty \centernot\implies A$ bounded | Example: $\bigcup_{k=1}^\infty ]k - 2^{-k-1}, k + 2^{-k-1}[$. |
