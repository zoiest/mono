* Overview

** What problem does this chapter solve?
In Chapter 1, we highlighted one of the most frustrating failures of the classical Riemann integral: *the pointwise limit of Riemann integrable functions often fails to be Riemann integrable*. 

In Lebesgue theory, limits are completely tamed. The first major triumph of this chapter is proving that the class of measurable functions is *closed under all standard limiting operations*:
- Suprema and infima of countable sequences of measurable functions are measurable.
- $\limsup$ and $\liminf$ of sequences of measurable functions are measurable.
- Pointwise limits of measurable functions are measurable.

The second major triumph is the *Simple Function Approximation Theorem*. 
Just as any real number can be approximated by finite decimal fractions, *any non-negative measurable function can be approximated from below by an increasing sequence of simple step functions*:
\[
0 \le s_1(x) \le s_2(x) \le s_3(x) \le \dots \nearrow f(x).
\]
This constructive staircase approximation is the engine of Lebesgue integration: once we define the integral for simple functions (which is just a finite sum of heights times base measures), we define the integral of *any* non-negative measurable function by taking the limit of the integrals of its approximating simple functions.

Finally, we introduce the indispensable concept of *almost everywhere* (a.e.): in measure theory, two functions that differ only on a set of measure zero are indistinguishable.

* Limits of Sequences of Measurable Functions

Let $A \in \mathcal{M}(\mathbb{R}^n)$ and let $(f_j)_{j=1}^\infty$ be a sequence of measurable functions $f_j: A \to \overline{\mathbb{R}}$.

** Suprema and Infima

#+begin_quote
*Theorem 6.1 (Measurability of Suprema and Infima).* 
Let $f_j: A \to \overline{\mathbb{R}}$ be measurable for each $j \in \mathbb{N}$. Define:
\[
g(x) = \sup_{j \in \mathbb{N}} f_j(x) \quad \text{and} \quad h(x) = \inf_{j \in \mathbb{N}} f_j(x).
\]
Then $g$ and $h$ are measurable functions on $A$.
#+end_quote

#+begin_proof
1. *Supremum:* For any $a \in \mathbb{R}$, observe that the supremum of a collection of numbers exceeds $a$ if and only if at least one number in the collection exceeds $a$:
   \[
   \sup_{j \in \mathbb{N}} f_j(x) > a \iff \exists j \in \mathbb{N} \text{ such that } f_j(x) > a.
   \]
   In terms of sets, this means:
   \[
   \{x \in A : g(x) > a\} = \bigcup_{j=1}^\infty \{x \in A : f_j(x) > a\}.
   \]
   Because each $f_j$ is measurable, each set $\{x \in A : f_j(x) > a\}$ belongs to $\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{R}^n)$.
   Since $\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{R}^n)$ is a $\sigma$-algebra, it is closed under countable unions.
   Thus $\{x \in A : g(x) > a\} \in \mathcal{M}(\mathbb{R}^n)$.
   By Theorem 5.2 (Ray Criterion 1), $g = \sup f_j$ is measurable.
2. *Infimum:* Dually, $\inf_{j} f_j(x) < a \iff \exists j \in \mathbb{N}$ such that $f_j(x) < a$:
   \[
   \{x \in A : h(x) < a\} = \bigcup_{j=1}^\infty \{x \in A : f_j(x) < a\}.
   \]
   Each set is measurable, so the countable union is measurable. By Theorem 5.2 (Criterion 3), $h = \inf f_j$ is measurable. (Alternatively, $h = -\sup(-f_j)$).
#+end_proof

** Limsup and Liminf

Recall that for any sequence of numbers $(x_j)_{j=1}^\infty \subset \overline{\mathbb{R}}$:
\[
\limsup_{j \to \infty} x_j = \inf_{k \ge 1} \left( \sup_{j \ge k} x_j \right), \qquad \liminf_{j \to \infty} x_j = \sup_{k \ge 1} \left( \inf_{j \ge k} x_j \right).
\]

#+begin_quote
*Theorem 6.2 (Measurability of $\limsup$ and $\liminf$).* 
If $(f_j)_{j=1}^\infty$ is a sequence of measurable functions $f_j: A \to \overline{\mathbb{R}}$, then
\[
f^*(x) = \limsup_{j \to \infty} f_j(x) \quad \text{and} \quad f_*(x) = \liminf_{j \to \infty} f_j(x)
\]
are measurable functions on $A$.
#+end_quote

#+begin_proof
For each $k \in \mathbb{N}$, define the tail supremum:
\[
u_k(x) = \sup_{j \ge k} f_j(x).
\]
By Theorem 6.1, each $u_k$ is measurable. 
The limsup is the infimum over all $k \in \mathbb{N}$ of these functions:
\[
f^*(x) = \inf_{k \in \mathbb{N}} u_k(x).
\]
Applying Theorem 6.1 again to the sequence of measurable functions $(u_k)_{k=1}^\infty$, their infimum $f^*$ is measurable.
The proof that $f_* = \liminf f_j$ is measurable is completely analogous.
#+end_proof

** Pointwise Limits

#+begin_quote
*Corollary 6.3 (Pointwise Limits are Measurable).* 
If $f_j: A \to \overline{\mathbb{R}}$ is a sequence of measurable functions, and the pointwise limit
\[
f(x) = \lim_{j \to \infty} f_j(x)
\]
exists for all $x \in A$, then $f$ is a measurable function on $A$.
#+end_quote

#+begin_proof
When the limit exists, $\limsup_{j \to \infty} f_j(x) = \liminf_{j \to \infty} f_j(x) = f(x)$. 
Since $\limsup f_j$ is measurable by Theorem 6.2, $f$ is measurable.
#+end_proof

* The Domain of Convergence

What if the sequence $f_j(x)$ does not converge at every point?

#+begin_quote
*Theorem 6.4.* Let $f_j: A \to \mathbb{R}$ be measurable functions for each $j \in \mathbb{N}$. 
The set of points $C \subset A$ where the sequence converges to a finite limit:
\[
C = \{x \in A : \lim_{j \to \infty} f_j(x) \text{ exists in } \mathbb{R}\}
\]
is a Lebesgue measurable set: $C \in \mathcal{M}(\mathbb{R}^n)$.
#+end_quote

#+begin_proof
A sequence of real numbers converges in $\mathbb{R}$ if and only if it is a Cauchy sequence!
By the Cauchy criterion:
\[
x \in C \iff \forall k \in \mathbb{N},\, \exists N \in \mathbb{N} \text{ such that } \forall j, l \ge N,\, |f_j(x) - f_l(x)| < \frac{1}{k}.
\]
Translating the quantifiers into set operations:
\[
C = \bigcap_{k=1}^\infty \bigcup_{N=1}^\infty \bigcap_{j=N}^\infty \bigcap_{l=N}^\infty \left\{x \in A : |f_j(x) - f_l(x)| < \frac{1}{k}\right\}.
\]
Since $f_j$ and $f_l$ are measurable, their difference and absolute value $|f_j - f_l|$ is measurable (Theorem 5.8).
Thus each set $\{x \in A : |f_j(x) - f_l(x)| < 1/k\}$ is measurable.
Because $C$ is constructed using only countable intersections and countable unions of these measurable sets, $C \in \mathcal{M}(\mathbb{R}^n)$.
#+end_proof

* The Concept of "Almost Everywhere" (a.e.)

** Intuition
In classical analysis, if two functions differ at a single point, they are different functions.
In measure theory, single points (and all countable sets) have measure zero. 
Changing the values of a function on a set of measure zero has zero impact on its integrals or volumes.

#+begin_quote
*Definition 6.5 (Almost Everywhere).* Let $A \in \mathcal{M}(\mathbb{R}^n)$. 
A mathematical statement $P(x)$ is said to hold *almost everywhere on $A$* (abbreviated *a.e.*, or in Finnish *melkein kaikkialla*, *m.k.*, or *p.k.* for *pisteittäin melkein kaikkialla*) if the set of points where it fails is a null set:
\[
m(\{x \in A : P(x) \text{ is false}\}) = 0.
\]
In particular:
- *Equality a.e.:* $f = g$ a.e. on $A$ means $m(\{x \in A : f(x) \neq g(x)\}) = 0$.
- *Convergence a.e.:* $f_j \to f$ a.e. on $A$ means $m(\{x \in A : f_j(x) \not\to f(x)\}) = 0$.
#+end_quote

#+begin_quote
*Theorem 6.6 (Modification on Null Sets Preserves Measurability).* 
Suppose $f: A \to \overline{\mathbb{R}}$ is measurable, and $g: A \to \overline{\mathbb{R}}$ satisfies $f = g$ a.e. on $A$. 
Then $g$ is measurable.
#+end_quote

#+begin_proof
Let $N = \{x \in A : f(x) \neq g(x)\}$. By assumption, $m(N) = 0$.
By Theorem 2.2, $N \in \mathcal{M}$ and every subset of $N$ is measurable.
For any $a \in \mathbb{R}$, examine the set where $g(x) > a$:
\[
\{x \in A : g(x) > a\} = \Bigl( \{x \in A \setminus N : f(x) > a\} \Bigr) \cup \Bigl( \{x \in N : g(x) > a\} \Bigr).
\]
The first set equals $\{x \in A : f(x) > a\} \setminus N$, which is measurable because $f$ is measurable and $N$ is measurable.
The second set is a subset of $N$, hence it is a null set, and therefore measurable by completeness.
The union of two measurable sets is measurable. Thus $g$ is measurable.
#+end_proof

#+begin_quote
*Corollary 6.7 (Limit a.e. is Measurable).* 
If $f_j: A \to \overline{\mathbb{R}}$ are measurable and $f_j(x) \to f(x)$ for almost every $x \in A$, then $f$ is measurable.
#+end_quote

* Simple Functions

** Canonical Representation
#+begin_quote
*Definition 6.8 (Simple Function).* A function $s: A \to \mathbb{R}$ is called a *simple function* if its image $s(A) \subset \mathbb{R}$ is a *finite set*:
\[
s(A) = \{a_1, a_2, \dots, a_k\},
\]
where $a_1, \dots, a_k$ are distinct real numbers.
#+end_quote

If we set $A_i = s^{-1}(\{a_i\}) = \{x \in A : s(x) = a_i\}$, then:
1. The sets $A_1, \dots, A_k$ are *pairwise disjoint*.
2. Their union is the entire domain: $\bigcup_{i=1}^k A_i = A$.
3. We can write $s$ in its *standard (canonical) representation*:
   \[
   s(x) = \sum_{i=1}^k a_i \chi_{A_i}(x).
   \]

#+begin_quote
*Theorem 6.9.* A simple function $s = \sum_{i=1}^k a_i \chi_{A_i}$ is measurable if and only if each set $A_i$ is measurable: $A_i \in \mathcal{M}(\mathbb{R}^n)$ for all $i = 1, \dots, k$.
#+end_quote

#+begin_proof
If each $A_i$ is measurable, each indicator $\chi_{A_i}$ is measurable (Theorem 5.7). By Theorem 5.8, finite linear combinations of measurable functions are measurable, so $s$ is measurable.
Conversely, if $s$ is measurable, each singleton $\{a_i\}$ is a closed set (hence a Borel set), so by Theorem 5.5, $A_i = s^{-1}(\{a_i\}) \in \mathcal{M}(\mathbb{R}^n)$.
#+end_proof

* The Simple Function Approximation Theorem

This is one of the most celebrated theorems in measure theory: *every non-negative measurable function can be built up monotonically from simple functions*.

#+begin_quote
*Theorem 6.10 (Monotone Simple Approximation).* 
Let $A \in \mathcal{M}(\mathbb{R}^n)$ and let $f: A \to [0, +\infty]$ be a non-negative measurable function. 
Then there exists a sequence of simple measurable functions $(s_k)_{k=1}^\infty$ such that:
1. *Monotone Increasing:* For all $x \in A$ and all $k \in \mathbb{N}$:
   \[
   0 \le s_1(x) \le s_2(x) \le s_3(x) \le \dots \le f(x).
   \]
2. *Pointwise Convergence:* For every $x \in A$:
   \[
   \lim_{k \to \infty} s_k(x) = f(x).
   \]
3. *Uniform Convergence on Bounded Sets:* If $f$ is bounded on a subset $E \subset A$, the convergence $s_k \rightrightarrows f$ is uniform on $E$.
#+end_quote

#+begin_proof
*The Dyadic Slicing Construction:*
Fix $k \in \mathbb{N}$. We divide the interval $[0, k[$ on the $y$-axis into $k \cdot 2^k$ subintervals of width $1/2^k$:
\[
I_{k, j} = \left[ \frac{j}{2^k}, \, \frac{j+1}{2^k} \right[, \quad j = 0, 1, \dots, k 2^k - 1.
\]
Define the preimage sets in the domain $A$:
\[
E_{k, j} = f^{-1}(I_{k, j}) = \left\{ x \in A : \frac{j}{2^k} \le f(x) < \frac{j+1}{2^k} \right\},
\]
and for values exceeding the cutoff $k$:
\[
E_{k, \infty} = f^{-1}([k, +\infty]) = \{x \in A : f(x) \ge k\}.
\]
Because $f$ is measurable, each set $E_{k, j}$ and $E_{k, \infty}$ is measurable.
Now define the step function $s_k: A \to \mathbb{R}$ by:
\[
s_k(x) = \sum_{j=0}^{k 2^k - 1} \frac{j}{2^k} \chi_{E_{k, j}}(x) + k \chi_{E_{k, \infty}}(x).
\]

*Verification of Properties:*
1. *Measurability and Simplicity:* $s_k$ takes only $k 2^k + 1$ values (the fractions $j/2^k$ and $k$), each on a measurable set. Thus $s_k$ is a simple measurable function.
2. *Monotonicity ($s_k \le s_{k+1}$):*
   Going from $k$ to $k + 1$, each interval of width $1/2^k$ is split into two halves of width $1/2^{k+1}$. 
   On the lower half, the value of $s_{k+1}$ matches $s_k$; on the upper half, $s_{k+1}$ increases by $1/2^{k+1}$.
   If $f(x) \ge k$, $s_k(x) = k$, while $s_{k+1}(x) \ge k$.
   Thus $s_k(x) \le s_{k+1}(x) \le f(x)$ for all $x \in A$.
3. *Pointwise Convergence:*
   Let $x \in A$.
   - If $f(x) < +\infty$, choose $k > f(x)$. For all such $k$, $x$ belongs to some interval $I_{k, j}$, so:
     \[
     0 \le f(x) - s_k(x) < \frac{1}{2^k}.
     \]
     As $k \to \infty$, $1/2^k \to 0$, so $s_k(x) \to f(x)$.
   - If $f(x) = +\infty$, then $f(x) \ge k$ for all $k$, so $s_k(x) = k$. As $k \to \infty$, $s_k(x) \to +\infty = f(x)$.
4. *Uniform Convergence:* If $f(x) \le M$ for all $x \in E$, then for all $k > M$, $|f(x) - s_k(x)| < 1/2^k$ uniformly across $x \in E$.
#+end_proof

* Worked Exercises

** Exercise 6.1 (Pointwise limit of continuous functions need not be continuous)
Consider $f_k: [0, 1] \to \mathbb{R}$ defined by $f_k(x) = x^k$.
1. Show that each $f_k$ is continuous (hence measurable).
2. Find the pointwise limit $f(x) = \lim_{k \to \infty} f_k(x)$.
3. Is $f$ continuous? Is $f$ measurable?

*Solution:*
1. Each $f_k$ is a polynomial, hence continuous on $[0, 1]$. By Theorem 5.4, each $f_k$ is measurable.
2. For $x \in [0, 1[$: $\lim_{k \to \infty} x^k = 0$.
   For $x = 1$: $\lim_{k \to \infty} 1^k = 1$.
   Thus:
   \[
   f(x) = \begin{cases} 0, & x \in [0, 1[, \\ 1, & x = 1. \end{cases}
   \]
3. The limit $f$ is *discontinuous* at $x = 1$ ($\lim_{x \to 1^-} f(x) = 0 \neq f(1) = 1$).
   However, $f = \chi_{\{1\}}$, and $\{1\}$ is a closed set (hence measurable). 
   By Theorem 5.7, $f$ is *measurable*! (Confirming Corollary 6.3: limits preserve measurability even when they destroy continuity).

** Exercise 6.2 (Homework 5, Problem 6: Pointwise vs Uniform Convergence)
Assume $A \subset \mathbb{R}^n$ is measurable with $m(A) < \infty$, and $f_j: A \to \mathbb{R}$ is a sequence of measurable functions such that $f_j(x) \to f(x)$ for all $x \in A$.
Prove that for every $\varepsilon > 0$ and $\delta > 0$, there exists an integer $N \in \mathbb{N}$ and a measurable set $E \subset A$ such that:
\[
m(A \setminus E) < \delta \quad \text{and} \quad |f_j(x) - f(x)| < \varepsilon \quad \text{for all } j \ge N \text{ and all } x \in E.
\]
(This is the core step of *Egorov's Theorem*!)

*Solution:*
1. For each $k \in \mathbb{N}$, define the set of points where the error is small for all indices $j \ge k$:
   \[
   E_k = \bigcap_{j=k}^\infty \{x \in A : |f_j(x) - f(x)| < \varepsilon\}.
   \]
2. Since each $f_j$ and $f$ are measurable, each set in the intersection is measurable, so $E_k \in \mathcal{M}$.
3. Notice that $E_1 \subset E_2 \subset E_3 \subset \dots$ is an increasing sequence of sets.
4. Because $f_j(x) \to f(x)$ for every $x \in A$, for every $x$ there is some index $k$ such that $|f_j(x) - f(x)| < \varepsilon$ for all $j \ge k$. Thus:
   \[
   \bigcup_{k=1}^\infty E_k = A.
   \]
5. By Theorem 4.6 (Continuity of Measure from Below):
   \[
   \lim_{k \to \infty} m(E_k) = m(A).
   \]
6. Since $m(A) < \infty$, subtractivity gives $\lim_{k \to \infty} m(A \setminus E_k) = m(A) - m(A) = 0$.
7. Therefore, for the given $\delta > 0$, there exists $N \in \mathbb{N}$ such that
   \[
   m(A \setminus E_N) < \delta.
   \]
8. Setting $E = E_N$, by definition of $E_N$, for all $j \ge N$ and all $x \in E$, $|f_j(x) - f(x)| < \varepsilon$.
