* Overview

** What problem does this chapter solve?
In calculus and differential equations, functions are not isolated objects; they live in *function spaces*. 
To solve partial differential equations, minimize energy functionals, or analyze Fourier series, we need a metric or norm to measure the "distance" between two functions:
\[
\|f - g\|.
\]
For a function space to be useful in analysis, it must be *complete*: every Cauchy sequence must converge to an element *inside* the space. 
A complete normed vector space is called a *Banach space*.

The classical space of continuous or Riemann integrable functions equipped with the \(L^1\)-norm is *incomplete*: Cauchy sequences of continuous functions can converge to discontinuous step functions or fractals that do not belong to the space.

Lebesgue integration permanently solves this crisis. 
In this concluding chapter, we construct the *Lebesgue spaces $L^p(E)$* for $1 \le p \le \infty$:
1. *The $L^p$ Norm:* Measuring the size of functions via $\left(\int |f|^p\right)^{1/p}$.
2. *Almost Everywhere Equivalence:* Why identifying functions that agree almost everywhere transforms $L^p$ into a genuine normed space.
3. *Fundamental Inequalities:* Young's inequality, Hölder's inequality (generalizing Cauchy--Schwarz), and Minkowski's inequality (the triangle inequality).
4. *The Riesz--Fischer Theorem:* Proving that every $L^p(E)$ is a Banach space.
5. *Modes of Convergence:* Exploring the relationships between convergence in $L^p$, pointwise almost everywhere convergence, and uniform convergence.

** Roadmap
- *10.1 Definition of $L^p(E)$ and the $L^p$ Norm:* For $1 \le p < \infty$ and $p = \infty$.

* Definition of $L^p(E)$ and the $L^p$ Norm

Let $(X, \mathcal{M}, \mu)$ be a measure space (for instance, $(\mathbb{R}^n, \mathcal{M}(\mathbb{R}^n), m)$) and let $E \in \mathcal{M}$.

** The Spaces $L^p(E)$ for $1 \le p < \infty$
#+begin_quote
*Definition 10.1 ($L^p$ Spaces).* For $1 \le p < \infty$, the space $\mathcal{L}^p(E)$ is the set of all measurable functions $f: E \to \overline{\mathbb{R}}$ such that
\[
\int_E |f|^p \, d\mu < \infty.
\]
The *\(L^p\)-norm* of $f$ is defined by:
\[
\|f\|_p = \left( \int_E |f|^p \, d\mu \right)^{1/p}.
\]
#+end_quote

Special cases:
- $p = 1$: The space of integrable functions $L^1(E)$, with norm $\|f\|_1 = \int_E |f| \, d\mu$.
- $p = 2$: The space of square-integrable functions $L^2(E)$, which is a *Hilbert space* with inner product $\langle f, g \rangle = \int_E f g \, d\mu$.

** The Space $L^\infty(E)$ and Essential Supremum
For $p = \infty$, we measure the maximum height of a function, ignoring values on sets of measure zero.

#+begin_quote
*Definition 10.2 (Essential Supremum and $L^\infty$).* 
Let $f: E \to \overline{\mathbb{R}}$ be measurable. 
The *essential supremum* of $|f|$ on $E$ is defined by:
\[
\|f\|_\infty = \text{ess\,sup}_{x \in E} |f(x)| = \inf \{M \ge 0 : \mu(\{x \in E : |f(x)| > M\}) = 0\}.
\]
If no such $M$ exists, $\|f\|_\infty = +\infty$.
The space $\mathcal{L}^\infty(E)$ consists of all measurable functions $f$ with $\|f\|_\infty < \infty$ (essentially bounded functions).
#+end_quote

* Equivalence Classes Almost Everywhere

Recall that a true mathematical norm on a vector space $V$ must satisfy three axioms:
1. *Positivity & Non-degeneracy:* $\|f\| \ge 0$, and $\|f\| = 0 \iff f = 0$.
2. *Homogeneity:* $\|\alpha f\| = |\alpha| \, \|f\|$.
3. *Triangle Inequality:* $\|f + g\| \le \|f\| + \|g\|$.

Notice that for $\mathcal{L}^p(E)$:
\[
\|f\|_p = 0 \iff \int_E |f|^p \, d\mu = 0 \iff f(x) = 0 \quad \text{for } \mu\text{-almost every } x \in E.
\]
If $f(x) \neq 0$ on a non-empty null set (e.g. $f = \chi_{\mathbb{Q}}$), then $\|f\|_p = 0$ even though $f$ is not the zero function! 
Thus $\|\cdot\|_p$ is technically only a *seminorm* on $\mathcal{L}^p(E)$.

To resolve this, we define an equivalence relation:
\[
f \sim g \iff f(x) = g(x) \quad \text{for almost every } x \in E.
\]
#+begin_quote
*Definition 10.3 (The True $L^p$ Space).* The space $L^p(E)$ is the quotient space:
\[
L^p(E) = \mathcal{L}^p(E) / \sim.
\]
An element of $L^p(E)$ is an *equivalence class* of functions that agree almost everywhere.
On this quotient space, $\|f\|_p = 0 \implies f = 0$ in $L^p(E)$, making $L^p(E)$ a genuine normed space!
#+end_quote

#+begin_quote
*Note:* In practice, we follow standard mathematical convention and refer to "the function $f \in L^p$", always remembering that modifying $f$ on a null set does not change the element.
#+end_quote

* The Big Inequalities: Young, Hölder, and Minkowski

Let $p, q \in ]1, \infty[$ be *conjugate exponents* (or Hölder conjugates), meaning:
\[
\frac{1}{p} + \frac{1}{q} = 1 \iff p + q = p q \iff q = \frac{p}{p - 1}.
\]
For $p = 1$, the conjugate exponent is $q = \infty$.

** Young's Inequality

#+begin_quote
*Lemma 10.4 (Young's Inequality).* Let $1 < p < \infty$ and $1/p + 1/q = 1$. 
For any non-negative real numbers $a, b \ge 0$:
\[
a b \le \frac{a^p}{p} + \frac{b^q}{q}.
\]
Equality holds if and only if $a^p = b^q$.
#+end_quote

#+begin_proof
If $a = 0$ or $b = 0$, the inequality is trivially $0 \le 0$.
Assume $a > 0$ and $b > 0$. Use the strict concavity of the natural logarithm function $\ln(x)$:
\[
\ln(a b) = \ln(a) + \ln(b) = \frac{1}{p} \ln(a^p) + \frac{1}{q} \ln(b^q).
\]
Because $1/p + 1/q = 1$ and $\ln$ is strictly concave:
\[
\frac{1}{p} \ln(a^p) + \frac{1}{q} \ln(b^q) \le \ln\left( \frac{a^p}{p} + \frac{b^q}{q} \right).
\]
Exponentiating both sides yields $a b \le \frac{a^p}{p} + \frac{b^q}{q}$.
#+end_proof

** Hölder's Inequality

#+begin_quote
*Theorem 10.5 (Hölder's Inequality).* Let $1 \le p \le \infty$ and $1/p + 1/q = 1$. 
If $f \in L^p(E)$ and $g \in L^q(E)$, then $f g \in L^1(E)$ and
\[
\|f g\|_1 = \int_E |f g| \, d\mu \le \|f\|_p \|g\|_q.
\]
#+end_quote

#+begin_proof
- If $\|f\|_p = 0$ or $\|g\|_q = 0$, then $f = 0$ a.e. or $g = 0$ a.e., so $f g = 0$ a.e., giving $0 \le 0$.
- If $p = 1$ and $q = \infty$: $|f(x) g(x)| \le |f(x)| \|g\|_\infty$ a.e. Integrating gives $\|f g\|_1 \le \|g\|_\infty \int |f| = \|f\|_1 \|g\|_\infty$.
- Now assume $1 < p < \infty$ and $\|f\|_p > 0, \|g\|_q > 0$.
  Normalize the functions:
  \[
  u(x) = \frac{|f(x)|}{\|f\|_p}, \qquad v(x) = \frac{|g(x)|}{\|g\|_q}.
  \]
  By Young's Inequality (Lemma 10.4):
  \[
  u(x) v(x) \le \frac{u(x)^p}{p} + \frac{v(x)^q}{q}.
  \]
  Integrate both sides over $E$:
  \[
  \int_E u v \, d\mu \le \frac{1}{p} \int_E u^p \, d\mu + \frac{1}{q} \int_E v^q \, d\mu = \frac{1}{p} \cdot 1 + \frac{1}{q} \cdot 1 = \frac{1}{p} + \frac{1}{q} = 1.
  \]
  Substituting $u$ and $v$ back:
  \[
  \frac{\int_E |f g| \, d\mu}{\|f\|_p \|g\|_q} \le 1 \implies \int_E |f g| \, d\mu \le \|f\|_p \|g\|_q.
  \]
#+end_proof

*Special Case:* For $p = q = 2$, this is the famous *Cauchy--Schwarz inequality*:
\[
\int_E |f g| \, d\mu \le \sqrt{\int_E |f|^2 \, d\mu} \sqrt{\int_E |g|^2 \, d\mu}.
\]

** Minkowski's Inequality

#+begin_quote
*Theorem 10.6 (Minkowski's Inequality).* For $1 \le p \le \infty$, if $f, g \in L^p(E)$, then $f + g \in L^p(E)$ and
\[
\|f + g\|_p \le \|f\|_p + \|g\|_p.
\]
#+end_quote

#+begin_proof
- For $p = 1$: $\|f + g\|_1 = \int |f + g| \le \int (|f| + |g|) = \|f\|_1 + \|g\|_1$.
- For $p = \infty$: $|f(x) + g(x)| \le |f(x)| + |g(x)| \le \|f\|_\infty + \|g\|_\infty$ a.e.
- For $1 < p < \infty$:
  \[
  \lvert f + g \rvert^p = |f + g| \cdot |f + g|^{p-1} \le (|f| + |g|) |f + g|^{p-1} = |f| |f + g|^{p-1} + |g| |f + g|^{p-1}.
  \]
  Integrate both sides and apply Hölder's Inequality to each term with conjugate exponent $q = p/(p-1)$ (so $(p-1)q = p$):
  \[
  \int_E |f| |f + g|^{p-1} \le \|f\|_p \left( \int_E |f + g|^{(p-1)q} \right)^{1/q} = \|f\|_p \left( \int_E |f + g|^p \right)^{(p-1)/p} = \|f\|_p \, \|f + g\|_p^{p-1}.
  \]
  Similarly:
  \[
  \int_E |g| |f + g|^{p-1} \le \|g\|_p \, \|f + g\|_p^{p-1}.
  \]
  Adding the two inequalities:
  \[
  \|f + g\|_p^p \le \bigl( \|f\|_p + \|g\|_p \bigr) \|f + g\|_p^{p-1}.
  \]
  Dividing by $\|f + g\|_p^{p-1}$ yields $\|f + g\|_p \le \|f\|_p + \|g\|_p$.
#+end_proof

* The Riesz--Fischer Completeness Theorem

#+begin_quote
*Theorem 10.7 (Riesz--Fischer Theorem, 1907).* 
For every $1 \le p \le \infty$, the space $L^p(E)$ is a *complete normed space* (a *Banach space*).
That is, every Cauchy sequence in $L^p(E)$ converges in the \(L^p\)-norm to some function in $L^p(E)$:
\[
\lim_{j, k \to \infty} \|f_j - f_k\|_p = 0 \implies \exists f \in L^p(E) \text{ such that } \lim_{k \to \infty} \|f_k - f\|_p = 0.
\]
#+end_quote

#+begin_proof
*Proof for $1 \le p < \infty$:*
1. *Extract a rapidly converging subsequence:*
   Let $(f_k)_{k=1}^\infty$ be a Cauchy sequence in $L^p(E)$.
   Choose a subsequence $(f_{k_j})_{j=1}^\infty$ such that:
   \[
   \|f_{k_{j+1}} - f_{k_j}\|_p \le \frac{1}{2^j} \quad \text{for all } j \in \mathbb{N}.
   \]
2. *Construct the dominating function:*
   Define the non-negative functions:
   \[
   g_N(x) = \sum_{j=1}^N |f_{k_{j+1}}(x) - f_{k_j}(x)|, \qquad g(x) = \sum_{j=1}^\infty |f_{k_{j+1}}(x) - f_{k_j}(x)|.
   \]
   By Minkowski's Inequality (Theorem 10.6):
   \[
   \|g_N\|_p \le \sum_{j=1}^N \|f_{k_{j+1}} - f_{k_j}\|_p \le \sum_{j=1}^N \frac{1}{2^j} < 1.
   \]
   Since $g_N(x) \nearrow g(x)$, by the Monotone Convergence Theorem (Theorem 7.6):
   \[
   \int_E g^p \, d\mu = \lim_{N \to \infty} \int_E g_N^p \, d\mu \le 1^p = 1 < \infty.
   \]
   Thus $g \in L^p(E)$.
3. *Pointwise convergence almost everywhere:*
   Because $\int_E g^p < \infty$, by Theorem 8.3(5), $g(x) < \infty$ for almost every $x \in E$.
   For any such $x$, the series of numbers $\sum_{j=1}^\infty (f_{k_{j+1}}(x) - f_{k_j}(x))$ is *absolutely convergent*!
   Therefore, the telescoping series converges:
   \[
   f_{k_1}(x) + \sum_{j=1}^\infty \bigl(f_{k_{j+1}}(x) - f_{k_j}(x)\bigr) = \lim_{j \to \infty} f_{k_j}(x) =: f(x) \in \mathbb{R}.
   \]
   Define $f(x) = 0$ on the null set where $g(x) = \infty$. Then $f_{k_j} \to f$ pointwise a.e. on $E$.
4. *$L^p$ convergence of the subsequence:*
   Notice that for any $j \in \mathbb{N}$ and almost all $x$:
   \[
   \lvert f(x) - f_{k_j}(x) \rvert = \lim_{m \to \infty} |f_{k_m}(x) - f_{k_j}(x)| \le \sum_{l=j}^\infty |f_{k_{l+1}}(x) - f_{k_l}(x)| \le g(x).
   \]
   By Fatou's Lemma (applied to $|f - f_{k_j}|^p$):
   \[
   \int_E |f - f_{k_j}|^p \, d\mu \le \liminf_{m \to \infty} \int_E |f_{k_m} - f_{k_j}|^p \, d\mu \le \left( \sum_{l=j}^\infty \frac{1}{2^l} \right)^p = \left( \frac{1}{2^{j-1}} \right)^p.
   \]
   Taking \(p\)-th roots: $\|f - f_{k_j}\|_p \le \frac{1}{2^{j-1}} \to 0$ as $j \to \infty$.
   Thus $f_{k_j} \to f$ in $L^p(E)$, and since $f = (f - f_{k_1}) + f_{k_1}$, $f \in L^p(E)$.
5. *$L^p$ convergence of the full sequence:*
   Finally, by the triangle inequality:
   \[
   \|f_k - f\|_p \le \|f_k - f_{k_j}\|_p + \|f_{k_j} - f\|_p.
   \]
   Given $\varepsilon > 0$, since $(f_k)$ is Cauchy, $\|f_k - f_{k_j}\|_p < \varepsilon/2$ for large $k, k_j$. 
   Since $\|f_{k_j} - f\|_p \to 0$, choosing $j$ large gives $\|f_k - f\|_p < \varepsilon$.
   Thus $\lim_{k \to \infty} \|f_k - f\|_p = 0$.
#+end_proof

* Subsequences and Convergence Modes

#+begin_quote
*Corollary 10.8 (Subsequence Property).* If $f_k \to f$ in $L^p(E)$, then there exists a subsequence $(f_{k_j})_{j=1}^\infty$ that converges *pointwise almost everywhere* to $f$:
\[
f_{k_j}(x) \to f(x) \quad \text{for a.e. } x \in E.
\]
#+end_quote

#+begin_quote
*Warning (Convergence in $L^p$ does NOT imply pointwise convergence):*
The full sequence $f_k$ can converge to $0$ in $L^p$ while *failing to converge at any single point*!
#+end_quote

*The Typewriter (Sliding Interval) Counterexample:*
On $[0, 1]$, define the sequence of indicator functions:
- Level 1: $f_1 = \chi_{[0, 1]}$.
- Level 2: $f_2 = \chi_{[0, 1/2]}, \, f_3 = \chi_{[1/2, 1]}$.
- Level 3: $f_4 = \chi_{[0, 1/4]}, \, f_5 = \chi_{[1/4, 2/4]}, \, f_6 = \chi_{[2/4, 3/4]}, \, f_7 = \chi_{[3/4, 1]}$.
- In general, at level $m$, we have $2^m$ intervals of width $2^{-m}$.
Notice:
1. *Convergence in $L^p$:* For any $f_k$ at level $m$:
   \[
   \|f_k\|_p = \left( \int_0^1 \chi^p \right)^{1/p} = (2^{-m})^{1/p} \to 0 \quad \text{as } m \to \infty.
   \]
   Thus $f_k \to 0$ in $L^p([0, 1])$.
2. *Pointwise Behavior:* For *every* point $x \in [0, 1]$, $x$ is covered by infinitely many intervals, and missed by infinitely many intervals.
   Therefore, the sequence of numbers $f_k(x)$ oscillates endlessly: $1, 0, 1, 0, 1, 0 \dots$!
   The limit $\lim_{k \to \infty} f_k(x)$ *does not exist at any point in $[0, 1]$*!
   (Yet, the subsequence $f_{2^m} = \chi_{[0, 2^{-m}]}$ converges pointwise to $0$ everywhere on $]0, 1]$, illustrating Corollary 10.8).

* Worked Exercises

** Exercise 10.1 (Inclusion of $L^p$ Spaces on Finite Measure Spaces)
Assume $(X, \mathcal{M}, \mu)$ has finite measure: $\mu(X) < \infty$.
Let $1 \le p < q \le \infty$.
Prove that $L^q(X) \subset L^p(X)$, and that for all $f \in L^q(X)$:
\[
\|f\|_p \le \|f\|_q \, \mu(X)^{\frac{1}{p} - \frac{1}{q}}.
\]

*Solution:*
1. Case $q = \infty$: If $f \in L^\infty(X)$, then $|f(x)| \le \|f\|_\infty$ a.e.
   \[
   \int_X |f|^p \, d\mu \le \int_X \|f\|_\infty^p \, d\mu = \|f\|_\infty^p \mu(X) < \infty.
   \]
   Taking \(p\)-th roots: $\|f\|_p \le \|f\|_\infty \mu(X)^{1/p}$.
2. Case $1 \le p < q < \infty$:
   We want to apply Hölder's Inequality to $\int_X |f|^p \cdot 1 \, d\mu$.
   Choose the exponent $r = q/p > 1$. Its Hölder conjugate is $s = \frac{r}{r - 1} = \frac{q/p}{q/p - 1} = \frac{q}{q - p}$.
   By Hölder's Inequality (Theorem 10.5):
   \[
   \int_X |f|^p \cdot 1 \, d\mu \le \left( \int_X (|f|^p)^r \, d\mu \right)^{1/r} \left( \int_X 1^s \, d\mu \right)^{1/s} = \left( \int_X |f|^q \, d\mu \right)^{p/q} \bigl( \mu(X) \bigr)^{(q - p)/q}.
   \]
3. Taking the \(p\)-th root of both sides:
   \[
   \|f\|_p = \left( \int_X |f|^p \, d\mu \right)^{1/p} \le \left( \int_X |f|^q \, d\mu \right)^{1/q} \bigl( \mu(X) \bigr)^{\frac{q - p}{p q}} = \|f\|_q \, \mu(X)^{\frac{1}{p} - \frac{1}{q}}.
   \]
4. Since $\mu(X) < \infty$, the factor $\mu(X)^{\frac{1}{p} - \frac{1}{q}}$ is finite.
   Therefore, $\|f\|_q < \infty \implies \|f\|_p < \infty$.
   Thus $L^q(X) \subset L^p(X)$.

** Exercise 10.2 (Counterexample to Inclusion on Infinite Measure Spaces)
Show that on $(\mathbb{R}, \mathcal{M}, m)$ (where $m(\mathbb{R}) = \infty$), neither $L^1(\mathbb{R}) \subset L^2(\mathbb{R})$ nor $L^2(\mathbb{R}) \subset L^1(\mathbb{R})$ holds!

*Solution:*
1. *In $L^1$ but NOT in $L^2$:*
   Consider $f(x) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{x}} \chi_{]0, 1[}(x)$.
   - $\int_{\mathbb{R}} |f| \, dx = \int_0^1 x^{-1/2} \, dx = [2 x^{1/2}]_0^1 = 2 < \infty \implies f \in L^1(\mathbb{R})$.
   - $\int_{\mathbb{R}} |f|^2 \, dx = \int_0^1 \frac{1}{x} \, dx = [\ln x]_0^1 = \infty \implies f \notin L^2(\mathbb{R})$.
   Thus $L^1(\mathbb{R}) \not\subset L^2(\mathbb{R})$.
2. *In $L^2$ but NOT in $L^1$:*
   Consider $g(x) = \frac{1}{x} \chi_{[1, \infty[}(x)$.
   - $\int_{\mathbb{R}} |g| \, dx = \int_1^\infty \frac{1}{x} \, dx = [\ln x]_1^\infty = \infty \implies g \notin L^1(\mathbb{R})$.
   - $\int_{\mathbb{R}} |g|^2 \, dx = \int_1^\infty \frac{1}{x^2} \, dx = \left[ -\frac{1}{x} \right]_1^\infty = 1 < \infty \implies g \in L^2(\mathbb{R})$.
   Thus $L^2(\mathbb{R}) \not\subset L^1(\mathbb{R})$.
On spaces of infinite measure, $L^p$ spaces cross each other without containment!
