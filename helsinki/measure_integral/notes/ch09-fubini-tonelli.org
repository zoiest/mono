* Overview

** What problem does this chapter solve?
In multivariable calculus, we routinely evaluate double and triple integrals by converting them into iterated single integrals:
\[
\iint_{\mathbb{R}^2} f(x, y) \, dx \, dy \overset{?}{=} \int_{-\infty}^\infty \left( \int_{-\infty}^\infty f(x, y) \, dy \right) dx \overset{?}{=} \int_{-\infty}^\infty \left( \int_{-\infty}^\infty f(x, y) \, dx \right) dy.
\]
In elementary courses, this interchange of integration order is taken for granted. 
However, in advanced analysis, swapping the order of integration can easily produce mathematical nonsense: one order might equal $+\pi/4$, while the reverse order equals $-\pi/4$!

The goal of this chapter is to place multivariable integration on an unshakeable measure-theoretic foundation:
1. *Product Measure:* How does Lebesgue measure on $\mathbb{R}^{n+k}$ relate to the product of Lebesgue measures on $\mathbb{R}^n$ and $\mathbb{R}^k$?
2. *Tonelli's Theorem (for $f \ge 0$):* For *any* non-negative measurable function, the order of integration can *always* be interchanged without checking integrability. If the integral diverges, both iterated integrals equal $+\infty$.
3. *Fubini's Theorem (for $f \in L^1$):* If $\int |f| < \infty$, the slice functions are integrable almost everywhere, and both iterated integrals agree with the double integral.
4. *The Standard Analytical Workflow:* How mathematicians use Tonelli to prove integrability, and then Fubini to compute the value.

** Roadmap
- *9.1 Product Spaces and Sections:* Definitions of \(x\)-sections of sets and slice functions.
- *9.2 Tonelli's Theorem:* Swapping integrals for non-negative functions ($f \ge 0$).
- *9.3 Fubini's Theorem:* Swapping integrals for integrable functions ($f \in L^1$).

- *9.5 The Classic Counterexample:* When Tonelli warns that Fubini fails.

* Product Spaces and Sections

Let $n, k \in \mathbb{N}$. We identify the Euclidean space $\mathbb{R}^{n+k}$ with the Cartesian product $\mathbb{R}^n \times \mathbb{R}^k$, so a point is written $z = (x, y)$ with $x \in \mathbb{R}^n$ and $y \in \mathbb{R}^k$.
Let $m_n$, $m_k$, and $m_{n+k}$ denote Lebesgue measures on $\mathbb{R}^n$, $\mathbb{R}^k$, and $\mathbb{R}^{n+k}$, respectively.

** Sections of Sets
#+begin_quote
*Definition 9.1 (Sections of Sets).* Let $E \subset \mathbb{R}^{n+k}$.
- For any fixed $x \in \mathbb{R}^n$, the *\(x\)-section* of $E$ is
  \[
  E_x = \{y \in \mathbb{R}^k : (x, y) \in E\} \subset \mathbb{R}^k.
  \]
- For any fixed $y \in \mathbb{R}^k$, the *\(y\)-section* of $E$ is
  \[
  E^y = \{x \in \mathbb{R}^n : (x, y) \in E\} \subset \mathbb{R}^n.
  \]
#+end_quote

#+begin_quote
*Theorem 9.2.* If $E \in \mathcal{M}(\mathbb{R}^{n+k})$ is a measurable set, then:
1. For almost every $x \in \mathbb{R}^n$, the section $E_x$ is a measurable set in $\mathbb{R}^k$ ($E_x \in \mathcal{M}(\mathbb{R}^k)$).
2. The function $x \mapsto m_k(E_x)$ is a measurable function on $\mathbb{R}^n$.
3. The measure of $E$ is obtained by integrating the cross-sectional measures (Cavalieri's Principle):
   \[
   m_{n+k}(E) = \int_{\mathbb{R}^n} m_k(E_x) \, dm_n(x) = \int_{\mathbb{R}^k} m_n(E^y) \, dm_k(y).
   \]
#+end_quote

** Slice Functions
#+begin_quote
*Definition 9.3 (Slice Functions).* Let $f: \mathbb{R}^{n+k} \to \overline{\mathbb{R}}$.
- For fixed $x \in \mathbb{R}^n$, the slice function $f_x: \mathbb{R}^k \to \overline{\mathbb{R}}$ is defined by $f_x(y) = f(x, y)$.
- For fixed $y \in \mathbb{R}^k$, the slice function $f^y: \mathbb{R}^n \to \overline{\mathbb{R}}$ is defined by $f^y(x) = f(x, y)$.
#+end_quote

* Tonelli's Theorem (Non-Negative Functions)

Tonelli's Theorem (1909) is the engine of product integration. 
Its miraculous feature is that **it requires no finiteness or integrability assumptions whatsoever**—only non-negativity and measurability!

#+begin_quote
*Theorem 9.4 (Tonelli's Theorem).* Let $f: \mathbb{R}^{n+k} \to [0, +\infty]$ be a non-negative Lebesgue measurable function.
Then:
1. For almost every $x \in \mathbb{R}^n$, the slice function $y \mapsto f(x, y)$ is measurable on $\mathbb{R}^k$.
2. The function $x \mapsto \int_{\mathbb{R}^k} f(x, y) \, dm_k(y)$ is measurable on $\mathbb{R}^n$.
3. The iterated integrals and the product integral are strictly equal:
   \[
   \int_{\mathbb{R}^{n+k}} f \, dm_{n+k} = \int_{\mathbb{R}^n} \left( \int_{\mathbb{R}^k} f(x, y) \, dm_k(y) \right) dm_n(x) = \int_{\mathbb{R}^k} \left( \int_{\mathbb{R}^n} f(x, y) \, dm_n(x) \right) dm_k(y).
   \]
(If any of the three integrals is infinite, all three are $+\infty$).
#+end_quote

#+begin_proof
*Proof Architecture:*
1. *Indicator functions of measurable rectangles:* Let $f = \chi_{A \times B}$, where $A \in \mathcal{M}(\mathbb{R}^n)$ and $B \in \mathcal{M}(\mathbb{R}^k)$.
   Then $f(x, y) = \chi_A(x) \chi_B(y)$.
   The inner integral is $\int_{\mathbb{R}^k} \chi_A(x) \chi_B(y) \, dy = \chi_A(x) m_k(B)$.
   The outer integral is $\int_{\mathbb{R}^n} \chi_A(x) m_k(B) \, dx = m_n(A) m_k(B) = m_{n+k}(A \times B)$.
   The equality holds identically for rectangles.
2. *Simple functions:* By linearity of non-negative integrals (Theorem 7.7), Tonelli holds for all non-negative simple functions $s = \sum c_i \chi_{E_i}$.
3. *General non-negative measurable functions:* By the Simple Function Approximation Theorem (Theorem 6.10), choose simple functions $0 \le s_1 \le s_2 \le \dots \nearrow f$.
   Apply Tonelli to each $s_j$.
   By the Monotone Convergence Theorem (Theorem 7.6), we pass the limit $j \to \infty$ through both the inner and outer integrals.
   The equality carries over to $f$.
#+end_proof

* Fubini's Theorem (Integrable Functions)

When a function $f$ takes both positive and negative values, we cannot apply Tonelli directly. 
Instead, Guido Fubini (1907) proved that if the function is *integrable* ($\int |f| < \infty$), the order of integration can be reversed.

#+begin_quote
*Theorem 9.5 (Fubini's Theorem).* Let $f: \mathbb{R}^{n+k} \to \overline{\mathbb{R}}$ be Lebesgue integrable:
\[
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n+k}} |f| \, dm_{n+k} < \infty \quad (f \in L^1(\mathbb{R}^{n+k})).
\]
Then:
1. For almost every $x \in \mathbb{R}^n$, the slice function $y \mapsto f(x, y)$ is Lebesgue integrable on $\mathbb{R}^k$:
   \[
   f_x \in L^1(\mathbb{R}^k) \quad \text{for a.e. } x \in \mathbb{R}^n.
   \]
2. The function defined almost everywhere on $\mathbb{R}^n$ by
   \[
   F(x) = \int_{\mathbb{R}^k} f(x, y) \, dm_k(y)
   \]
   is Lebesgue integrable on $\mathbb{R}^n$ ($F \in L^1(\mathbb{R}^n)$).
3. The iterated integrals equal the product integral:
   \[
   \int_{\mathbb{R}^{n+k}} f \, dm_{n+k} = \int_{\mathbb{R}^n} \left( \int_{\mathbb{R}^k} f(x, y) \, dm_k(y) \right) dm_n(x) = \int_{\mathbb{R}^k} \left( \int_{\mathbb{R}^n} f(x, y) \, dm_n(x) \right) dm_k(y).
   \]
#+end_quote

#+begin_proof
1. Decompose $f = f^+ - f^-$ into its positive and negative parts.
2. Since $f \in L^1(\mathbb{R}^{n+k})$, both $\int f^+ < \infty$ and $\int f^- < \infty$.
3. Since $f^+ \ge 0$ and $f^- \ge 0$, Tonelli's Theorem (Theorem 9.4) applies to each part separately:
   \[
   \int f^+ = \int \left( \int f^+(x, y) \, dy \right) dx < \infty, \qquad \int f^- = \int \left( \int f^-(x, y) \, dy \right) dx < \infty.
   \]
4. Because the outer integrals are finite, their integrands must be finite almost everywhere:
   $\int f^+(x, y) \, dy < \infty$ and $\int f^-(x, y) \, dy < \infty$ for almost every $x \in \mathbb{R}^n$.
   This means $y \mapsto f(x, y)$ is integrable on $\mathbb{R}^k$ for almost every $x$.
5. By linearity of the integral (Theorem 8.3):
   \[
   \int_{\mathbb{R}^k} f(x, y) \, dy = \int_{\mathbb{R}^k} f^+(x, y) \, dy - \int_{\mathbb{R}^k} f^-(x, y) \, dy.
   \]
6. Integrating over $x \in \mathbb{R}^n$ and using linearity again completes the proof.
#+end_proof

* The Standard Two-Step Working Procedure

#+begin_quote
*Tip (Standard Two-Step Recipe for Iterated Integrals):*
In mathematical practice, whenever you encounter an iterated integral, you should follow this exact two-step recipe:
1. *Step 1 (Test integrability using Tonelli):* Place absolute values inside the integrand and evaluate the iterated integral:
   \[
   I_{\text{abs}} = \int \left( \int \lvert f(x, y)\rvert \, dy \right) dx.
   \]
   Because $\lvert f\rvert \ge 0$, Tonelli guarantees you can compute this in whichever order is easiest.
2. *Step 2 (Evaluate using Fubini):*
   - If $I_{\text{abs}} < \infty$, then $f \in L^1$! You are legally licensed by Fubini's Theorem to swap the order of integration and evaluate the original integral $\iint f$.
   - If $I_{\text{abs}} = \infty$, Fubini does *not* apply, and swapping the order of integration may produce incorrect results!
#+end_quote

* The Classic Counterexample: When Fubini Fails

Consider the function defined on the unit square $[0, 1] \times [0, 1]$:
\[
f(x, y) = \frac{x^2 - y^2}{(x^2 + y^2)^2} \quad \text{for } (x, y) \neq (0, 0), \quad f(0, 0) = 0.
\]

** Calculation 1: Integrate with respect to $y$ first
Notice that $\frac{\partial}{\partial y} \left( \frac{y}{x^2 + y^2} \right) = \frac{(x^2 + y^2) - y(2y)}{(x^2 + y^2)^2} = \frac{x^2 - y^2}{(x^2 + y^2)^2} = f(x, y)$.
Therefore, for any fixed $x \in ]0, 1]$:
\[
\int_0^1 f(x, y) \, dy = \left[ \frac{y}{x^2 + y^2} \right]_{y=0}^{y=1} = \frac{1}{x^2 + 1} - 0 = \frac{1}{1 + x^2}.
\]
Now integrate with respect to $x$:
\[
\int_0^1 \left( \int_0^1 f(x, y) \, dy \right) dx = \int_0^1 \frac{1}{1 + x^2} \, dx = [\arctan(x)]_0^1 = \frac{\pi}{4}.
\]

** Calculation 2: Integrate with respect to $x$ first
Because $f(x, y) = -f(y, x)$, by exact symmetry:
\[
\int_0^1 f(x, y) \, dx = -\frac{1}{1 + y^2}.
\]
Integrating with respect to $y$:
\[
\int_0^1 \left( \int_0^1 f(x, y) \, dx \right) dy = \int_0^1 -\frac{1}{1 + y^2} \, dy = -\frac{\pi}{4}.
\]

** The Discrepancy
\[
\int_0^1 \left( \int_0^1 f(x, y) \, dy \right) dx = \frac{\pi}{4} \;\neq\; -\frac{\pi}{4} = \int_0^1 \left( \int_0^1 f(x, y) \, dx \right) dy!
\]
*Why did Fubini fail?*
Let's check Tonelli's integrability condition:
Convert to polar coordinates: $|f(r, \theta)| = \frac{|r^2 \cos^2\theta - r^2 \sin^2\theta|}{r^4} = \frac{|\cos(2\theta)|}{r^2}$.
Then:
\[
\iint_{[0, 1]^2} |f(x, y)| \, dx \, dy \ge \int_0^{\pi/2} |\cos(2\theta)| \, d\theta \int_0^1 \frac{1}{r^2} \, r \, dr = \text{const} \times \int_0^1 \frac{1}{r} \, dr = \infty.
\]
Because $\int |f| = \infty$, $f$ is *not integrable*: $f \notin L^1([0, 1]^2)$. 
Fubini's hypothesis was violated!

* Worked Exercises

** Exercise 9.1 (The Gaussian Integral via Fubini--Tonelli)
Compute the famous Gaussian integral:
\[
I = \int_{-\infty}^\infty e^{-x^2} \, dx.
\]

*Solution:*
1. Consider the function $f: \mathbb{R}^2 \to \mathbb{R}$ given by $f(x, y) = e^{-(x^2 + y^2)} = e^{-x^2} e^{-y^2}$.
2. Notice that $f(x, y) \ge 0$ for all $(x, y) \in \mathbb{R}^2$.
   By *Tonelli's Theorem* (Theorem 9.4), we can compute the product integral as an iterated integral:
   \[
   \iint_{\mathbb{R}^2} e^{-(x^2 + y^2)} \, dm_2 = \int_{-\infty}^\infty e^{-x^2} \left( \int_{-\infty}^\infty e^{-y^2} \, dy \right) dx = \left( \int_{-\infty}^\infty e^{-x^2} \, dx \right) \left( \int_{-\infty}^\infty e^{-y^2} \, dy \right) = I^2.
   \]
3. On the other hand, compute the double integral using polar coordinates $x = r \cos\theta$, $y = r \sin\theta$ ($dx \, dy = r \, dr \, d\theta$):
   Since the integrand is non-negative, Tonelli allows switching to polar slices:
   \[
   \iint_{\mathbb{R}^2} e^{-(x^2 + y^2)} \, dm_2 = \int_0^{2\pi} d\theta \int_0^\infty e^{-r^2} r \, dr.
   \]
4. Evaluate the single integrals:
   \[
   \int_0^{2\pi} d\theta = 2\pi, \qquad \int_0^\infty e^{-r^2} r \, dr = \left[ -\frac{1}{2} e^{-r^2} \right]_0^\infty = 0 - \left(-\frac{1}{2}\right) = \frac{1}{2}.
   \]
5. Multiplying:
   \[
   I^2 = 2\pi \cdot \frac{1}{2} = \pi \implies I = \sqrt{\pi}.
   \]
Thus $\int_{-\infty}^\infty e^{-x^2} \, dx = \sqrt{\pi}$.

** Exercise 9.2 (Volume under Graph / Hypograph)
Let $E \in \mathcal{M}(\mathbb{R}^n)$ and let $f: E \to [0, +\infty]$ be measurable. 
The *subgraph* (or hypograph) of $f$ is
\[
\mathcal{U}(f) = \{(x, y) \in \mathbb{R}^n \times \mathbb{R} : x \in E, \, 0 \le y \le f(x)\}.
\]
Prove that $\mathcal{U}(f)$ is a measurable set in $\mathbb{R}^{n+1}$ and that its measure equals the integral of $f$:
\[
m_{n+1}(\mathcal{U}(f)) = \int_E f \, dm_n.
\]

*Solution:*
1. Consider the indicator function $\chi_{\mathcal{U}(f)}(x, y)$ on $\mathbb{R}^n \times \mathbb{R}$.
2. Notice that $\chi_{\mathcal{U}(f)}(x, y) \ge 0$.
3. By Tonelli's Theorem (Theorem 9.4), we integrate with respect to $y \in \mathbb{R}$ first:
   For a fixed $x \in E$, the \(x\)-section of the hypograph is the 1-dimensional interval:
   \[
   (\mathcal{U}(f))_x = \{y \in \mathbb{R} : 0 \le y \le f(x)\} = [0, f(x)].
   \]
4. The 1-dimensional measure of this interval is:
   \[
   m_1\bigl((\mathcal{U}(f))_x\bigr) = f(x) - 0 = f(x).
   \]
5. Integrating over $x \in E$ using Cavalieri's principle (Theorem 9.2):
   \[
   m_{n+1}(\mathcal{U}(f)) = \int_E m_1\bigl((\mathcal{U}(f))_x\bigr) \, dm_n(x) = \int_E f(x) \, dm_n(x).
   \]
This proves that the abstract Lebesgue integral is geometrically identical to the \((n+1)\)-dimensional volume beneath the graph!
