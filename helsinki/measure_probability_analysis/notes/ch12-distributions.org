* Overview

** What problem does this chapter solve?

In Chapters 1–11 we built the general machinery of measure spaces, integration, and product spaces.
Starting with this chapter, we put that machinery to work to study *real-valued random variables*
systematically.

A real-valued random variable \(X\) on a probability space \((\Omega, \mathcal F, \mathbb P)\) induces
its *law* (or *distribution*) \(\mu_X = X_\# \mathbb P\), which is a Borel probability measure on
\(\mathbb R\) defined by \(\mu_X(B) = \mathbb P(X \in B)\) for every \(B \in \mathcal B(\mathbb R)\)
(Chapter 3). Dealing directly with a function on the uncountable \(\sigma\)-algebra \(\mathcal B(\mathbb R)\)
sounds daunting, until we remember from Chapter 4 that a finite Borel measure on \(\mathbb R\) is
uniquely determined by its values on half-lines \((-\infty, x]\). That single observation lets us
compress the entire probability measure \(\mu_X\) into an ordinary real-valued function of one real
variable: its *cumulative distribution function (CDF)*
\[
F_X(x) := \mathbb P(X \le x) = \mu_X((-\infty, x]), \qquad x \in \mathbb R.
\]

This chapter answers three fundamental questions about laws and CDFs on \(\mathbb R\):

1. *From laws to CDFs (Section 12.1):* Which functions \(F : \mathbb R \to [0,1]\) can arise as the CDF
   of a random variable? Proposition 12.1 shows that every CDF is nondecreasing, right-continuous, and
   tends to \(0\) at \(-\infty\) and \(1\) at \(+\infty\), with jumps corresponding to point masses
   (*atoms*).
2. *From CDFs back to measures (Section 12.2):* Conversely, if we write down *any* nondecreasing,
   right-continuous function \(F : \mathbb R \to \mathbb R\), does there exist a Borel measure \(\mu_F\)
   on \(\mathbb R\) whose interval increments are \(\mu_F((a,b]) = F(b) - F(a)\)? Theorem 12.3
   (Lebesgue–Stieltjes measure) proves that the answer is *yes*, and shows how integrating against
   \(\mu_F\) unifies discrete sums, continuous density integrals, and mixed distributions under a single
   integral \(\int g(x)\,dF(x)\).
3. *From a CDF to an explicit random variable (Section 12.3):* Given a valid CDF \(F\), can we construct
   an explicit random variable \(X\) having CDF \(F\) on a concrete probability space? Proposition 12.6
   (the *quantile transform*) shows that if \(U\) is uniform on \((0,1)\) and \(Q(u) = \inf\{x : F(x) \ge u\}\)
   is the generalized inverse of \(F\), then \(X = Q(U)\) has CDF \(F\).

** Probability motivation

In elementary probability courses, discrete random variables (specified by probability mass functions
and summed with \(\sum_x g(x) p(x)\)) and continuous random variables (specified by probability density
functions and integrated with \(\int g(x) f(x)\,dx\)) are treated as two separate worlds. Worse still,
many real-world variables belong to *neither* world:
- Insurance claims or daily rainfall equals \(0\) with positive probability (say \(1/2\), an *atom* at
  \(0\)), and varies continuously over \((0, \infty)\) when positive (Example 12.2).
- Singular distributions (such as the random ternary series in Example 13.7 of the next chapter) have
  *no* atoms (continuous CDF) yet *no* density either!

The Lebesgue–Stieltjes measure \(\mu_F\) and the quantile transform \(Q(U)\) handle *all* probability
laws on \(\mathbb R\) on an equal footing. Note also what the law \(\mu_X\) (or \(F_X\)) does *and does
not* record: it records the probabilities of all value events \(\{X \in B\}\), so it determines
\(\mathbb E[g(X)]\) for every Borel function \(g\), but it does *not* retain how \(X\) is coupled to
other random variables \(Y\) on \(\Omega\) (that is the subject of Chapter 13).

* Cumulative distribution functions

** Intuition

Think of a probability measure \(\mu_X\) on \(\mathbb R\) as one unit of "probability mass" (or sand)
spread out along the real line. Some of the sand may be spread smoothly like a layer of dust, and some
may be concentrated in heavy pebbles (*atoms*) at specific points.

To describe this distribution of mass by a function of one variable \(x \in \mathbb R\), imagine
sweeping a bulldozer from \(-\infty\) up to position \(x\), *including* the point \(x\) itself, and
weighing all the mass collected in \((-\infty, x]\):
\[
F_X(x) = \mathbb P(X \le x) = \mu_X((-\infty, x]).
\]
What happens as we move \(x\) from left to right?
- As \(x\) moves right, we only collect *more* mass, so \(F_X(x)\) never decreases.
- If there is a heavy pebble of mass \(p > 0\) sitting at \(x_0\), then as \(x\) approaches \(x_0\) from
   the *left* (\(y \uparrow x_0\)), the bulldozer \((-\infty, y]\) stops just short of the pebble,
  measuring \(\mathbb P(X < x_0)\). The instant \(x\) reaches \(x_0\), the pebble is scooped up into
  \((-\infty, x_0]\), so \(F_X\) jumps up by \(p = \mathbb P(X = x_0)\).
- If \(x_n\) approaches \(x_0\) from the *right* (\(x_n \downarrow x_0\)), the pebble at \(x_0\) is
  already inside every \((-\infty, x_n]\), and the extra interval \((x_0, x_n]\) shrinks to the empty
  set, so \(F_X(x_n) \downarrow F_X(x_0)\) (*right-continuity*).

** Definition and basic properties

#+begin_quote
*Definition (Cumulative distribution function).* Let \(X\) be a real-valued random variable on
\((\Omega, \mathcal F, \mathbb P)\) with law \(\mu_X = X_\# \mathbb P\). The *cumulative distribution
function* (abbreviated *CDF*) of \(X\) is the function \(F_X : \mathbb R \to [0,1]\) defined by
\[
F_X(x) := \mathbb P(X \le x) = \mu_X((-\infty, x]), \qquad x \in \mathbb R.
\]
#+end_quote

#+begin_quote
*Proposition 12.1.* Every CDF \(F_X\) is nondecreasing and right-continuous, with limits zero at
\(-\infty\) and one at \(+\infty\):
\[
\lim_{x \to -\infty} F_X(x) = 0, \qquad \lim_{x \to +\infty} F_X(x) = 1.
\]
Furthermore, for every \(a < b\) and every \(x \in \mathbb R\),
\[
\mathbb P(a < X \le b) = F_X(b) - F_X(a), \qquad \mathbb P(X = x) = F_X(x) - F_X(x-),
\]
where \(F_X(x-) := \lim_{y \uparrow x} F_X(y)\) denotes the left-hand limit at \(x\).
#+end_quote

** Proof

Every single statement translates a set operation on half-lines into a property of the probability
measure \(\mathbb P\) (or \(\mu_X\)) from Chapter 2:

1. *Nondecreasing:* If \(x_1 \le x_2\), then \((-\infty, x_1] \subseteq (-\infty, x_2]\), so
   \(\{X \le x_1\} \subseteq \{X \le x_2\}\). By monotonicity of \(\mathbb P\),
   \(F_X(x_1) \le F_X(x_2)\).
2. *Right-continuity:* Fix \(x \in \mathbb R\) and let \(x_n \downarrow x\) (meaning \(x_1 \ge x_2 \ge \dots \ge x\)
   and \(\lim_{n\to\infty} x_n = x\)). Then the events \(A_n := \{X \le x_n\}\) decrease (\(A_1 \supseteq A_2 \supseteq \dots\)),
   and since \(X(\omega) \le x_n\) for all \(n\) holds if and only if \(X(\omega) \le x\), their
   intersection is
   \[
   \bigcap_{n=1}^\infty \{X \le x_n\} = \{X \le x\}.
   \]
   Since \(\mathbb P(A_1) \le 1 < \infty\), continuity of \(\mathbb P\) from above (Chapter 2) gives
   \(\lim_{n\to\infty} F_X(x_n) = F_X(x)\). Because \(F_X\) is monotone, checking sequential limits
   \(x_n \downarrow x\) implies \(\lim_{y \downarrow x} F_X(y) = F_X(x)\).
3. *Limits at \(\pm \infty\):* Since \(F_X\) is nondecreasing, its limits as \(x \to +\infty\) and
   \(x \to -\infty\) exist and equal the sequential limits along \(n \to \infty\) and \(-n \to -\infty\).
   - As \(n \uparrow \infty\), the events \(\{X \le n\}\) increase to \(\bigcup_{n=1}^\infty \{X \le n\} = \Omega\)
     (since \(X\) is real-valued, every outcome \(X(\omega) \in \mathbb R\) is \(\le n\) for some integer
     \(n\)). By continuity from below, \(\lim_{n\to\infty} F_X(n) = \mathbb P(\Omega) = 1\).
   - As \(n \uparrow \infty\), the events \(\{X \le -n\}\) decrease to \(\bigcap_{n=1}^\infty \{X \le -n\} = \emptyset\)
     (no real number is \(\le -n\) for all \(n\)). By continuity from above,
     \(\lim_{n\to\infty} F_X(-n) = \mathbb P(\emptyset) = 0\).
4. *Half-open interval formula:* For \(a < b\), we have the disjoint union
   \(\{X \le b\} = \{X \le a\} \sqcup \{a < X \le b\}\). Subtracting \(\mathbb P(X \le a) < \infty\)
   gives \(\mathbb P(a < X \le b) = F_X(b) - F_X(a)\).
5. *Point-mass (jump) formula:* Fix \(x \in \mathbb R\). Since \(F_X\) is nondecreasing, the left limit
   \(F_X(x-) = \lim_{y \uparrow x} F_X(y)\) exists and can be computed along \(y_n = x - 1/n \uparrow x\).
   The events \(\{X \le x - 1/n\}\) increase as \(n \uparrow \infty\), and a real number satisfies
   \(X(\omega) < x\) if and only if \(X(\omega) \le x - 1/n\) for some \(n \ge 1\):
   \[
   \bigcup_{n=1}^\infty \{X \le x - 1/n\} = \{X < x\}.
   \]
   By continuity from below, \(F_X(x-) = \lim_{n\to\infty} F_X(x - 1/n) = \mathbb P(X < x)\). Finally,
   since \(\{X \le x\} = \{X < x\} \sqcup \{X = x\}\), subtracting gives
   \(\mathbb P(X = x) = \mathbb P(X \le x) - \mathbb P(X < x) = F_X(x) - F_X(x-)\). \(\blacksquare\)

** Atoms and discontinuities of a CDF

#+begin_quote
*Definition (Atom).* A point \(x \in \mathbb R\) with \(\mathbb P(X = x) = \mu_X(\{x\}) > 0\) is called
an *atom* (or *point mass*) of the law \(\mu_X\).
#+end_quote

By Proposition 12.1, \(x\) is an atom of \(\mu_X\) if and only if \(F_X(x) - F_X(x-) > 0\), which means
\(F_X\) has a jump discontinuity at \(x\) (since \(F_X\) is right-continuous, \(F_X\) is continuous at
\(x\) iff \(F_X(x-) = F_X(x)\)).

*Why can a probability law have at most countably many atoms?*
Suppose we collect all atoms into the set \(A = \{x \in \mathbb R : \mathbb P(X = x) > 0\}\). Every
positive real number exceeds \(1/m\) for some integer \(m \ge 1\), so we can write \(A\) as a countable
union of threshold sets:
\[
A = \bigcup_{m=1}^\infty A_m, \qquad \text{where } A_m := \Big\{x \in \mathbb R : \mathbb P(X = x) \ge \frac{1}{m}\Big\}.
\]
How many points can belong to \(A_m\)? *At most \(m\)!* Indeed, if \(x_1, \dots, x_k \in A_m\) are distinct
points, the singletons \(\{X = x_1\}, \dots, \{X = x_k\}\) are pairwise disjoint events, so finite
additivity gives
\[
\frac{k}{m} \le \sum_{j=1}^k \mathbb P(X = x_j) = \mathbb P\big(X \in \{x_1, \dots, x_k\}\big) \le \mathbb P(\Omega) = 1 \quad \Longrightarrow \quad k \le m.
\]
Thus each \(A_m\) is a *finite* set (of size at most \(m\)), and \(A = \bigcup_{m=1}^\infty A_m\) is a
countable union of finite sets, hence *countable* (finite or countably infinite). In particular:

#+begin_quote
*Corollary.* Every CDF has at most countably many discontinuities, and all of its discontinuities are
jump discontinuities.
#+end_quote

** Example from the notes: a mixed law

#+begin_quote
*Example 12.2 (A law with an atom and a continuous part).* On \((\mathbb R, \mathcal B(\mathbb R))\),
let
\[
\mu = \frac{1}{2}\delta_0 + \frac{1}{2}\lambda|_{(0,1)},
\]
where \(\delta_0\) is the Dirac point mass at \(0\) and \(\lambda|_{(0,1)}\) is the restriction of
Lebesgue measure \(\lambda\) to \((0,1)\), defined by
\[
\lambda|_{(0,1)}(B) := \lambda\big(B \cap (0,1)\big), \qquad B \in \mathcal B(\mathbb R).
\]
Let us compute the CDF \(F(x) = \mu((-\infty, x])\) in three regimes:
1. *For \(x < 0\):* \((-\infty, x]\) does not contain \(0\) and does not intersect \((0,1)\), so
   \(F(x) = \frac{1}{2}(0) + \frac{1}{2}(0) = 0\).
2. *For \(0 \le x \le 1\):* \((-\infty, x]\) contains \(0\) (so \(\delta_0((-\infty, x]) = 1\)) and its
   intersection with \((0,1)\) is \((0,x]\) of length \(x\). Thus
   \[
   F(x) = \frac{1}{2}\cdot 1 + \frac{1}{2}\cdot x = \frac{1 + x}{2}.
   \]
3. *For \(x > 1\):* \((-\infty, x]\) contains \(0\) and all of \((0,1)\), so
   \(F(x) = \frac{1}{2}(1) + \frac{1}{2}(1) = 1\).

Thus \(\mu(\{0\}) = F(0) - F(0-) = 1/2 - 0 = 1/2\), while \(\mu((0, 1/2]) = F(1/2) - F(0) = 3/4 - 1/2 = 1/4\).
The same CDF contains both a discrete jump at \(0\) and a continuous sloping ramp on \([0,1]\). A
probability law need not be purely discrete or have a density!
#+end_quote

** Additional worked examples

*Example A (All four interval probabilities from \(F_X\)).* For \(a < b\), Proposition 12.1 gives
\(\mathbb P(a < X \le b) = F_X(b) - F_X(a)\). What about the other three endpoint combinations
\([a,b]\), \([a,b)\), and \((a,b)\)? Whenever you include or exclude an endpoint, simply add or subtract
the point mass at that endpoint using \(\mathbb P(X = c) = F_X(c) - F_X(c-)\):
- \(\mathbb P(a \le X \le b) = \mathbb P(X = a) + \mathbb P(a < X \le b) = F_X(b) - F_X(a-)\).
- \(\mathbb P(a < X < b) = \mathbb P(a < X \le b) - \mathbb P(X = b) = F_X(b-) - F_X(a)\).
- \(\mathbb P(a \le X < b) = F_X(b-) - F_X(a-)\).

For instance, in Example 12.2 with \(a = 0\) and \(b = 1/2\):
- \(\mu((0, 1/2]) = F(1/2) - F(0) = 3/4 - 1/2 = 1/4\),
- \(\mu([0, 1/2]) = F(1/2) - F(0-) = 3/4 - 0 = 3/4\) (includes the atom of mass \(1/2\) at \(0\)!).

* Constructing a measure from interval increments

** Intuition

Proposition 12.1 showed that every Borel probability measure \(\mu\) on \(\mathbb R\) produces a
nondecreasing, right-continuous function \(F\) with \(\mu((a,b]) = F(b) - F(a)\). Now we want to go
*backwards*: given *any* nondecreasing, right-continuous function \(F : \mathbb R \to \mathbb R\), can
we construct a locally finite Borel measure \(\mu_F\) on \(\mathbb R\) whose mass on every half-open
interval \((a,b]\) is \(F(b) - F(a)\)?

Wait — haven't we seen this story before? Yes! In Chapter 5, we constructed *Lebesgue measure* \(\lambda\)
from the special function \(F(x) = x\), where \(\lambda((a,b]) = b - a\). The exact same blueprint works
for a general nondecreasing right-continuous \(F\):
1. Work first on a bounded half-open interval \((-m, m]\), on the algebra of finite unions of half-open
   subintervals \((u, v]\), where we prescribe \(\mu_0((u,v]) := F(v) - F(u)\).
2. Check that \(\mu_0\) is continuous at \(\emptyset\) by shrinking each \((u,v]\) slightly at its
   *left* endpoint to \((u+\delta, v]\). Why does the lost mass \(\mu_0((u, u+\delta]) = F(u+\delta) - F(u)\)
   become tiny as \(\delta \downarrow 0\)? *Because \(F\) is right-continuous at \(u\)!* (This is the
   exact spot where right-continuity of \(F\) is used!)
3. Apply Carathéodory's theorem on \((-m, m]\) to get a finite Borel measure \(\mu_F^{(m)}\), and then
   let \(m \to \infty\).

** The Lebesgue–Stieltjes theorem

#+begin_quote
*Theorem 12.3 (Lebesgue–Stieltjes measure).* Let \(F : \mathbb R \to \mathbb R\) be right-continuous and
nondecreasing. There is a unique Borel measure \(\mu_F\) on \(\mathbb R\), finite on compact sets, such
that
\[
\mu_F((a,b]) = F(b) - F(a) \qquad (a < b). \tag{12.1}
\]
If in addition \(F(-\infty) := \lim_{x\to-\infty} F(x) = 0\) and \(F(+\infty) := \lim_{x\to+\infty} F(x) = 1\),
then \(\mu_F\) is a probability measure with CDF \(F\).
#+end_quote

** Proof

We follow the four steps of the notes, unpacking every detail.

*Step 1. Work on a bounded interval \((a,b]\) and define the premeasure on its algebra.*
Fix \(a < b\) and take \(X = (a,b]\) as the underlying space. Let \(\mathcal A\) be the algebra of all
finite unions of half-open intervals \((u,v] \subseteq (a,b]\) (recall from Chapter 5 that every
\(E \in \mathcal A\) can be written as a finite union of *pairwise disjoint* intervals
\(E = \bigsqcup_{i=1}^k (u_i, v_i]\)). Define \(\mu_0 : \mathcal A \to [0, \infty)\) by summing the
increments over the disjoint pieces:
\[
\mu_0\Big(\bigsqcup_{i=1}^k (u_i, v_i]\Big) := \sum_{i=1}^k \big(F(v_i) - F(u_i)\big) \ge 0.
\]
Why is \(\mu_0\) well-defined and finitely additive? If a single interval \((u,v]\) is split at an
interior point \(w \in (u,v)\) into \((u,w] \sqcup (w,v]\), the sum telescopes:
\[
\big(F(w) - F(u)\big) + \big(F(v) - F(w)\big) = F(v) - F(u).
\]
By induction, splitting \((u,v]\) at any finite number of points preserves the sum. Given any two
representations of \(E \in \mathcal A\) as disjoint unions of half-open intervals, taking the common
refinement formed by *all* endpoints of both representations splits each interval of either representation
into finitely many subintervals, and both sums equal the sum over the common refinement. Finite additivity
follows immediately, and the total mass \(\mu_0((a,b]) = F(b) - F(a) < \infty\) is finite.

*Step 2. Prove continuity at the empty set on \(\mathcal A\).*
By Chapter 5, a finitely additive finite measure \(\mu_0\) on an algebra \(\mathcal A\) is countably
additive (a premeasure) if and only if whenever \(E_1 \supseteq E_2 \supseteq \dots\) in \(\mathcal A\)
decrease to \(\bigcap_{n=1}^\infty E_n = \emptyset\), we have \(\lim_{n\to\infty} \mu_0(E_n) = 0\).

Fix \(\varepsilon > 0\) and fix \(n \ge 1\). The algebra set \(E_n\) is a disjoint union of finitely
many half-open intervals, say \(E_n = \bigsqcup_{i=1}^{k_n} (u_i, v_i]\) with \(u_i < v_i\). For each
piece \((u_i, v_i]\), because \(F\) is *right-continuous* at \(u_i\), we have
\[
\lim_{\delta \downarrow 0} \mu_0((u_i, u_i + \delta]) = \lim_{\delta \downarrow 0} \big(F(u_i + \delta) - F(u_i)\big) = 0.
\]
Choose \(\delta_i \in (0, v_i - u_i)\) small enough that \(F(u_i + \delta_i) - F(u_i) < \varepsilon 2^{-n} / k_n\).
Define the shrunken algebra set \(C_n \in \mathcal A\) and its closure \(K_n\) (which includes the new
left endpoints \(u_i + \delta_i\)) by
\[
C_n := \bigsqcup_{i=1}^{k_n} (u_i + \delta_i, v_i] \in \mathcal A, \qquad
K_n := \bigcup_{i=1}^{k_n} [u_i + \delta_i, v_i].
\]
Then \(K_n\) is a finite union of closed bounded intervals, hence *compact*, and
\[
C_n \subseteq K_n \subseteq E_n, \qquad
\mu_0(E_n \setminus C_n) = \sum_{i=1}^{k_n} \mu_0((u_i, u_i + \delta_i]) < k_n \cdot \frac{\varepsilon 2^{-n}}{k_n} = \varepsilon 2^{-n}.
\]
(Notice the careful bookkeeping: \(\mu_0\) is only defined on the algebra \(\mathcal A\), so we never
write "\(\mu_0(K_n)\)"; we only apply \(\mu_0\) to the algebra sets \(E_n\), \(C_n\), and \(E_n \setminus C_n\).)

Now consider the compact sets \(K_n \subseteq E_n\). Since \(\bigcap_{n=1}^\infty E_n = \emptyset\), we
also have \(\bigcap_{n=1}^\infty K_n = \emptyset\). By the finite intersection property of compact sets
(Chapter 0 / Chapter 8: if every finite intersection \(\bigcap_{j=1}^N K_j\) were nonempty, the
decreasing sequence of nonempty compact sets \(\widetilde K_N := \bigcap_{j=1}^N K_j \subseteq K_1\)
would have a common point, contradicting \(\bigcap_{n=1}^\infty K_n = \emptyset\)), there is some finite
index \(N \ge 1\) such that
\[
\bigcap_{j=1}^N K_j = \emptyset.
\]
Take any point \(x \in E_N\). Since \(E_1 \supseteq E_2 \supseteq \dots \supseteq E_N\), \(x\) belongs
to \(E_j\) for every \(1 \le j \le N\). On the other hand, since \(\bigcap_{j=1}^N K_j = \emptyset\),
\(x\) must fail to belong to \(K_j\) for at least one \(j \in \{1, \dots, N\}\). For that \(j\),
\(x \in E_j \setminus K_j \subseteq E_j \setminus C_j\) (because \(C_j \subseteq K_j\)). This proves the
set inclusion
\[
E_N \subseteq \bigcup_{j=1}^N (E_j \setminus C_j).
\]
Since \(E_N\) and all \(E_j \setminus C_j\) belong to the algebra \(\mathcal A\), finite subadditivity
of \(\mu_0\) on \(\mathcal A\) applies and yields
\[
\mu_0(E_N) \le \sum_{j=1}^N \mu_0(E_j \setminus C_j) < \sum_{j=1}^N \varepsilon 2^{-j} < \varepsilon.
\]
Since \(\mu_0(E_n)\) is nonincreasing in \(n\), we have \(\mu_0(E_n) \le \mu_0(E_N) < \varepsilon\) for
all \(n \ge N\). Because \(\varepsilon > 0\) was arbitrary, \(\lim_{n\to\infty} \mu_0(E_n) = 0\).

*Step 3. Extend to Borel sets on \((-m, m]\) and glue across \(m \ge 1\).*
By Carathéodory's extension theorem (Chapter 5), \(\mu_0\) extends to a finite measure on
\(((a,b], \sigma(\mathcal A))\). Since every open interval \((u,v) \subseteq (a,b]\) is a countable union
of half-open intervals \(\bigcup_{k} (u, v - 1/k]\), we have \(\sigma(\mathcal A) = \mathcal B((a,b])\).

Apply this construction on \((-m, m]\) for each integer \(m \ge 1\) to obtain a finite Borel measure
\(\mu_F^{(m)}\) on \((-m, m]\). For \(m' > m\), the restriction of \(\mu_F^{(m')}\) to \((-m, m]\)
agrees with \(\mu_F^{(m)}\) on all half-open subintervals \((u,v] \subseteq (-m, m]\) (both give
\(F(v) - F(u)\)), which form a \(\pi\)-system generating \(\mathcal B((-m,m])\). By the finite-measure
uniqueness theorem (Chapter 4), \(\mu_F^{(m')}\) restricts to \(\mu_F^{(m)}\) on \((-m, m]\).

Therefore, for any Borel set \(E \in \mathcal B(\mathbb R)\), the sequence \(\mu_F^{(m)}(E \cap (-m, m])\)
is *nondecreasing* in \(m\) (since \(E \cap (-m, m] \subseteq E \cap (-(m+1), m+1]\) and the measures
are compatible). We define
\[
\mu_F(E) := \lim_{m \to \infty} \mu_F^{(m)}\big(E \cap (-m, m]\big) \in [0, \infty].
\]
Clearly \(\mu_F(\emptyset) = 0\). To verify countable additivity of \(\mu_F\) on pairwise disjoint Borel
sets \(E_1, E_2, \dots \in \mathcal B(\mathbb R)\):
1. For each *fixed* \(m \ge 1\), countable additivity of the measure \(\mu_F^{(m)}\) gives
   \[
   \mu_F^{(m)}\Big(\Big(\bigsqcup_{j=1}^\infty E_j\Big) \cap (-m, m]\Big)
   = \sum_{j=1}^\infty \mu_F^{(m)}\big(E_j \cap (-m, m]\big).
   \]
2. As \(m \uparrow \infty\), each non-negative summand \(a_m(j) := \mu_F^{(m)}(E_j \cap (-m, m])\)
   increases monotonically to \(\mu_F(E_j)\). Viewing \(\sum_{j=1}^\infty a_m(j)\) as the integral of
   \(j \mapsto a_m(j)\) with respect to *counting measure* on \(\mathbb N\) (which does not depend on
   the measure \(\mu_F\) we are constructing!), the Monotone Convergence Theorem (Chapter 6) allows us
   to swap \(\lim_{m\to\infty}\) and \(\sum_{j=1}^\infty\):
   \[
   \mu_F\Big(\bigsqcup_{j=1}^\infty E_j\Big)
   = \lim_{m\to\infty} \sum_{j=1}^\infty \mu_F^{(m)}\big(E_j \cap (-m, m]\big)
   = \sum_{j=1}^\infty \lim_{m\to\infty} \mu_F^{(m)}\big(E_j \cap (-m, m]\big)
   = \sum_{j=1}^\infty \mu_F(E_j).
   \]
Thus \(\mu_F\) is a Borel measure on \(\mathbb R\). Every compact set \(K \subseteq \mathbb R\) is
bounded, so \(K \subseteq (-m, m]\) for some \(m \ge 1\), giving \(\mu_F(K) = \mu_F^{(m)}(K) \le F(m) - F(-m) < \infty\)
(locally finite). Similarly, any \((a,b]\) lies inside \((-m, m]\) for large \(m\), so
\(\mu_F((a,b]) = F(b) - F(a)\). Finally, if any other Borel measure \(\nu\) satisfies (12.1), then
\(\nu\) and \(\mu_F\) agree on each \((-m, m]\) by \(\pi\)-system uniqueness, hence on all of
\(\mathcal B(\mathbb R)\) by continuity from below along \(E \cap (-m, m] \uparrow E\).

*Step 4. Check the CDF case.*
Suppose \(\lim_{x\to-\infty} F(x) = 0\) and \(\lim_{x\to+\infty} F(x) = 1\). Since \((-m, m] \uparrow \mathbb R\)
as \(m \to \infty\), continuity from below gives
\[
\mu_F(\mathbb R) = \lim_{m\to\infty} \mu_F((-m, m]) = \lim_{m\to\infty} \big(F(m) - F(-m)\big) = 1 - 0 = 1,
\]
so \(\mu_F\) is a probability measure. And for any fixed \(x \in \mathbb R\), since \((-m, x] \uparrow (-\infty, x]\)
for \(m > |x|\), continuity from below gives
\[
\mu_F((-\infty, x]) = \lim_{m\to\infty} \mu_F((-m, x]) = \lim_{m\to\infty} \big(F(x) - F(-m)\big) = F(x) - 0 = F(x). \quad \blacksquare
\]

#+begin_quote
*Takeaway.* Combining Proposition 12.1 and Theorem 12.3 gives a *complete characterization of CDFs*: a
function \(F : \mathbb R \to \mathbb R\) is the CDF of a Borel probability measure on \(\mathbb R\) if
and only if \(F\) is nondecreasing, right-continuous, \(\lim_{x\to-\infty} F(x) = 0\), and
\(\lim_{x\to+\infty} F(x) = 1\). Notice also why we used half-open intervals \((a,b]\) with the right
endpoint included: the mass at \(b\) is retained in \((a,b]\) and reflected in \(F(b)\).
#+end_quote

** The Lebesgue–Stieltjes integral

#+begin_quote
*Definition (Lebesgue–Stieltjes integral).* For a nondecreasing right-continuous function
\(F : \mathbb R \to \mathbb R\) and a Borel measurable function \(g : \mathbb R \to \mathbb R\) (either
nonnegative, or integrable with respect to \(\mu_F\)), the *Lebesgue–Stieltjes integral* of \(g\) with
respect to \(F\), written
\[
\int g\,dF \qquad \text{or} \qquad \int_{\mathbb R} g(x)\,dF(x),
\]
is simply another notation for the measure-theoretic integral \(\int_{\mathbb R} g\,d\mu_F\). It is *not*
a separate definition of integration!
#+end_quote

In particular, if \(X\) is a real-valued random variable with law \(\mu_X = \mu_{F_X}\) and CDF \(F_X\),
the image-measure integration formula (Chapter 6) states that
\[
\mathbb E[g(X)] = \int_{\Omega} g(X(\omega))\,d\mathbb P(\omega) = \int_{\mathbb R} g(x)\,d\mu_X(x) = \int_{\mathbb R} g(x)\,dF_X(x)
\]
whenever either side is defined (i.e. \(g \ge 0\) Borel, or \(g(X) \in L^1(\mathbb P)\)).

** Examples and remarks from the notes

#+begin_quote
*Example 12.4 (Three kinds of integral).*
1. *Lebesgue measure:* If \(F(x) = x\), then \(\mu_F((a,b]) = b - a\), so \(\mu_F = \lambda\) is
   Lebesgue measure and \(\int g(x)\,dF(x) = \int g(x)\,dx\).
2. *Point mass:* If \(F(x) = \mathbf 1_{[a, \infty)}(x)\) (which is \(0\) for \(x < a\) and \(1\) for
   \(x \ge a\)), then \(\mu_F = \delta_a\) is the Dirac point mass at \(a\), and
   \(\int g(x)\,dF(x) = g(a)\).
3. *Mixed law:* For the mixed law \(\mu = \frac{1}{2}\delta_0 + \frac{1}{2}\lambda|_{(0,1)}\) of
   Example 12.2, integrating any Borel function \(g \ge 0\) (or \(g \in L^1(\mu)\)) gives
   \[
   \int_{\mathbb R} g\,dF = \frac{1}{2} g(0) + \frac{1}{2} \int_0^1 g(x)\,dx.
   \]
   For instance, if \(X\) has this law:
   - Its *mean* (\(g(x) = x\)) is \(\mathbb E[X] = \frac{1}{2}(0) + \frac{1}{2}\int_0^1 x\,dx = \frac{1}{2}\cdot\frac{1}{2} = \frac{1}{4}\).
   - Its *second moment* (\(g(x) = x^2\)) is \(\mathbb E[X^2] = \frac{1}{2}(0^2) + \frac{1}{2}\int_0^1 x^2\,dx = \frac{1}{2}\cdot\frac{1}{3} = \frac{1}{6}\).
   - What if we take \(g(x) = \mathbf 1_{\{0\}}(x)\) or \(g(x) = \cos x\)? Then \(\mathbb E[\cos X] = \frac{1}{2}\cos(0) + \frac{1}{2}\int_0^1 \cos x\,dx = \frac{1}{2} + \frac{\sin 1}{2}\).
   Notice that if you naively tried to replace \(\int_{\mathbb R} g(x)\,dF(x)\) by \(\int_{\mathbb R} g(x) F'(x)\,dx\)
   using the ordinary derivative \(F'(x) = \frac{1}{2}\mathbf 1_{(0,1)}(x)\) (where defined), you would
   get \(\frac{1}{2}\int_0^1 g(x)\,dx\), *completely losing the atom of mass \(1/2\) at zero* (for
   \(g \equiv 1\), it would give total probability \(1/2\) instead of \(1\)!).
#+end_quote

#+begin_quote
*Remark 12.5 (Densities are an additional property).* A probability law \(\mu\) on \(\mathbb R\) is said
to have a *density* (or *probability density function*, PDF) \(f\) with respect to Lebesgue measure if
\(f : \mathbb R \to [0, \infty)\) is Borel measurable and
\[
\mu(B) = \int_B f(x)\,dx \qquad \text{for every } B \in \mathcal B(\mathbb R).
\]
When a density \(f\) exists:
- Necessarily \(\int_{\mathbb R} f(x)\,dx = \mu(\mathbb R) = 1\), and the CDF is given by
  \(F(x) = \mu((-\infty, x]) = \int_{-\infty}^x f(t)\,dt\).
- Conversely, if \(f \ge 0\) is Borel measurable with \(\int_{\mathbb R} f(t)\,dt = 1\), then
  \(F(x) := \int_{-\infty}^x f(t)\,dt\) defines a CDF whose Lebesgue–Stieltjes measure \(\mu_F\) agrees
  with \(B \mapsto \int_B f(x)\,dx\) on all half-lines \((-\infty, x]\), hence on all Borel sets by
  uniqueness.
- *When can a law fail to have a density?*
  1. *Any atom rules out a density:* If \(\mu(\{x_0\}) > 0\), then \(\mu\) cannot have a density,
     because for any function \(f\), the Lebesgue integral over a singleton is always zero:
     \(\int_{\{x_0\}} f(x)\,dx = 0 \ne \mu(\{x_0\})\).
  2. *Even an atom-free law (continuous CDF) need not have a density!* In Section 13.3 (Example 13.7),
     we will construct a random variable \(Z = \sum_{n=1}^\infty 2 B_n 3^{-n}\) (the Cantor distribution)
     whose CDF is continuous everywhere (\(\mu_Z(\{x\}) = 0\) for all \(x\)), yet all of its probability
     mass lives on a Borel set \(K\) of Lebesgue measure \(\lambda(K) = 0\)! Any density would assign
     \(\int_K f(x)\,dx = 0\) to \(K\), so no density can exist.
#+end_quote

* Quantiles and realizing a prescribed law

** Intuition

Suppose someone hands you a valid CDF \(F\) — say the mixed law of Example 12.2, or an exponential CDF
\(F(x) = (1 - e^{-x})\mathbf 1_{[0,\infty)}(x)\). Does there actually exist a probability space
\((\Omega, \mathcal F, \mathbb P)\) and a random variable \(X\) on it whose CDF is \(F\)?
- Abstractly, yes: Theorem 12.3 gives the measure \(\mu_F\) on \((\mathbb R, \mathcal B(\mathbb R))\),
  and the identity map \(X(x) = x\) on \((\mathbb R, \mathcal B(\mathbb R), \mu_F)\) has CDF \(F\).
- Practically and probabilistically, we want much more: we want a single fixed probability space on
  which we can manufacture random variables with *any* prescribed CDF \(F\) out of a single standard
  \(\mathrm{Uniform}(0,1)\) random variable \(U\)!

How do we transform \(U \sim \mathrm{Uniform}(0,1)\) into a random variable \(X\) with CDF \(F\)?
First imagine the easy case where \(F : \mathbb R \to (0,1)\) is continuous and *strictly increasing*,
so it has a true inverse function \(F^{-1} : (0,1) \to \mathbb R\). Then for every \(u \in (0,1)\) and
\(x \in \mathbb R\),
\[
F^{-1}(u) \le x \quad \Longleftrightarrow \quad u \le F(x).
\]
Consequently, if \(X = F^{-1}(U)\), then \(\mathbb P(X \le x) = \mathbb P(F^{-1}(U) \le x) = \mathbb P(U \le F(x)) = F(x)\)!

What if \(F\) is a general CDF with *jumps* (atoms, where \(F\) skips over vertical intervals of \(u\))
and *flat spots* (empty regions of zero probability, where \(F(x) = u\) for a whole horizontal interval
of \(x\))?

#+ATTR_HTML: :width 600px :align center
[[file:figures/fig12_quantile.png]]

We replace \(F^{-1}(u)\) by the *quantile function* (or *generalized inverse*): for each threshold
\(u \in (0,1)\), look at all \(x \in \mathbb R\) where \(F(x)\) has reached at least level \(u\), and
take the *first* (infimum) such \(x\):
\[
Q(u) := \inf\{x \in \mathbb R : F(x) \ge u\}.
\]
Because \(F\) is *right-continuous*, the set \(\{x \in \mathbb R : F(x) \ge u\}\) is a closed half-line
\([Q(u), \infty)\) that *contains* its infimum \(Q(u)\)! That single fact makes the magic equivalence
\(Q(u) \le x \iff u \le F(x)\) hold for *every* CDF \(F\), jumps and flat spots included!

** Definition and the quantile construction

#+begin_quote
*Definition (Quantile function).* Let \(F : \mathbb R \to [0,1]\) be a CDF. For \(0 < u < 1\), define
its *quantile function* \(Q : (0,1) \to \mathbb R\) by
\[
Q(u) := \inf\{x \in \mathbb R : F(x) \ge u\}.
\]
#+end_quote

Let us check that \(Q(u)\) is always a finite real number for every \(u \in (0,1)\):
- *The set \(S_u := \{x \in \mathbb R : F(x) \ge u\}\) is nonempty:* Since \(\lim_{x\to+\infty} F(x) = 1 > u\),
  there exists \(x_1 \in \mathbb R\) large enough that \(F(x_1) \ge u\), so \(x_1 \in S_u\) and
  \(Q(u) \le x_1 < +\infty\).
- *The set \(S_u\) is bounded below:* Since \(\lim_{x\to-\infty} F(x) = 0 < u\), there exists
  \(x_0 \in \mathbb R\) negative enough that \(F(x) < u\) for all \(x \le x_0\). Thus every \(x \in S_u\)
  satisfies \(x > x_0\), so \(Q(u) \ge x_0 > -\infty\).

#+begin_quote
*Proposition 12.6 (Quantile construction).* Let \(F\) be a CDF with quantile function \(Q\). For every
\(x \in \mathbb R\) and \(u \in (0,1)\),
\[
Q(u) \le x \quad \Longleftrightarrow \quad u \le F(x). \tag{12.2}
\]
Consequently, \(Q : (0,1) \to \mathbb R\) is Borel measurable. If \(U : \Omega \to (0,1)\) is measurable
and has the uniform law \(\lambda|_{(0,1)}\), then the random variable \(X = Q(U)\) has CDF \(F\).
#+end_quote

** Proof

Fix \(u \in (0,1)\) and write \(q := Q(u) = \inf\{y \in \mathbb R : F(y) \ge u\}\).

1. *Key step: \(F\) attains at least \(u\) at the infimum \(q\), i.e. \(F(q) \ge u\).*
   For each integer \(n \ge 1\), since \(q + 1/n > q = \inf\{y : F(y) \ge u\}\), \(q + 1/n\) is not a
   lower bound for \(\{y : F(y) \ge u\}\). Therefore there exists some \(y_n < q + 1/n\) with
   \(F(y_n) \ge u\). Since \(F\) is nondecreasing and \(y_n < q + 1/n\), we have
   \[
   F(q + 1/n) \ge F(y_n) \ge u \qquad \text{for every } n \ge 1.
   \]
   Now let \(n \to \infty\). Since \(q + 1/n \downarrow q\) and \(F\) is *right-continuous* at \(q\),
   \[
   F(q) = \lim_{n\to\infty} F(q + 1/n) \ge u.
   \]
2. *Proof of (\(\Longrightarrow\)) in (12.2):* Suppose \(q = Q(u) \le x\). Since \(F\) is nondecreasing,
   \(F(x) \ge F(q) \ge u\) by Step 1.
3. *Proof of (\(\Longleftarrow\)) in (12.2):* Conversely, suppose \(u \le F(x)\). Then \(x\) belongs to
   the set \(\{y \in \mathbb R : F(y) \ge u\}\) whose infimum defines \(q = Q(u)\). Since the infimum of
   a set is \(\le\) every element of that set, \(Q(u) = q \le x\). This proves (12.2).
4. *Measurability of \(Q\):* By the half-line test for measurability (Chapter 3), \(Q : (0,1) \to \mathbb R\)
   is Borel measurable if and only if \(\{u \in (0,1) : Q(u) \le x\} \in \mathcal B((0,1))\) for every
   \(x \in \mathbb R\). By (12.2),
   \[
   \{u \in (0,1) : Q(u) \le x\} = \{u \in (0,1) : u \le F(x)\} = (0,1) \cap (0, F(x)],
   \]
   which is a half-open interval (or \(\emptyset\) if \(F(x) = 0\)), hence a Borel subset of \((0,1)\).
5. *CDF of \(Q(U)\):* Suppose \(U : \Omega \to (0,1)\) has uniform law on \((0,1)\), meaning
   \(\mathbb P(U \in (0, c]) = c\) for every \(c \in [0,1]\). For any \(x \in \mathbb R\), since
   \(F(x) \in [0,1]\), applying (12.2) at every outcome \(\omega \in \Omega\) gives
   \[
   \mathbb P(Q(U) \le x) = \mathbb P(U \le F(x)) = F(x). \quad \blacksquare
   \]

#+begin_quote
*Technical remark on the range of \(U\).* In practice (for example in Section 13.3, where \(U = \sum_{n=1}^\infty 2^{-n} B_n\)
is built from coin tosses), a random variable \(U : \Omega \to \mathbb R\) with the uniform law on
\((0,1)\) might take values in \([0,1]\) (or \(\mathbb R\)) rather than strictly in \((0,1)\) at every
single outcome \(\omega\), even though \(\mathbb P(U \notin (0,1)) = 0\). Because \(Q(u)\) is only
defined on \((0,1)\), before composing \(U\) with \(Q\) we redefine \(U\) on the measurable null event
\(N := \{U \notin (0,1)\} \in \mathcal F\) by setting
\[
\widetilde U(\omega) := \begin{cases} U(\omega) & \text{if } U(\omega) \in (0,1), \\ 1/2 & \text{if } U(\omega) \notin (0,1). \end{cases}
\]
This modification changes \(U\) only on the specified measurable null set \(N \in \mathcal F\), so it
preserves measurability and the uniform law (without needing any completeness assumption on
\((\Omega, \mathcal F, \mathbb P)\)!) and ensures \(\widetilde U(\omega) \in (0,1)\) for *every*
\(\omega \in \Omega\), making \(Q(\widetilde U(\omega))\) well-defined everywhere.
#+end_quote

** Examples from the notes

#+begin_quote
*Example 12.7 (An atom becomes an interval of quantiles; exponential law).*
1. *The mixed law of Example 12.2:* Recall that \(F(x) = 0\) for \(x < 0\), \(F(x) = (1+x)/2\) for
   \(0 \le x \le 1\), and \(F(x) = 1\) for \(x > 1\). Fix \(u \in (0,1)\) and ask: when is \(F(x) \ge u\)?
   - *Case 1: \(0 < u \le 1/2\).* For every \(x < 0\) we have \(F(x) = 0 < u\), whereas at \(x = 0\)
     the jump already reaches \(F(0) = 1/2 \ge u\). Thus \(\{x : F(x) \ge u\} = [0, \infty)\), whose
     infimum is \(Q(u) = 0\). Notice how the *entire half-interval* \(u \in (0, 1/2]\) of uniform
     parameters is mapped to the *single atom* \(0\), giving \(\mathbb P(Q(U) = 0) = \mathbb P(U \in (0, 1/2]) = 1/2\)!
   - *Case 2: \(1/2 < u < 1\).* Here \(F(x) \ge u\) requires \(x \in (0,1]\) with \((1+x)/2 \ge u\) (or
     \(x > 1\)), which solves to \(x \ge 2u - 1\). Thus \(\{x : F(x) \ge u\} = [2u - 1, \infty)\), and
     \(Q(u) = 2u - 1\).
   In summary:
   \[
   Q(u) = \begin{cases} 0, & 0 < u \le 1/2, \\ 2u - 1, & 1/2 < u < 1. \end{cases}
   \]
   You can also check (12.2) directly by substitution: e.g. for \(0 \le x \le 1\), \(Q(u) \le x\) holds
   for all \(u \in (0, 1/2]\) (since \(0 \le x\)) and for \(u \in (1/2, 1)\) with \(2u - 1 \le x \iff u \le (1+x)/2 = F(x)\).
2. *The exponential distribution \(\mathrm{Exp}(1)\):* Let \(F(x) = 1 - e^{-x}\) for \(x \ge 0\) and
   \(F(x) = 0\) for \(x < 0\). For \(0 < u < 1\), since \(F(x) = 0 < u\) on \((-\infty, 0)\) and \(F\)
   is strictly increasing on \([0, \infty)\), we solve
   \[
   1 - e^{-x} \ge u \quad \Longleftrightarrow \quad e^{-x} \le 1 - u \quad \Longleftrightarrow \quad x \ge -\log(1 - u).
   \]
   Thus the quantile function is
   \[
   Q(u) = -\log(1 - u), \qquad 0 < u < 1.
   \]
   This is how computer random number generators simulate an exponential waiting time from a uniform
   random number \(U \in (0,1)\)!
#+end_quote

** Historical remark (Stieltjes, 1894)

In his 1894 memoir /Recherches sur les fractions continues/ (/Annales de la Faculté des Sciences de
Toulouse/ 8 (1894), Section 38, pp. J70–J71), Thomas Joannes Stieltjes interpreted an increasing
function \(F\) on the line as the accumulated mass of a rod, with its jumps representing point masses
concentrated at individual points. Stieltjes defined integrals \(\int_a^b g(x)\,dF(x)\) of *continuous*
functions \(g\) as limits of Riemann-type sums \(\sum_i g(\xi_i)(F(x_i) - F(x_{i-1}))\) weighted by
increments of the accumulated mass, and explicitly discussed how the value chosen for \(F\) at a jump
allocates the point mass between adjacent intervals. Our right-continuous convention \(F(x) = \mu((-\infty, x])\)
makes this endpoint allocation canonical, and combining Stieltjes's interval increments with Carathéodory's
extension and Lebesgue integration allows arbitrary Borel measurable integrands \(g\).

* Worked Exercises

** Exercise 1
Let \(X\) have the CDF
\[
F_X(x) = \begin{cases}
0, & x < 0, \\
\frac{1}{4} + \frac{x}{4}, & 0 \le x < 1, \\
\frac{3}{4}, & 1 \le x < 2, \\
1, & x \ge 2.
\end{cases}
\]
1. Verify that \(F_X\) is a valid CDF.
2. Find all atoms of \(\mu_X\) and their probabilities.
3. Compute \(\mathbb P(0 < X < 1)\), \(\mathbb P(0 \le X \le 1)\), and \(\mathbb P(X \in (1, 2))\).
4. Write the Lebesgue–Stieltjes measure \(\mu_X\) explicitly in terms of Dirac masses and restricted
   Lebesgue measure, and compute \(\mathbb E[X]\).

** Solution
1. \(F_X\) is clearly nondecreasing on each piece, and at the breakpoints:
   - \(F_X(0-) = 0 \le 1/4 = F_X(0) = \lim_{x\downarrow 0}(1/4 + x/4)\),
   - \(F_X(1-) = 1/4 + 1/4 = 1/2 \le 3/4 = F_X(1) = \lim_{x\downarrow 1} 3/4\),
   - \(F_X(2-) = 3/4 \le 1 = F_X(2) = \lim_{x\downarrow 2} 1\).
   Also \(\lim_{x\to-\infty} F_X(x) = 0\) and \(\lim_{x\to+\infty} F_X(x) = 1\). Thus \(F_X\) is a valid CDF.
2. Jumps occur only at \(x = 0\), \(x = 1\), and \(x = 2\):
   - \(\mathbb P(X = 0) = F_X(0) - F_X(0-) = 1/4 - 0 = 1/4\),
   - \(\mathbb P(X = 1) = F_X(1) - F_X(1-) = 3/4 - 1/2 = 1/4\),
   - \(\mathbb P(X = 2) = F_X(2) - F_X(2-) = 1 - 3/4 = 1/4\).
3. Using Proposition 12.1:
   - \(\mathbb P(0 < X < 1) = F_X(1-) - F_X(0) = 1/2 - 1/4 = 1/4\),
   - \(\mathbb P(0 \le X \le 1) = F_X(1) - F_X(0-) = 3/4 - 0 = 3/4\),
   - \(\mathbb P(X \in (1,2)) = F_X(2-) - F_X(1) = 3/4 - 3/4 = 0\) (\(F_X\) is flat on \([1,2)\)!).
4. Comparing interval increments shows that
   \[
   \mu_X = \frac{1}{4}\delta_0 + \frac{1}{4}\lambda|_{(0,1)} + \frac{1}{4}\delta_1 + \frac{1}{4}\delta_2.
   \]
   Therefore
   \[
   \mathbb E[X] = \int_{\mathbb R} x\,dF_X(x) = \frac{1}{4}(0) + \frac{1}{4}\int_0^1 x\,dx + \frac{1}{4}(1) + \frac{1}{4}(2)
   = 0 + \frac{1}{8} + \frac{1}{4} + \frac{1}{2} = \frac{7}{8}.
   \]

** Exercise 2
Find the quantile function \(Q(u)\) for \(u \in (0,1)\) of the CDF \(F_X\) from Exercise 1, and explain
what happens both at the atoms (\(x = 0, 1, 2\)) and along the flat spot \([1, 2)\).

** Solution
We find \(Q(u) = \inf\{x \in \mathbb R : F_X(x) \ge u\}\) for \(u \in (0,1)\):
- For \(0 < u \le 1/4\): \(F_X(x) = 0 < u\) for \(x < 0\) and \(F_X(0) = 1/4 \ge u\), so \(Q(u) = 0\).
- For \(1/4 < u \le 1/2\): \(F_X(x) \ge u\) on \([0,1)\) iff \(1/4 + x/4 \ge u \iff x \ge 4u - 1\), so
  \(Q(u) = 4u - 1\).
- For \(1/2 < u \le 3/4\): on \([0,1)\) we have \(F_X(x) < 1/2 < u\), whereas at \(x = 1\) the jump
  gives \(F_X(1) = 3/4 \ge u\). Thus \(\{x : F_X(x) \ge u\} = [1, \infty)\), and \(Q(u) = 1\).
- For \(3/4 < u < 1\): even on the flat spot \([1, 2)\) we only have \(F_X(x) = 3/4 < u\); the first
  point where \(F_X(x) \ge u\) is \(x = 2\), where \(F_X(2) = 1 \ge u\). Thus \(Q(u) = 2\).

In summary:
\[
Q(u) = \begin{cases}
0, & 0 < u \le 1/4, \\
4u - 1, & 1/4 < u \le 1/2, \\
1, & 1/2 < u \le 3/4, \\
2, & 3/4 < u < 1.
\end{cases}
\]
Notice the duality between \(F_X\) and \(Q\):
- Each *jump* of \(F_X\) (at \(x = 0, 1, 2\), each of size \(1/4\)) becomes a *flat spot* of \(Q\) (of
  length \(1/4\)).
- The *flat spot* of \(F_X\) on \([1, 2)\) (where \(\mu_X((1,2)) = 0\)) becomes a *jump* of \(Q\) at
  \(u = 3/4\) (where \(Q\) jumps from \(1\) to \(2\), skipping over \((1,2)\)).

** Exercise 3
Is the quantile function \(Q : (0,1) \to \mathbb R\) of a general CDF \(F\) left-continuous or
right-continuous? Prove your answer.

** Solution
Every quantile function \(Q : (0,1) \to \mathbb R\) is nondecreasing and *left-continuous*!
- *Nondecreasing:* If \(0 < u_1 \le u_2 < 1\), then \(\{x : F(x) \ge u_2\} \subseteq \{x : F(x) \ge u_1\}\).
  Taking the infimum of a smaller set gives a larger or equal number, so \(Q(u_1) \le Q(u_2)\).
- *Left-continuous:* Fix \(u \in (0,1)\) and let \(u_n \uparrow u\) in \((0,1)\). Since \(Q\) is
  nondecreasing, \(Q(u_n)\) is nondecreasing and bounded above by \(Q(u)\), so the limit
  \(L := \lim_{n\to\infty} Q(u_n) \le Q(u)\) exists. To show \(L \ge Q(u)\), note that for every \(n\),
  \(Q(u_n) \le L\), which by the quantile equivalence (12.2) means \(u_n \le F(L)\) for all \(n\).
  Letting \(n \to \infty\) gives \(u = \lim_{n\to\infty} u_n \le F(L)\), and applying (12.2) again gives
  \(Q(u) \le L\). Thus \(\lim_{n\to\infty} Q(u_n) = Q(u)\).

(Notice in Exercise 2 that at the jump \(u = 3/4\), \(Q(3/4) = 1 = \lim_{u \uparrow 3/4} Q(u)\), while
\(\lim_{u \downarrow 3/4} Q(u) = 2\), confirming left-continuity!)

** Exercise 4
Let \(X \ge 0\) be a nonnegative random variable on \((\Omega, \mathcal F, \mathbb P)\) with CDF \(F_X\).
Using Tonelli's theorem (Chapter 9) and the Lebesgue–Stieltjes measure \(\mu_{F_X}\), prove the tail
integral formula
\[
\mathbb E[X] = \int_0^\infty \mathbb P(X > t)\,dt = \int_0^\infty \big(1 - F_X(t)\big)\,dt.
\]

** Solution
For every \(x \ge 0\), we can write \(x = \int_0^\infty \mathbf 1_{\{t < x\}}\,dt\). Since \(X \ge 0\)
almost surely (\(\mu_{F_X}((-\infty, 0)) = F_X(0-) = 0\)), the image-measure integration formula and
Tonelli's theorem on \([0, \infty) \times [0, \infty)\) with product measure \(\mu_{F_X} \otimes \lambda\)
give
\[
\mathbb E[X] = \int_{[0,\infty)} x\,d\mu_{F_X}(x)
= \int_{[0,\infty)} \Big(\int_0^\infty \mathbf 1_{\{t < x\}}\,dt\Big)\,d\mu_{F_X}(x)
= \int_0^\infty \Big(\int_{[0,\infty)} \mathbf 1_{(t, \infty)}(x)\,d\mu_{F_X}(x)\Big)\,dt.
\]
The inner integral is \(\mu_{F_X}((t, \infty)) = \mathbb P(X > t) = 1 - \mathbb P(X \le t) = 1 - F_X(t)\),
giving the desired identity.

** Exercise 5
Suppose \(X\) is a random variable whose CDF \(F\) is *continuous* on \(\mathbb R\) (i.e. \(\mu_X\) has
no atoms). Show that the random variable \(Y := F(X)\) has the \(\mathrm{Uniform}(0,1)\) distribution!
Where does this fail if \(F\) has a jump?

** Solution
Because \(F\) is continuous on \(\mathbb R\) with \(\lim_{x\to-\infty} F(x) = 0\) and \(\lim_{x\to+\infty} F(x) = 1\), the Intermediate Value Theorem ensures that for every \(u \in (0,1)\), the level set \(\{x \in \mathbb R : F(x) = u\}\) is nonempty and bounded from above.

Fix \(u \in (0,1)\) and define unconditionally
\[
r := \sup\{x \in \mathbb R : F(x) = u\}.
\]
Because \(F\) is continuous, \(F(r) = u\). By monotonicity of \(F\):
- If \(X(\omega) \le r\), then \(F(X(\omega)) \le F(r) = u\).
- If \(X(\omega) > r\), then \(F(X(\omega)) > u\) (since \(F\) cannot equal \(u\) anywhere to the right of its supremum \(r\), and \(F\) is nondecreasing).

Thus \(\{F(X) \le u\} = \{X \le r\}\). Therefore,
\[
\mathbb P(F(X) \le u) = \mathbb P(X \le r) = F(r) = u.
\]
Since this holds for every \(u \in (0,1)\), \(Y = F(X)\) is uniformly distributed on \((0,1)\).
- *Why this fails when \(F\) has a jump:* If \(X \equiv 0\) is a constant random variable, then
  \(F(x) = \mathbf 1_{[0,\infty)}(x)\) and \(F(0) = 1\), so \(Y = F(X) = F(0) = 1\) almost surely, which
  is a point mass at \(1\), not \(\mathrm{Uniform}(0,1)\)!

** Exercise 6
Let \(F(x) = e^x\) for \(x \in \mathbb R\). Does Theorem 12.3 apply to \(F\)? Is \(\mu_F\) a probability
measure? Compute \(\mu_F((a,b])\), \(\mu_F((-\infty, 0])\), and \(\int_0^1 x\,dF(x)\).

** Solution
- *Does Theorem 12.3 apply?* Yes! \(F(x) = e^x\) is continuous (hence right-continuous) and strictly
  increasing (hence nondecreasing) on \(\mathbb R\). Thus there is a unique locally finite Borel measure
  \(\mu_F\) on \(\mathbb R\) with \(\mu_F((a,b]) = e^b - e^a\).
- *Is \(\mu_F\) a probability measure?* No: while \(\lim_{x\to-\infty} e^x = 0\), we have
  \(\lim_{x\to+\infty} e^x = +\infty \ne 1\), so \(\mu_F(\mathbb R) = \infty\).
- *Measures and integral:*
  - \(\mu_F((a,b]) = e^b - e^a\).
  - \(\mu_F((-\infty, 0]) = \lim_{m\to\infty} (e^0 - e^{-m}) = 1 - 0 = 1\).
  - Since \(e^b - e^a = \int_a^b e^x\,dx\), \(\mu_F\) has density \(e^x\) with respect to Lebesgue
    measure on \(\mathbb R\) (by uniqueness on each \((-m, m]\)). Therefore, integrating by parts,
    \[
    \int_0^1 x\,dF(x) = \int_0^1 x e^x\,dx = \big[x e^x - e^x\big]_0^1 = (e - e) - (0 - 1) = 1.
    \]

*Key takeaways*

- A Borel probability measure on \(\mathbb R\) is in one-to-one correspondence with its CDF
  \(F(x) = \mu((-\infty, x])\), characterized by four properties: nondecreasing, right-continuous,
  \(F(-\infty) = 0\), and \(F(+\infty) = 1\) (Proposition 12.1 and Theorem 12.3).
- Right-continuity of \(F\) matches the half-open interval convention \(\mu_F((a,b]) = F(b) - F(a)\), and
  is the exact analytical ingredient needed to shrink \((u,v]\) to a compact \([u+\delta, v]\) in the
  Carathéodory extension proof.
- The Lebesgue–Stieltjes integral \(\int g\,dF = \int g\,d\mu_F\) is simply the Lebesgue integral with
  respect to \(\mu_F\); it automatically accounts for atoms \(\sum_x g(x)\mu_F(\{x\})\) as well as
  continuous slopes (Example 12.4).
- The quantile function \(Q(u) = \inf\{x : F(x) \ge u\}\) satisfies the clean Galois-style equivalence
  \(Q(u) \le x \iff u \le F(x)\) (thanks again to right-continuity of \(F\)!), turning any
  \(\mathrm{Uniform}(0,1)\) variable \(U\) into a random variable \(Q(U)\) with CDF \(F\)
  (Proposition 12.6).
