* Overview

** What problem does this chapter solve?

The Weak Law of Large Numbers ([[file:ch15-weak-law.org][Chapter 15]]) tells us that at any *single* large sample size \(n\),
the sample average \(\overline X_n = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^n X_j\) is very likely to be close to the
true mean \(\mathbb E X_1\). However, as we saw with the typewriter sequence in [[file:ch14-convergence.org][Chapter 14]], convergence
in probability does *not* by itself rule out the nightmare scenario where, as you keep adding new
observations \(X_{n+1}, X_{n+2}, \dots\), every individual simulation trajectory \(\omega\) keeps
experiencing occasional wild swings away from \(\mathbb E X_1\) forever!

The *Strong Law of Large Numbers (SLLN)* rules out that nightmare completely: for independent and
identically distributed (iid) random variables with \(\mathbb E|X_1| < \infty\),
\[
\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^n X_j \longrightarrow \mathbb E X_1 \quad \text{almost surely} .
\]
Once the outcome \(\omega\) is fixed outside a single probability-zero exceptional set, the infinite
sequence of numbers \(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^n X_j(\omega)\) converges to \(\mathbb E X_1\) in the
ordinary sense of first-year calculus.

** Two proofs at two levels of generality

Following the lecture notes, we prove the Strong Law in two stages:
1. *Finite variance (Section 16.1, Theorem 16.1):* Using *only* Chebyshev's inequality, Borel–Cantelli,
   and a clever trick (evaluate along the square indices \(n = k^2\) where \(\sum_k 1/k^2 < \infty\),
   and use monotonicity of nonnegative partial sums to sandwich all intermediate indices
   \(k^2 \le n < (k+1)^2\)), we get a short, completely elementary proof when \(\mathbb E[X_1^2] < \infty\).
2. *Integrable variables (Sections 16.2–16.3, Theorem 16.6):* To remove the finite-variance hypothesis
   and assume only \(\mathbb E|X_1| < \infty\) (Kolmogorov's Strong Law), we introduce three classic
   tools:
   - *Kolmogorov's maximal inequality* (Lemma 16.3), which controls the *entire running maximum*
     \(\max_{1 \le k \le n} |T_k|\) of a random walk for the same price as Chebyshev's bound on the
     final position \(|T_n|\);
   - *Almost-sure convergence of random series* \(\sum_j Z_j\) with summable variances (Lemma 16.4);
   - *Kronecker's lemma* (Lemma 16.5) and index-dependent truncation \(Y_j = X_j \mathbf 1_{\{|X_j| \le j\}}\).

Finally, Section 16.4 harvests two flagship applications: *Monte Carlo integration* (Example 16.7) and
the simultaneous almost-sure convergence of *empirical distribution functions* \(F_n(x) \to F(x)\)
(Example 16.8).

* A square subsequence and monotonicity

** Intuition

Why did our proof of the finite-variance Weak Law (Theorem 15.6) stop short of almost-sure convergence?
Because for \(S_n = \sum_{j=1}^n X_j\), Chebyshev gave
\[
\mathbb P\Big(\Big|\frac{S_n}{n} - m\Big| > \varepsilon\Big) \le \frac{\sigma^2}{n\varepsilon^2} ,
\]
and the harmonic series \(\sum_{n=1}^\infty \frac{1}{n} = \infty\) diverges!

How can we make the series converge without assuming higher moments?
- *Idea 1 (Sample along the squares \(n = k^2\)):* If we only check the sample average at perfect square
  times \(n = 1, 4, 9, 16, \dots, k^2, \dots\), the Chebyshev bound becomes \(\frac{\sigma^2}{k^2 \varepsilon^2}\).
  Since \(\sum_{k=1}^\infty \frac{1}{k^2} < \infty\), Corollary 15.3 immediately tells us that
  \(S_{k^2} / k^2 \to m\) *almost surely* as \(k \to \infty\)!
- *Idea 2 (Sandwich the intermediate indices using monotonicity):* What happens at the non-square times
  \(n\) between \(k^2\) and \((k+1)^2\)? If the variables \(X_j \ge 0\) are *nonnegative*, then the
  partial sums \(S_n = \sum_{j=1}^n X_j\) can only grow as \(n\) increases:
  \[
  S_{k^2} \le S_n \le S_{(k+1)^2} \quad \text{for all } k^2 \le n < (k+1)^2 .
  \]
  Dividing by \(n\) (where \(k^2 \le n < (k+1)^2\)) traps \(S_n / n\) between two square averages:

#+caption: Figure 16.1: Squeezing $S_n / n$ Between Square Subsequence Averages
#+ATTR_LATEX: :width 0.85\textwidth :center t
#+ATTR_HTML: :width 650px :align center
[[file:figures/fig16_square_sandwich.png]]

Because the gap between consecutive squares \((k+1)^2 - k^2 = 2k + 1\) is tiny compared to \(k^2\), the
ratio \(\frac{(k+1)^2}{k^2} = (1 + \frac{1}{k})^2 \to 1\), so both ends of the sandwich converge to \(m\)!
Finally, for general signed variables \(X_j\), we simply split \(X_j = X_j^+ - X_j^-\) into nonnegative
parts.

** The finite-variance Strong Law

#+begin_quote
*Theorem 16.1 (Strong law, finite variance).* Let \(X_j\) be iid and square-integrable. Then
\[
\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^n X_j \longrightarrow \mathbb E X_1 \quad \text{almost surely} .
\]
#+end_quote

** Proof (unpacked step by step)

*** Step 1. Converge along the squares

Write \(S_n := \sum_{j=1}^n X_j\), \(m := \mathbb E X_1\), and \(\sigma^2 := \operatorname{var}(X_1) < \infty\).
For each positive integer \(k \ge 1\), the square-index average \(S_{k^2} / k^2\) has mean \(m\) and
variance \(\sigma^2 / k^2\). For any \(\varepsilon > 0\), Chebyshev's inequality (Proposition 15.4) gives
\[
\mathbb P\Big(\Big|\frac{S_{k^2}}{k^2} - m\Big| > \varepsilon\Big) \le \frac{\sigma^2}{k^2 \varepsilon^2} .
\]
Since \(\sum_{k=1}^\infty \frac{\sigma^2}{k^2 \varepsilon^2} = \frac{\sigma^2}{\varepsilon^2}\sum_{k=1}^\infty \frac{1}{k^2} < \infty\),
Corollary 15.3 gives a probability-one event \(\Omega_{\text{sq}}\) on which
\[
\frac{S_{k^2}}{k^2} \xrightarrow[k\to\infty]{} m \quad \text{almost surely} .
\]

*** Step 2. Fill the gaps for nonnegative variables

Suppose first that \(X_j \ge 0\) for all \(j\).
- Because we add nonnegative terms \(X_{n+1} \ge 0\), the partial sums \(S_n(\omega)\) are nondecreasing
  in \(n\) at every outcome \(\omega\).
- Given any integer \(n \ge 1\), let \(k = \lfloor \sqrt{n} \rfloor \ge 1\) be the unique integer such that
  \[
  k^2 \le n < (k+1)^2 .
  \]
- Because \(S_{k^2} \le S_n \le S_{(k+1)^2}\) are nonnegative and \(\frac{1}{(k+1)^2} < \frac{1}{n} \le \frac{1}{k^2}\),
  we have the pointwise sandwich
  \[
  \frac{k^2}{(k+1)^2} \cdot \frac{S_{k^2}}{k^2} = \frac{S_{k^2}}{(k+1)^2}
    \le \frac{S_n}{n}
    \le \frac{S_{(k+1)^2}}{k^2} = \frac{(k+1)^2}{k^2} \cdot \frac{S_{(k+1)^2}}{(k+1)^2} .
  \]
- Fix any outcome \(\omega \in \Omega_{\text{sq}}\) from Step 1. As \(n \to \infty\), the corresponding
  \(k = \lfloor \sqrt{n} \rfloor \to \infty\). On \(\Omega_{\text{sq}}\), both \(\frac{S_{k^2}(\omega)}{k^2} \to m\)
  and \(\frac{S_{(k+1)^2}(\omega)}{(k+1)^2} \to m\), while \(\frac{(k+1)^2}{k^2} = (1 + \frac{1}{k})^2 \to 1\).
- Thus both the left bound and the right bound converge to \(1 \cdot m = m\). By the ordinary squeeze
  theorem for real sequences, \(\frac{S_n(\omega)}{n} \to m\) as \(n \to \infty\).

*** Step 3. Separate positive and negative parts

Now drop the assumption \(X_j \ge 0\). For general square-integrable iid variables \(X_j\), decompose
each \(X_j = X_j^+ - X_j^-\) into its positive part \(X_j^+ = \max(X_j, 0) \ge 0\) and negative part
\(X_j^- = \max(-X_j, 0) \ge 0\).
- Because \(x \mapsto \max(x, 0)\) and \(x \mapsto \max(-x, 0)\) are Borel measurable functions on
  \(\mathbb R\), Lemma 13.3 guarantees that \((X_j^+)_{j\ge 1}\) is an iid sequence of nonnegative
  variables, and \((X_j^-)_{j\ge 1}\) is also an iid sequence of nonnegative variables.
- Since \(0 \le X_j^\pm \le |X_j|\), both sequences are square-integrable (\(\mathbb E[(X_1^\pm)^2] \le \mathbb E[X_1^2] < \infty\)).
- Applying Steps 1–2 to \((X_j^+)_{j\ge 1}\) gives a probability-one set \(\Omega_+\) on which
  \(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^n X_j^+ \to \mathbb E[X_1^+]\).
- Applying Steps 1–2 to \((X_j^-)_{j\ge 1}\) gives a probability-one set \(\Omega_-\) on which
  \(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^n X_j^- \to \mathbb E[X_1^-]\).
- On the intersection \(\Omega_+ \cap \Omega_-\) (which still has probability \(1\)), subtracting the two
  convergent numerical sequences gives
  \[
  \frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^n X_j = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^n X_j^+ - \frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^n X_j^-
    \xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{} \mathbb E[X_1^+] - \mathbb E[X_1^-] = \mathbb E X_1 . \quad \blacksquare
  \]

*Why couldn't we skip Step 3 and interpolate signed sums directly?* Because when \(X_j\) takes both
positive and negative values, \(S_n\) can go both up and down between \(k^2\) and \((k+1)^2\), so
\(S_{k^2} \le S_n \le S_{(k+1)^2}\) is false! Splitting into \(X_j^+\) and \(X_j^-\) is what supplies
the monotonicity used in Step 2.

** Application: Empirical frequencies

#+begin_quote
*Example 16.2 (Empirical frequencies).* For iid observations \(X_j\) with law \(\mu\) and a fixed Borel
set \(B\), the indicators \(\mathbf 1_{\{X_j \in B\}}\) are iid and bounded. The strong law gives
\[
\frac{1}{n}\,\#\{1 \le j \le n : X_j \in B\} \longrightarrow \mu(B) \quad \text{almost surely} ,
\]
where \(\#\) denotes the number of elements of a finite set. For a countable specified family of sets
\(B\), intersect the corresponding probability-one events to obtain simultaneous convergence. One must
not silently make this intersection over an arbitrary uncountable family.
#+end_quote

Let's unpack Example 16.2:
- For a fixed Borel set \(B \subseteq \mathbb R\), let \(I_j := \mathbf 1_{\{X_j \in B\}} = \mathbf 1_B(X_j)\).
  Since \(I_j \in \{0,1\}\), the variables \(I_j\) are iid, bounded (hence square-integrable!), with
  mean \(\mathbb E I_1 = \mathbb P(X_1 \in B) = \mu(B)\).
- The sum \(\sum_{j=1}^n I_j = \#\{1 \le j \le n : X_j \in B\}\) is the number of observations among the
  first \(n\) that land in \(B\). Theorem 16.1 says that on a probability-one event \(\Omega_B\), the
  empirical relative frequency converges to the theoretical probability \(\mu(B)\).
- *Countable vs. uncountable families of sets \(B\):*
  - If we specify a *countable* collection of sets \(\mathcal C = \{B_1, B_2, \dots\}\) in advance, then
    \(\bigcap_{m=1}^\infty \Omega_{B_m}\) is a countable intersection of probability-one events, so it
    still has probability \(1\). On this single event, empirical frequencies converge to \(\mu(B_m)\) for
    all \(m\) simultaneously.
  - Why must we *never* take an intersection \(\bigcap_{B \in \mathcal B(\mathbb R)} \Omega_B\) over all
    Borel sets (or even over all singletons \(B = \{x\}\), \(x \in \mathbb R\))? Suppose \(\mu\) is the
    uniform distribution on \([0,1]\), so \(\mu(\{x\}) = 0\) for every \(x \in [0,1]\). Yet for any fixed
    outcome \(\omega\), the sample sequence \(X_1(\omega), X_2(\omega), \dots\) is a countable set of points
    \(B(\omega) := \{X_1(\omega), X_2(\omega), \dots\}\). For this set \(B(\omega)\), every \(X_j(\omega) \in B(\omega)\),
    so the empirical frequency is \(1\) for all \(n\), while \(\mu(B(\omega)) = 0\)! Each individual
    \(B\) has a null exceptional set \(\Omega_B^c\), but the uncountable union \(\bigcup_{B} \Omega_B^c\)
    covers the entire sample space \(\Omega\).

** Additional worked examples

*Example A (Borel's normal number theorem).* Let \(\omega \in [0,1)\) be chosen uniformly at random, and
let \(\omega = 0.d_1 d_2 d_3 \dots\) be its base-\(10\) decimal expansion. In [[file:ch10-infinite-products.org][Chapter 10]], we saw that the
digits \(d_1, d_2, \dots\) are iid uniform on \(\{0, 1, \dots, 9\}\). Applying Example 16.2 to each of
the \(10\) sets \(B_k = \{k\}\) (\(k = 0, \dots, 9\)) and intersecting the \(10\) probability-one events
shows that for Lebesgue-almost every real number \(\omega \in [0,1)\), every digit \(0, 1, \dots, 9\)
appears with asymptotic frequency exactly \(1/10\)!

* A maximal inequality

** Intuition

In Section 16.3 we will prove the Strong Law assuming only \(\mathbb E|X_1| < \infty\). Why didn't our
square-subsequence trick from Section 16.1 immediately solve the general case? Because when
\(\mathbb E[X_1^2] = \infty\), we have to truncate \(X_j\) at a threshold that grows with \(j\), which
destroys identical distribution and forces us to control partial sums of independent variables with
*different* variances.

To control all partial sums \(T_k = \sum_{j=1}^k Z_j\) simultaneously, we need a much stronger version
of Chebyshev's inequality. Look at the difference between the two questions for a centered random walk
\(T_k = Z_1 + \dots + Z_k\) (\(\mathbb E Z_j = 0\)):
- *Chebyshev's question:* What is the probability that at the *final step \(n\)*, \(|T_n| \ge a\)?
  Answer: \(\mathbb P(|T_n| \ge a) \le \frac{1}{a^2} \mathbb E[T_n^2] = \frac{1}{a^2}\sum_{j=1}^n \mathbb E[Z_j^2]\).
- *Kolmogorov's question:* What is the probability that the walk *ever* reaches \(\ge a\) at *any*
  intermediate step \(k \in \{1, 2, \dots, n\}\), even if it later wanders back below \(a\) by time \(n\)?

#+caption: Figure 16.2: Kolmogorov's Maximal Inequality Stopping Time Event
#+ATTR_LATEX: :width 0.75\textwidth :center t
#+ATTR_HTML: :width 600px :align center
[[file:figures/fig16_maximal_inequality.png]]

Astonishingly, *Kolmogorov's maximal inequality* says the upper bound for \(\mathbb P(\max_{1 \le k \le n} |T_k| \ge a)\)
is the *exact same formula* \(\frac{1}{a^2}\sum_{j=1}^n \mathbb E[Z_j^2]\) as Chebyshev's bound for the
single term \(|T_n|\)!

Why does this miracle happen? Suppose the walk first crosses level \(a\) at step \(k\) (the event \(A_k\)).
After step \(k\), the future increment \(T_n - T_k = Z_{k+1} + \dots + Z_n\) is *independent* of the past
\(Z_1, \dots, Z_k\) and has mean \(0\). When you square \(T_n = T_k + (T_n - T_k)\) on the event \(A_k\),
the cross-term has expectation \(0\), and the future squared increment \((T_n - T_k)^2 \ge 0\) only
*adds* to the second moment: \(\mathbb E[T_n^2 \mathbf 1_{A_k}] \ge \mathbb E[T_k^2 \mathbf 1_{A_k}] \ge a^2 \mathbb P(A_k)\)!

** Kolmogorov's maximal inequality

#+begin_quote
*Lemma 16.3 (Kolmogorov's maximal inequality).* Let \(Z_1, \dots, Z_n\) be independent square-integrable
variables with \(\mathbb E Z_j = 0\). Put \(T_k = \sum_{j=1}^k Z_j\). For \(a > 0\),
\[
\mathbb P\Big(\max_{1 \le k \le n} |T_k| \ge a\Big) \le \frac{1}{a^2} \sum_{j=1}^n \mathbb E[Z_j^2] .
\]
#+end_quote

** Proof (unpacked step by step)

*Step 1. Decompose the maximum event by the time of the FIRST crossing.*
The event \(M := \{\max_{1 \le k \le n} |T_k| \ge a\}\) means that \(|T_k| \ge a\) for at least one
\(k \in \{1, \dots, n\}\). Partition \(M\) according to the *first* index \(k\) where \(|T_k| \ge a\):
\[
A_k := \big\{|T_1| < a,\, \dots,\, |T_{k-1}| < a,\, |T_k| \ge a\big\}, \qquad k = 1, \dots, n .
\]
(For \(k = 1\), \(A_1 = \{|T_1| \ge a\}\).)
- The events \(A_1, \dots, A_n\) are *pairwise disjoint* (a trajectory can only have one "first" crossing
  time!), and their union is \(\bigsqcup_{k=1}^n A_k = \{\max_{1 \le k \le n} |T_k| \ge a\}\).
- Crucially, the event \(A_k\) depends *only* on the first \(k\) variables \((Z_1, \dots, Z_k)\).

*Step 2. Use independence of the future increment \(T_n - T_k\) from the past up to \(k\).*
Fix \(k \in \{1, \dots, n\}\). The future increment after time \(k\) is
\[
T_n - T_k = \sum_{j=k+1}^n Z_j
\]
(for \(k = n\), \(T_n - T_n = 0\)).
- Because \(T_k \mathbf 1_{A_k}\) is a measurable function of \((Z_1, \dots, Z_k)\) and \(T_n - T_k\) is a
  measurable function of \((Z_{k+1}, \dots, Z_n)\), Lemma 13.3 implies that \(T_k \mathbf 1_{A_k}\) and
  \(T_n - T_k\) are *independent*.
- Both are integrable (since \(Z_j \in L^2 \subseteq L^1\)), and \(\mathbb E[T_n - T_k] = \sum_{j=k+1}^n \mathbb E Z_j = 0\).
  By Proposition 13.4 (expectation of a product of independent integrable variables),
  \[
  \mathbb E\big[T_k \mathbf 1_{A_k} (T_n - T_k)\big] = \mathbb E[T_k \mathbf 1_{A_k}]\,\mathbb E[T_n - T_k] = 0 .
  \]
- Likewise, \(\mathbf 1_{A_k}\) and \((T_n - T_k)^2\) are independent by Lemma 13.3.

*Step 3. Expand \(T_n^2\) on \(A_k\) and sum over \(k\).*
Write \(T_n = T_k + (T_n - T_k)\), square both sides, and multiply by \(\mathbf 1_{A_k}\) (noting that
\(\mathbf 1_{A_k}^2 = \mathbf 1_{A_k}\)):
\[
T_n^2 \mathbf 1_{A_k} = T_k^2 \mathbf 1_{A_k} + 2 T_k \mathbf 1_{A_k} (T_n - T_k) + \mathbf 1_{A_k} (T_n - T_k)^2 .
\]
Take expectations using Step 2: the middle term vanishes, and the third term is \(\mathbb P(A_k)\mathbb E[(T_n - T_k)^2] \ge 0\).
On the event \(A_k\), we have \(|T_k| \ge a\), so \(T_k^2 \mathbf 1_{A_k} \ge a^2 \mathbf 1_{A_k}\). Therefore:
\[
\mathbb E[T_n^2 \mathbf 1_{A_k}]
  = \mathbb E[T_k^2 \mathbf 1_{A_k}] + 0 + \mathbb P(A_k)\mathbb E[(T_n - T_k)^2]
  \ge \mathbb E[T_k^2 \mathbf 1_{A_k}]
  \ge a^2 \mathbb P(A_k) .
\]
Now sum this inequality over \(k = 1, \dots, n\). Because \(A_1, \dots, A_n\) are disjoint,
\(\sum_{k=1}^n \mathbf 1_{A_k} = \mathbf 1_{\bigcup_{k=1}^n A_k} \le 1\), and because \(T_n^2 \ge 0\),
\[
a^2 \mathbb P\Big(\max_{1 \le k \le n} |T_k| \ge a\Big)
  = a^2 \sum_{k=1}^n \mathbb P(A_k)
  \le \sum_{k=1}^n \mathbb E[T_n^2 \mathbf 1_{A_k}]
  = \mathbb E\Big[T_n^2 \mathbf 1_{\bigcup_{k=1}^n A_k}\Big]
  \le \mathbb E[T_n^2] .
\]
Finally, since \(Z_1, \dots, Z_n\) are independent with mean \(0\), \(\mathbb E[T_n^2] = \operatorname{var}(T_n) = \sum_{j=1}^n \mathbb E[Z_j^2]\).
Dividing by \(a^2\) completes the proof. \(\blacksquare\)

** Convergence of random series with summable variances

Using Kolmogorov's maximal inequality, we can now prove that if independent mean-zero variables \(Z_j\)
have *summable variances* \(\sum_{j=1}^\infty \mathbb E[Z_j^2] < \infty\), then the random infinite series
\(\sum_{j=1}^\infty Z_j(\omega)\) converges almost surely!

#+begin_quote
*Lemma 16.4 (Convergence of a series with summable variances).* If \(Z_j\) are independent centered
square-integrable variables and \(\sum_j \mathbb E[Z_j^2] < \infty\), then \(\sum_j Z_j\) converges almost
surely to a finite real number.
#+end_quote

** Proof (unpacked step by step)

Recall from real analysis ([[file:ch00-toolkit.org][Chapter 0]]) that a series \(\sum_{j=1}^\infty Z_j(\omega)\) of real numbers
converges to a finite limit if and only if its sequence of partial sums is *Cauchy*, meaning that the tail
partial sums \(\big|\sum_{j=\ell+1}^n Z_j(\omega)\big|\) become arbitrarily small for all \(n > \ell\)
sufficiently large.

*Step 1. Choose tail cutoffs \(m_k\) where the remaining variance is tiny.*
Because the series \(\sum_{j=1}^\infty \mathbb E[Z_j^2] < \infty\) converges, its tail
\(\sum_{j \ge m} \mathbb E[Z_j^2] \to 0\) as \(m \to \infty\). Thus for each integer \(k \ge 1\), we can
choose increasing integers \(m_1 < m_2 < m_3 < \dots\) such that
\[
\sum_{j \ge m_k} \mathbb E[Z_j^2] \le 2^{-3k} .
\]

*Step 2. Apply Kolmogorov's maximal inequality to the tail block \(Z_{m_k}, \dots, Z_N\).*
Fix \(k \ge 1\) and any finite upper index \(N \ge m_k\). Apply Lemma 16.3 to the independent mean-zero
variables \(Z_{m_k}, Z_{m_k+1}, \dots, Z_N\) with threshold \(a = 2^{-k}\):
\[
\mathbb P\Big(\max_{m_k \le n \le N} \Big|\sum_{j=m_k}^n Z_j\Big| > 2^{-k}\Big)
  \le \frac{1}{(2^{-k})^2} \sum_{j=m_k}^N \mathbb E[Z_j^2]
  \le 2^{2k} \cdot 2^{-3k} = 2^{-k} .
\]
Now let \(N \to \infty\). The events \(B_N := \big\{\max_{m_k \le n \le N} \big|\sum_{j=m_k}^n Z_j\big| > 2^{-k}\big\}\)
increase with \(N\), and their union over all \(N \ge m_k\) is the event that *at least one* partial sum
starting at \(m_k\) exceeds \(2^{-k}\):
\[
\bigcup_{N \ge m_k} B_N = \Big\{\sup_{n \ge m_k} \Big|\sum_{j=m_k}^n Z_j\Big| > 2^{-k}\Big\} .
\]
By *continuity of measure from below* (Proposition 2.3),
\[
\mathbb P\Big(\sup_{n \ge m_k} \Big|\sum_{j=m_k}^n Z_j\Big| > 2^{-k}\Big)
  = \lim_{N\to\infty} \mathbb P(B_N) \le 2^{-k} .
\]

*Step 3. Apply Borel–Cantelli and check the Cauchy criterion.*
Because \(\sum_{k=1}^\infty 2^{-k} = 1 < \infty\), the First Borel–Cantelli Lemma (Theorem 15.1) implies
that outside a single null set \(E\) (\(\mathbb P(E) = 0\)), the event
\(\big\{\sup_{n \ge m_k} \big|\sum_{j=m_k}^n Z_j\big| > 2^{-k}\big\}\) occurs for only *finitely many* \(k\).
- Fix any outcome \(\omega \in \Omega \setminus E\). There is an integer \(K(\omega)\) such that for all
  \(k \ge K(\omega)\) and all \(n \ge m_k\),
  \[
  \Big|\sum_{j=m_k}^n Z_j(\omega)\Big| \le 2^{-k} .
  \]
- Then for any two indices \(n > \ell \ge m_k\), we can write \(\sum_{j=\ell+1}^n Z_j = \sum_{j=m_k}^n Z_j - \sum_{j=m_k}^\ell Z_j\),
  so the triangle inequality gives
  \[
  \Big|\sum_{j=\ell+1}^n Z_j(\omega)\Big|
    \le \Big|\sum_{j=m_k}^n Z_j(\omega)\Big| + \Big|\sum_{j=m_k}^\ell Z_j(\omega)\Big|
    \le 2^{-k} + 2^{-k} = 2^{1-k} .
  \]
- Given any tolerance \(\varepsilon > 0\), choose \(k \ge K(\omega)\) with \(2^{1-k} < \varepsilon\). Then
  for all \(n > \ell \ge m_k\), \(\big|\sum_{j=\ell+1}^n Z_j(\omega)\big| < \varepsilon\).
  Thus the partial sums at \(\omega\) form a Cauchy sequence in \(\mathbb R\), which converges to a finite
  real number by completeness of \(\mathbb R\). \(\blacksquare\)

** Kronecker's lemma: turning convergent series into vanishing averages

Lemma 16.4 gives convergence of the weighted series \(\sum_{j=1}^\infty \frac{Y_j - \mathbb E Y_j}{j}\).
How do we pass from convergence of \(\sum_{j=1}^\infty \frac{a_j}{j}\) to the conclusion that the
unweighted average \(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^n a_j \to 0\)? That is a purely deterministic fact of real
analysis called *Kronecker's lemma*.

#+begin_quote
*Lemma 16.5 (Kronecker's lemma for arithmetic averages).* If a real sequence \((a_j)\) satisfies
convergence of \(\sum_{j \ge 1} a_j / j\), then \(n^{-1} \sum_{j=1}^n a_j \to 0\).
#+end_quote

** Proof (unpacked step by step)

*Step 1. Summation by parts (Abel's transformation).*
Let \(b_0 := 0\) and let \(b_n := \sum_{j=1}^n \frac{a_j}{j}\) be the partial sums of the convergent
series, so \(b_n \to b \in \mathbb R\) as \(n \to \infty\).
Notice that \(\frac{a_j}{j} = b_j - b_{j-1}\), so we can recover \(a_j\) from the partial sums \(b_j\):
\[
a_j = j(b_j - b_{j-1}) .
\]
Sum this identity from \(j = 1\) to \(n\) and regroup the coefficients of each \(b_j\) (write out \(n = 3\)
to see the telescoping pattern: \(1(b_1 - b_0) + 2(b_2 - b_1) + 3(b_3 - b_2) = 3b_3 - b_1 - b_2\)):
\[
\sum_{j=1}^n a_j = \sum_{j=1}^n j b_j - \sum_{j=1}^n j b_{j-1}
  = n b_n + \sum_{j=1}^{n-1} j b_j - \sum_{j=1}^{n-1} (j+1) b_j
  = n b_n - \sum_{j=1}^{n-1} b_j .
\]
Dividing both sides by \(n\) gives the exact identity
\[
\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^n a_j = b_n - \frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n-1} b_j .
\]

*Step 2. Show that the arithmetic (Cesàro) averages \(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n-1} b_j\) also converge to \(b\).*
Fix any \(\varepsilon > 0\). Since \(b_j \to b\), there is an index \(J\) such that \(|b_j - b| < \varepsilon\)
for all \(j > J\). For \(n > J + 1\), split the sum \(\sum_{j=1}^{n-1} (b_j - b)\) at \(J\):
\[
\Big|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n-1} b_j - b\Big|
  \le \frac{|b|}{n} + \frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^J |b_j - b| + \frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=J+1}^{n-1} |b_j - b|
  \le \frac{|b| + \sum_{j=1}^J |b_j - b|}{n} + \frac{n - 1 - J}{n}\,\varepsilon .
\]
Keeping \(J\) fixed and letting \(n \to \infty\), the first fraction has a fixed numerator divided by
\(n \to \infty\), so it tends to \(0\), leaving \(\limsup_{n\to\infty} \big|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n-1} b_j - b\big| \le \varepsilon\).
Since \(\varepsilon > 0\) was arbitrary, \(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n-1} b_j \to b\).

*Step 3. Subtract the two equal limits.*
In our summation-by-parts identity \(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^n a_j = b_n - \frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n-1} b_j\),
both \(b_n \to b\) and \(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n-1} b_j \to b\). Therefore their difference tends to
\(b - b = 0\). \(\blacksquare\)

** Additional worked examples

*Example B (Random signs and the random harmonic series).* Let \(\varepsilon_1, \varepsilon_2, \dots\) be
iid Rademacher random variables (\(\mathbb P(\varepsilon_j = +1) = \mathbb P(\varepsilon_j = -1) = 1/2\)).
Recall from calculus that the deterministic harmonic series \(\sum_{j=1}^\infty \frac{1}{j} = \infty\)
diverges, and more generally \(\sum_{j=1}^\infty \frac{1}{j^p}\) converges deterministically only for
\(p > 1\). What if we attach random signs \(\varepsilon_j\)?
- Let \(Z_j = \frac{\varepsilon_j}{j^p}\). Then \(Z_j\) are independent with \(\mathbb E Z_j = 0\) and
  \(\mathbb E[Z_j^2] = \frac{1}{j^{2p}}\).
- Whenever \(p > 1/2\), we have \(2p > 1\), so \(\sum_{j=1}^\infty \mathbb E[Z_j^2] = \sum_{j=1}^\infty \frac{1}{j^{2p}} < \infty\)!
- By Lemma 16.4, the *random harmonic series* \(\sum_{j=1}^\infty \frac{\varepsilon_j}{j}\) (\(p = 1\)),
  and even \(\sum_{j=1}^\infty \frac{\varepsilon_j}{\sqrt{j}\,\log(j+1)}\), converges almost surely to a
  finite real number because of random cancellations!

* Removing the finite-variance assumption

** Intuition

Now we assemble Lemmas 16.3–16.5 to prove Kolmogorov's Strong Law of Large Numbers assuming only
\(\mathbb E|X_1| < \infty\).

Look at how we truncate \(X_j\): instead of a fixed threshold \(K\) (as in Theorem 15.7), we truncate the
\(j\)-th variable at threshold \(j\):
\[
Y_j := X_j \mathbf 1_{\{|X_j| \le j\}} .
\]
Why is truncating at \(|X_j| \le j\) the magic choice? Because of two identities:
1. *Tail probabilities are summable:* \(\sum_{j=1}^\infty \mathbb P(X_j \ne Y_j) = \sum_{j=1}^\infty \mathbb P(|X_1| > j) \le \mathbb E|X_1| < \infty\).
   By Borel–Cantelli, \(X_j(\omega) = Y_j(\omega)\) for all sufficiently large \(j\) almost surely!
2. *Weighted variances are summable:* Even though \(\operatorname{var}(Y_j)\) can grow with \(j\), dividing
   by \(j^2\) gives \(\sum_{j=1}^\infty \frac{\operatorname{var}(Y_j)}{j^2} \le 2\mathbb E|X_1| < \infty\).
   Then Lemma 16.4 says \(\sum_{j=1}^\infty \frac{Y_j - \mathbb E Y_j}{j}\) converges a.s., and Kronecker's
   Lemma 16.5 immediately turns this into \(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^n (Y_j - \mathbb E Y_j) \to 0\) a.s.!

** Kolmogorov's Strong Law

#+begin_quote
*Theorem 16.6 (Strong law, integrable iid variables).* If \(X_j\) are iid and \(\mathbb E|X_1| < \infty\),
then
\[
\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^n X_j \longrightarrow \mathbb E X_1 \quad \text{almost surely} .
\]
#+end_quote

** Proof (unpacked step by step)

*** Step 1. Truncate at the index \(j\) and show \(X_j = Y_j\) eventually a.s.

Define \(Y_j := X_j \mathbf 1_{\{|X_j| \le j\}}\) for each \(j \ge 1\).
- By Lemma 13.3, the variables \(Y_1, Y_2, \dots\) are still *independent* (though their laws now depend on
  \(j\) because the cutoff \(j\) grows).
- What is the sum of the probabilities that \(X_j\) differs from \(Y_j\)? Since \(X_j\) has the same law as
  \(X_1\), \(\mathbb P(X_j \ne Y_j) = \mathbb P(|X_j| > j) = \mathbb P(|X_1| > j)\). By Tonelli's theorem
  for nonnegative series (Proposition 6.6), we can swap \(\sum_{j=1}^\infty\) and \(\mathbb E\):
  \[
  \sum_{j=1}^\infty \mathbb P(X_j \ne Y_j)
    = \sum_{j=1}^\infty \mathbb P(|X_1| > j)
    = \mathbb E\Big[\sum_{j=1}^\infty \mathbf 1_{\{|X_1| > j\}}\Big]
    \le \mathbb E|X_1| < \infty .
  \]
  Why does \(\sum_{j=1}^\infty \mathbf 1_{\{|X_1| > j\}} \le |X_1|\) hold pointwise at every outcome?
  Because for any real number \(x = |X_1(\omega)| \ge 0\), the indicator \(\mathbf 1_{\{x > j\}}\) is \(1\)
  for \(j = 1, 2, \dots, \lfloor x \rfloor\) (or \(\lceil x \rceil - 1\)) and \(0\) for larger \(j\); it
  simply counts how many positive integers \(j\) are strictly less than \(x\), which is at most \(x\)!
- By the First Borel–Cantelli Lemma (Theorem 15.1), \(\mathbb P(X_j \ne Y_j \text{ i.o.}) = 0\). That means
  on a probability-one event \(\Omega_1\), there is a random index \(J(\omega)\) such that
  \(X_j(\omega) = Y_j(\omega)\) for all \(j \ge J(\omega)\).
- Consequently, on \(\Omega_1\), the difference \(\sum_{j=1}^n (X_j(\omega) - Y_j(\omega)) = \sum_{j=1}^{J(\omega)} (X_j(\omega) - Y_j(\omega))\)
  stops changing once \(n \ge J(\omega)\)! Dividing this fixed finite sum by \(n \to \infty\) gives
  \[
  \frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^n (X_j - Y_j) \xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{} 0 \quad \text{almost surely} .
  \]

*** Step 2. Sum the truncated variances \(\sum_j \operatorname{var}(Y_j) / j^2\)

Since \(\operatorname{var}(Y_j) = \mathbb E[Y_j^2] - (\mathbb E Y_j)^2 \le \mathbb E[Y_j^2] = \mathbb E[|X_1|^2 \mathbf 1_{\{|X_1| \le j\}}]\)
(using identical distribution of \(X_j\) and \(X_1\)), Tonelli's theorem gives
\[
\sum_{j=1}^\infty \frac{\operatorname{var}(Y_j)}{j^2}
  \le \sum_{j=1}^\infty \mathbb E\Big[\frac{|X_1|^2}{j^2}\,\mathbf 1_{\{|X_1| \le j\}}\Big]
  = \mathbb E\Big[|X_1|^2 \sum_{j=1}^\infty \frac{1}{j^2}\,\mathbf 1_{\{|X_1| \le j\}}\Big] .
\]
Let us prove the pointwise scalar inequality
\[
x^2 \sum_{j=1}^\infty \frac{1}{j^2}\,\mathbf 1_{\{x \le j\}} \le 2x \quad \text{for all } x \ge 0 .
\]
We check two cases:
- *Case 1: \(0 \le x \le 1\).* Here \(x^2 \le x\), and \(\sum_{j=1}^\infty \frac{1}{j^2} = 1 + \sum_{j=2}^\infty \frac{1}{j^2} \le 1 + \int_1^\infty \frac{dt}{t^2} = 2\).
  Multiplying gives \(x^2 \sum_{j=1}^\infty j^{-2} \mathbf 1_{\{x \le j\}} \le 2x^2 \le 2x\).
- *Case 2: \(x > 1\).* Let \(r := \lceil x \rceil \ge 2\) be the smallest integer \(\ge x\). Then
  \(\mathbf 1_{\{x \le j\}}\) is \(0\) for \(j < r\) and \(1\) for \(j \ge r\). Comparing the tail sum
  \(\sum_{j=r}^\infty \frac{1}{j^2}\) with the integral of \(t^{-2}\) (after separating the first term
  \(j = r\)) gives
  \[
  \sum_{j=r}^\infty \frac{1}{j^2}
    = \frac{1}{r^2} + \sum_{j=r+1}^\infty \frac{1}{j^2}
    \le \frac{1}{r^2} + \int_r^\infty \frac{dt}{t^2}
    = \frac{1}{r^2} + \frac{1}{r}
    \le \frac{2}{r} \le \frac{2}{x} ,
  \]
  where the last step used \(r = \lceil x \rceil \ge x\). Multiplying both sides by \(x^2\) gives
  \(x^2 \sum_{j=r}^\infty j^{-2} \le x^2 \cdot \frac{2}{x} = 2x\).
Applying this scalar inequality to \(x = |X_1(\omega)|\) and taking expectations yields
\[
\sum_{j=1}^\infty \frac{\operatorname{var}(Y_j)}{j^2} \le 2\mathbb E|X_1| < \infty .
\]

*** Step 3. Center, sum the weighted series, and apply Kronecker's lemma

Define the centered weighted variables
\[
Z_j := \frac{Y_j - \mathbb E Y_j}{j}, \qquad j = 1, 2, \dots
\]
The variables \(Z_j\) are independent, have mean \(\mathbb E Z_j = 0\), and satisfy
\[
\sum_{j=1}^\infty \mathbb E[Z_j^2] = \sum_{j=1}^\infty \frac{\operatorname{var}(Y_j)}{j^2} \le 2\mathbb E|X_1| < \infty
\]
by Step 2. Therefore, Lemma 16.4 applies: on a probability-one event \(\Omega_2\), the numerical series
\(\sum_{j=1}^\infty \frac{Y_j(\omega) - \mathbb E Y_j}{j}\) converges!
For each fixed outcome \(\omega \in \Omega_2\), apply Kronecker's Lemma (Lemma 16.5) to the real sequence
\(a_j := Y_j(\omega) - \mathbb E Y_j\): we obtain
\[
\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^n (Y_j - \mathbb E Y_j) \xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{} 0 \quad \text{almost surely} .
\]

*** Step 4. Restore the mean \(\mathbb E X_1\) and the original variables \(X_j\)

- Because \(X_j\) has the same law as \(X_1\), we have \(\mathbb E Y_j = \mathbb E[X_1 \mathbf 1_{\{|X_1| \le j\}}]\).
  As \(j \to \infty\), \(X_1 \mathbf 1_{\{|X_1| \le j\}} \to X_1\) pointwise and is dominated by \(|X_1| \in L^1(\mathbb P)\).
  By the Dominated Convergence Theorem, \(\mathbb E Y_j \to \mathbb E X_1\) as \(j \to \infty\).
- As shown in Step 2 of the proof of Lemma 16.5, if a deterministic sequence \(\mathbb E Y_j\) converges
  to \(\mathbb E X_1\), then its arithmetic averages also converge to the same limit:
  \[
  \frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^n \mathbb E Y_j \xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{} \mathbb E X_1 .
  \]
- Adding this deterministic limit to Step 3 gives \(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^n Y_j \to \mathbb E X_1\) almost
  surely. Finally, adding the almost-sure zero limit \(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^n (X_j - Y_j) \to 0\) from
  Step 1 gives
  \[
  \frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^n X_j \xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{} \mathbb E X_1 \quad \text{almost surely} . \quad \blacksquare
  \]

*** Comparing the two truncation schemes (Theorem 15.7 vs. Theorem 16.6)

Notice how the lecture notes contrast our two uses of truncation:
- For the *Weak Law* (Theorem 15.7), we only needed to control one large \(n\) at a time for a fixed
  tolerance \(\varepsilon, \delta\), so a *fixed cutoff* \(|X_j| \le K\) sufficed: \(K\) was chosen first
  to make \(\mathbb E|R_1|\) uniformly small.
- For the *Strong Law* (Theorem 16.6), any fixed cutoff \(K\) would leave a positive probability
  \(\mathbb P(|X_j| > K) > 0\) of truncation at every step \(j\), so by Second Borel–Cantelli infinitely
  many \(X_j\) would be altered! Letting the *cutoff grow with the index* (\(|X_j| \le j\)) makes the tail
  probabilities \(\mathbb P(|X_j| > j)\) summable over \(j\), so almost surely only *finitely many* variables
  are ever modified.

* Averages as integrals

** Intuition

The Strong Law of Large Numbers turns expectations \(\mathbb E[f(X_1)] = \int f\,d\mu_{X_1}\) and
probabilities \(\mathbb P(X_1 \le x) = F(x)\) into limits of *sample averages*. This gives two foundational
tools of applied mathematics and statistics:
1. *Monte Carlo integration (Example 16.7):* To compute a definite integral \(\int_0^1 f(x)\,dx\), draw iid
   uniform points \(U_1, U_2, \dots \in (0,1)\) and average the function values \(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^n f(U_j)\).
2. *Empirical distribution functions (Example 16.8):* From an iid sample \(X_1, X_2, \dots\) with unknown
   CDF \(F\), form the step-function CDF \(F_n(x) = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^n \mathbf 1_{\{X_j \le x\}}\) of
   the first \(n\) data points. Using a countable rational grid \(\mathbb Q\) and monotonicity, we prove
   that on a *single* probability-one event, \(F_n(x)\) converges to \(F(x)\) at *every* continuity point
   \(x\) of \(F\) simultaneously!

** Monte Carlo integration

#+begin_quote
*Example 16.7 (Monte Carlo integration).* Let \(U_j : \Omega \to (0,1)\) be iid uniform variables and let
\(f : (0,1) \to \mathbb R\) be Borel measurable with \(\int_0^1 |f(x)|\,dx < \infty\). The specified range
makes every \(f(U_j)\) defined at every outcome. They are independent by Lemma 13.3; the image-measure
formula gives their common law and integrability. Therefore
\[
\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^n f(U_j) \longrightarrow \int_0^1 f(x)\,dx \quad \text{almost surely} .
\]
For \(f(x) = x^{-2/3}\), the integral is three but the second moment is infinite. The integrable strong
law still applies; the finite-variance error bound does not. The almost-sure limit itself supplies no
rate of approximation.
#+end_quote

Let's check every detail of Example 16.7:
- Because \(U_j\) has law \(\lambda|_{(0,1)}\) (Lebesgue measure on \((0,1)\)), the image-measure formula
  ([[file:ch06-integration.org][Chapter 6]]) gives
  \[
  \mathbb E|f(U_1)| = \int_0^1 |f(x)|\,dx < \infty, \qquad \mathbb E[f(U_1)] = \int_0^1 f(x)\,dx .
  \]
- By Lemma 13.3, \(f(U_1), f(U_2), \dots\) are iid integrable random variables, so Theorem 16.6 applies
  immediately!
- Look at the singular integrand \(f(x) = x^{-2/3}\) on \((0,1)\):
  - *First moment:* \(\int_0^1 x^{-2/3}\,dx = \big[3 x^{1/3}\big]_0^1 = 3 < \infty\).
  - *Second moment:* \(\mathbb E[f(U_1)^2] = \int_0^1 x^{-4/3}\,dx = \big[-3 x^{-1/3}\big]_0^1 = +\infty\).
  Here the finite-variance Strong Law (Theorem 16.1) and Chebyshev's \(O(n^{-1/2})\) error bound do *not*
  apply, because occasional draws of \(U_j\) very close to \(0\) produce huge spikes \(U_j^{-2/3}\). Yet
  Kolmogorov's integrable Strong Law (Theorem 16.6) still guarantees that \(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^n U_j^{-2/3} \to 3\)
  almost surely!

** Empirical distribution functions

#+begin_quote
*Example 16.8 (Empirical distribution functions).* For iid \(X_j\) with CDF \(F\), define
\[
F_n(x) := \frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^n \mathbf 1_{\{X_j \le x\}} .
\]
For each rational \(q\), the strong law gives \(F_n(q) \to F(q)\) almost surely. Countably many
intersections give one probability-one set on which this holds for every rational \(q\). Fix an outcome in
that set, a continuity point \(x\) of \(F\), and rational numbers \(q < x < r\). Monotonicity gives
\(F_n(q) \le F_n(x) \le F_n(r)\), and hence
\[
F(q) \le \liminf_n F_n(x) \le \limsup_n F_n(x) \le F(r) .
\]
Let \(q \uparrow x\) and \(r \downarrow x\) through rational values. Continuity at \(x\) squeezes both
limits to \(F(x)\). The probability-one set was chosen using only rational points, and the subsequent
argument works at every continuity point on that same set. Thus the convergence holds simultaneously at
all continuity points; no uncountable intersection of probability-one events was taken. This is a
pointwise conclusion, not a uniform convergence theorem.
#+end_quote

Let's admire the countable-grid technique in Example 16.8—a technique you will see throughout probability
and analysis:
1. There are *uncountably many* real numbers \(x \in \mathbb R\). If we applied Example 16.2 to each
   \(B_x = (-\infty, x]\), we would get an exceptional null set \(N_x\) for each \(x \in \mathbb R\), and
   we could not union \(\bigcup_{x \in \mathbb R} N_x\) without potentially losing measurability or getting
   a set of positive probability.
2. Instead, we restrict first to the *countable* dense set of rational numbers \(q \in \mathbb Q\). Since
   \(\mathbb Q\) is countable, the intersection
   \[
   \Omega_{\mathbb Q} := \bigcap_{q \in \mathbb Q} \{\omega : F_n(q)(\omega) \to F(q)\}
   \]
   has \(\mathbb P(\Omega_{\mathbb Q}) = 1\)!
3. Once \(\omega \in \Omega_{\mathbb Q}\) is fixed, *no more probability is needed*: for *any* real
   continuity point \(x \in \mathbb R\) of \(F\) and any rationals \(q < x < r\), the indicator inequality
   \(\mathbf 1_{\{X_j \le q\}} \le \mathbf 1_{\{X_j \le x\}} \le \mathbf 1_{\{X_j \le r\}}\) makes
   \(F_n(q)(\omega) \le F_n(x)(\omega) \le F_n(r)(\omega)\) hold deterministically. Letting \(n \to \infty\)
   and then squeezing \(q \uparrow x\) and \(r \downarrow x\) through \(\mathbb Q\) proves
   \(F_n(x)(\omega) \to F(x)\) simultaneously for all continuity points \(x\) of \(F\) on \(\Omega_{\mathbb Q}\)!
   (In fact, with a bit more work using a finite quantile grid, the *Glivenko–Cantelli theorem* shows
   that \(\sup_{x \in \mathbb R} |F_n(x) - F(x)| \to 0\) almost surely; see Exercise 6 below.)

** Historical remark (Kolmogorov, 1933)

Andrey Nikolaevich Kolmogorov's 1933 monograph /Grundbegriffe der Wahrscheinlichkeitsrechnung/ (Springer,
Berlin) stated the iid Strong Law of Large Numbers assuming only a finite expectation (p. 59) and attached
a striking footnote (p. 59, footnote 2): *the proof had not yet been published!*
- Kolmogorov's definition of expectation required absolute integrability \(\mathbb E|X_1| < \infty\) (p. 36),
  so his statement was the full integrable Strong Law of Theorem 16.6, not merely a finite-variance version.
- On the exact same page, Kolmogorov carefully distinguished the two: he stated his 1930 sufficient
  condition \(\sum_{j=1}^\infty \frac{\operatorname{var}(X_j)}{j^2} < \infty\) (which comes directly from
  Lemmas 16.4 and 16.5!) for independent variables that *need not have the same law*, alongside the iid
  theorem requiring only \(\mathbb E|X_1| < \infty\). These are different hypotheses serving different
  results, and our proof of Theorem 16.6 bridges the two via truncation at \(j\).

(Example 16.7). State the integrable theorem (Theorem 16.6) and explain its truncation scheme. The maximal
inequality (Lemma 16.3), series argument (Lemma 16.4), and Kronecker lemma (Lemma 16.5) form a complete

* Worked Exercises

** Exercise 1 (Kolmogorov's SLLN for non-identically distributed variables)
Let \(X_1, X_2, \dots\) be independent (not necessarily identically distributed) square-integrable random
variables with means \(m_j = \mathbb E X_j\) and variances \(\sigma_j^2 = \operatorname{var}(X_j)\).
Suppose that
\[
\sum_{j=1}^\infty \frac{\sigma_j^2}{j^2} < \infty .
\]
Use Lemmas 16.4 and 16.5 directly to prove that
\[
\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^n (X_j - m_j) \longrightarrow 0 \quad \text{almost surely} .
\]

** Solution
Define \(Z_j := \frac{X_j - m_j}{j}\) for \(j \ge 1\). Then \(Z_1, Z_2, \dots\) are independent, centered
(\(\mathbb E Z_j = 0\)), and square-integrable with
\[
\sum_{j=1}^\infty \mathbb E[Z_j^2] = \sum_{j=1}^\infty \frac{\operatorname{var}(X_j)}{j^2} = \sum_{j=1}^\infty \frac{\sigma_j^2}{j^2} < \infty .
\]
By Lemma 16.4, the series \(\sum_{j=1}^\infty \frac{X_j - m_j}{j}\) converges almost surely. At every
outcome \(\omega\) where this series converges, Kronecker's Lemma (Lemma 16.5) applied to
\(a_j = X_j(\omega) - m_j\) gives \(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^n (X_j(\omega) - m_j) \to 0\).

** Exercise 2 (Converse to the Strong Law when \(\mathbb E|X_1| = \infty\))
Let \(X_1, X_2, \dots\) be iid with \(\mathbb E|X_1| = \infty\).
1. Using the layer-cake bound \(\mathbb E|X_1| \le 1 + \sum_{j=1}^\infty \mathbb P(|X_1| \ge j)\) and the
   Second Borel–Cantelli Lemma, show that \(\mathbb P(|X_n| \ge n \text{ infinitely often}) = 1\).
2. Deduce that \(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^n X_j\) cannot converge to any finite real number almost surely.
   (/Hint:/ Express \(\frac{X_n}{n}\) in terms of \(\overline X_n = \frac{S_n}{n}\) and \(\overline X_{n-1} = \frac{S_{n-1}}{n-1}\).)

** Solution
1. For any \(x \ge 0\), \(x \le 1 + \sum_{j=1}^\infty \mathbf 1_{\{x \ge j\}}\). Taking \(x = |X_1|\) and
   integrating via Tonelli gives
   \[
   \infty = \mathbb E|X_1| \le 1 + \sum_{j=1}^\infty \mathbb P(|X_1| \ge j) = 1 + \sum_{j=1}^\infty \mathbb P(|X_j| \ge j) .
   \]
   Thus \(\sum_{j=1}^\infty \mathbb P(|X_j| \ge j) = \infty\). Since the events \(A_j := \{|X_j| \ge j\}\)
   are independent, the Second Borel–Cantelli Lemma (Theorem 15.1) gives \(\mathbb P(|X_n| \ge n \text{ i.o.}) = 1\).
2. Write \(S_n = \sum_{j=1}^n X_j\). Then \(X_n = S_n - S_{n-1}\), so dividing by \(n\) gives
   \[
   \frac{X_n}{n} = \frac{S_n}{n} - \frac{n-1}{n}\,\frac{S_{n-1}}{n-1} .
   \]
   If \(S_n(\omega) / n \to L \in \mathbb R\) at an outcome \(\omega\), then the right-hand side converges
   to \(L - 1 \cdot L = 0\), which forces \(X_n(\omega) / n \to 0\). By Part 1, \(|X_n(\omega)| / n \ge 1\)
   for infinitely many \(n\) almost surely, so \(\mathbb P(S_n / n \text{ converges to a finite limit}) = 0\)!

** Exercise 3 (Fourth-moment proof of the Strong Law without subsequences)
Suppose \(X_1, X_2, \dots\) are iid with mean \(0\) and finite fourth moment \(\mathbb E[X_1^4] = M < \infty\).
1. Expand \(S_n^4 = (\sum_{j=1}^n X_j)^4\) and use independence and \(\mathbb E X_j = 0\) to show that
   \[
   \mathbb E[S_n^4] = n \mathbb E[X_1^4] + 3n(n-1)(\mathbb E[X_1^2])^2 \le 3 M n^2 .
   \]
2. Use Markov's inequality on \(|S_n / n|^4\) and Corollary 15.3 to give a 3-line proof that
   \(S_n / n \to 0\) almost surely (without needing the square subsequence \(k^2\)).

** Solution
1. When we expand \((\sum_{j=1}^n X_j)^4 = \sum_{i,j,k,\ell} X_i X_j X_k X_\ell\), any term where at least
   one index appears with exponent \(1\) (such as \(X_1^3 X_2\), \(X_1^2 X_2 X_3\), or \(X_1 X_2 X_3 X_4\))
   factors by independence (Proposition 13.4) to include \(\mathbb E X_r = 0\), and therefore has expectation
   \(0\)! The only surviving terms are:
   - \(n\) terms of the form \(X_j^4\), each with expectation \(\mathbb E[X_1^4] = M\);
   - \(\binom{n}{2} \binom{4}{2} = \frac{n(n-1)}{2} \cdot 6 = 3n(n-1)\) terms of the form \(X_i^2 X_j^2\)
     (\(i \ne j\)), each with expectation \(\mathbb E[X_i^2]\mathbb E[X_j^2] = (\mathbb E[X_1^2])^2 \le \mathbb E[X_1^4] = M\)
     (by Cauchy–Schwarz/Jensen).
   Adding these gives \(\mathbb E[S_n^4] \le nM + 3n(n-1)M \le 3Mn^2\).
2. For any \(\varepsilon > 0\), Markov's inequality gives
   \[
   \mathbb P\Big(\Big|\frac{S_n}{n}\Big| > \varepsilon\Big)
     = \mathbb P(S_n^4 > n^4 \varepsilon^4)
     \le \frac{\mathbb E[S_n^4]}{n^4 \varepsilon^4}
     \le \frac{3M}{\varepsilon^4}\,\frac{1}{n^2} .
   \]
   Since \(\sum_{n=1}^\infty n^{-2} < \infty\), Corollary 15.3 immediately yields \(S_n / n \to 0\) a.s.

** Exercise 4 (Monte Carlo estimation of \(\pi\))
Let \((U_j, V_j)_{j\ge 1}\) be an iid sequence of points uniformly distributed on the unit square
\((0,1)^2\) (constructed by grouping an iid sequence of uniform \((0,1)\) variables into pairs). Show that
\[
\frac{4}{n}\,\#\{1 \le j \le n : U_j^2 + V_j^2 \le 1\} \longrightarrow \pi \quad \text{almost surely} ,
\]
and bound the probability that this estimator differs from \(\pi\) by \(\ge 0.01\) when \(n = 10^6\).

** Solution
Let \(I_j := \mathbf 1_{\{U_j^2 + V_j^2 \le 1\}}\). Since \((U_j, V_j)\) has law \(\lambda^2|_{(0,1)^2}\),
\[
p := \mathbb E I_1 = \mathbb P(U_1^2 + V_1^2 \le 1) = \text{area of the quarter-disk in } (0,1)^2 = \frac{\pi}{4} .
\]
By Theorem 16.1 (Example 16.2), \(\overline I_n = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^n I_j \to \frac{\pi}{4}\) a.s.,
so \(4\overline I_n \to \pi\) a.s.
For \(n = 10^6\), Chebyshev's inequality (Example 15.5) gives
\[
\mathbb P(|4\overline I_n - \pi| \ge 0.01)
  = \mathbb P(|\overline I_n - p| \ge 0.0025)
  \le \frac{p(1-p)}{n (0.0025)^2}
  = \frac{(\pi/4)(1 - \pi/4)}{10^6 \times 6.25 \times 10^{-6}} \approx 0.027 .
\]

** Exercise 5 (Renewal / waiting times via the Strong Law)
Let \(T_1, T_2, \dots\) be iid strictly positive waiting times between consecutive bus arrivals, with
mean \(\mathbb E T_1 = \mu \in (0, \infty)\). The arrival time of the \(n\)-th bus is \(S_n = \sum_{j=1}^n T_j\),
and the number of buses that have arrived by time \(t > 0\) is
\[
N_t := \max\{n \ge 0 : S_n \le t\} \quad (S_0 := 0) .
\]
Prove that \(\frac{N_t}{t} \to \frac{1}{\mu}\) almost surely as \(t \to \infty\).
(/Hint:/ Notice that \(S_{N_t} \le t < S_{N_t + 1}\) by definition of \(N_t\).)

** Solution
By Theorem 16.6, \(\frac{S_n}{n} \to \mu > 0\) almost surely as \(n \to \infty\). In particular,
\(S_n \to \infty\) a.s., so for each fixed \(t\), \(N_t < \infty\) a.s., and \(N_t \to \infty\) a.s. as
\(t \to \infty\).
By definition of \(N_t\), the \(N_t\)-th bus arrived at or before time \(t\), and the \((N_t + 1)\)-st bus
arrived strictly after time \(t\):
\[
S_{N_t} \le t < S_{N_t + 1} .
\]
For \(N_t \ge 1\), divide through by \(N_t\):
\[
\frac{S_{N_t}}{N_t} \le \frac{t}{N_t} < \frac{S_{N_t + 1}}{N_t + 1} \cdot \frac{N_t + 1}{N_t} .
\]
On the probability-one event where \(S_n / n \to \mu\) and \(N_t \to \infty\), both outer bounds converge
to \(\mu \cdot 1 = \mu\) as \(t \to \infty\). Taking reciprocals gives \(\frac{N_t}{t} \to \frac{1}{\mu}\)
almost surely.

** Exercise 6 (Uniform convergence of empirical CDFs when \(F\) is continuous)
In Example 16.8, suppose the true CDF \(F : \mathbb R \to [0,1]\) is *continuous*. Given any integer
\(m \ge 1\), pick finite grid points \(-\infty = x_0 < x_1 < \dots < x_{m-1} < x_m = +\infty\) such that
\(F(x_k) - F(x_{k-1}) = 1/m\) for \(k = 1, \dots, m\). Show that for every \(x \in \mathbb R\), if
\(x_{k-1} \le x \le x_k\), then
\[
\lvert F_n(x) - F(x)\rvert \le \max_{1 \le j \le m-1} \lvert F_n(x_j) - F(x_j)\rvert + \frac{1}{m} ,
\]
and deduce the *Glivenko–Cantelli theorem* for continuous \(F\):
\(\sup_{x \in \mathbb R} |F_n(x) - F(x)| \to 0\) almost surely.

** Solution
For \(x \in [x_{k-1}, x_k]\), monotonicity of \(F_n\) and \(F\) gives
\[
F_n(x_{k-1}) - F(x_k) \le F_n(x) - F(x) \le F_n(x_k) - F(x_{k-1}) .
\]
Since \(F(x_k) - F(x_{k-1}) = 1/m\), we can substitute \(F(x_k) = F(x_{k-1}) + 1/m\) on the left and
\(F(x_{k-1}) = F(x_k) - 1/m\) on the right:
\[
\big(F_n(x_{k-1}) - F(x_{k-1})\big) - \frac{1}{m}
  \le F_n(x) - F(x)
  \le \big(F_n(x_k) - F(x_k)\big) + \frac{1}{m}
\]
(with \(F_n(\pm\infty) = F(\pm\infty) \in \{0,1\}\)). Taking absolute values and the supremum over all
\(x \in \mathbb R\) gives
\[
\sup_{x \in \mathbb R} |F_n(x) - F(x)| \le \max_{1 \le j \le m-1} |F_n(x_j) - F(x_j)| + \frac{1}{m} .
\]
For each fixed \(m\), the maximum on the right is over *finitely many* points \(x_1, \dots, x_{m-1}\), so
by the Strong Law it tends to \(0\) as \(n \to \infty\) on a probability-one set \(\Omega_m\). On the
countable intersection \(\bigcap_{m=1}^\infty \Omega_m\), we have
\(\limsup_{n\to\infty} \sup_{x \in \mathbb R} |F_n(x) - F(x)| \le 1/m\) for every \(m \ge 1\), hence \(0\).

*Key takeaways*

- In the finite-variance SLLN (Theorem 16.1), evaluating along squares \(n = k^2\) makes the Chebyshev
  error bounds \(\sigma^2/(k^2\varepsilon^2)\) summable; monotonicity of \(S_n\) for nonnegative variables
  and the ratio \((k+1)^2/k^2 \to 1\) fill the gaps \(k^2 \le n < (k+1)^2\); splitting \(X_j = X_j^+ - X_j^-\)
  handles signed variables.
- Kolmogorov's maximal inequality (Lemma 16.3) splits \(\{\max_{1\le k\le n}|T_k|\ge a\}\) by the *first*
  crossing time \(A_k\), and uses independence of the future increment \(T_n - T_k\) from \(A_k\) to show
  \(\mathbb E[T_n^2 \mathbf 1_{A_k}] \ge a^2 \mathbb P(A_k)\).
- For the general integrable SLLN (Theorem 16.6), truncating at the index \(Y_j = X_j \mathbf 1_{\{|X_j|\le j\}}\)
  achieves two things simultaneously via \(\mathbb E|X_1| < \infty\): \(\sum_j \mathbb P(X_j \ne Y_j) \le \mathbb E|X_1| < \infty\)
  (so \(X_j = Y_j\) eventually a.s.) and \(\sum_j \operatorname{var}(Y_j)/j^2 \le 2\mathbb E|X_1| < \infty\)
  (so Lemma 16.4 and Kronecker's Lemma 16.5 apply).
- Whenever you need an almost-sure statement to hold *simultaneously* across an uncountable parameter
  (like all continuity points \(x \in \mathbb R\) of a CDF in Example 16.8), prove it first on a countable
  dense subset (\(\mathbb Q\)) and extend deterministically by monotonicity or continuity.
