* Overview

** What problem does this chapter solve?

Suppose we want to measure the "size" of subsets of a set \(\Omega\) — whether that size is length on
the real line \(\mathbb{R}\), area in the plane \(\mathbb{R}^2\), or the probability of an event in a
random experiment. A natural first guess is to assign a size to *every* subset of \(\Omega\). For a
finite set like the outcomes of rolling a single die, \(\Omega = \{1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6\}\), this works
perfectly. However, there are two fundamental reasons why we must specify a dedicated collection
\(\mathcal{F}\) of "measurable" subsets of \(\Omega\) rather than always using all subsets:

1. *Technical obstruction on uncountable spaces:* As we will see in Chapter 11, on \(\mathbb{R}\) it
   is mathematically impossible to assign a translation-invariant length to *every* subset of
   \(\mathbb{R}\) while keeping length countably additive. Certain pathological subsets (constructed
   using the Axiom of Choice) refuse to have a consistent length. Restricting our domain to a sticky,
   well-behaved collection of subsets — the *Borel sets* or *Lebesgue measurable sets* — avoids these
   paradoxes completely.
2. *Modelling information in probability:* Even on a tiny finite set like \(\Omega = \{1,2,3,4,5,6\}\),
   a collection of measurable sets \(\mathcal{F}\) records *which questions can be answered from a
   given partial observation*. For instance, if an observer is only told whether the die roll was even
   or odd, the subset \(\{2, 4, 6\}\) is observable ("measurable" relative to that information),
   whereas \(\{6\}\) is not.

Before we introduce any numbers or probabilities (which happens in Chapter 2), this chapter defines
the *stage* on which measure and probability take place: a *measurable space* \((\Omega, \mathcal{F})\),
consisting of an underlying set \(\Omega\) and a collection \(\mathcal{F}\) of subsets closed under
the logical operations of negation (complements) and countable "or" / "and" (countable unions and
intersections). Such a collection \(\mathcal{F}\) is called a *\(\sigma\)-algebra* (read
"sigma-algebra").

** Probability motivation

When we throw a die once, the outcome \(\omega\) belongs to the sample space
\(\Omega = \{1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6\}\). A subset such as \(\{2, 4, 6\}\) represents the statement "the
outcome is even," while \(\{6\}\) represents "the outcome is six." To build a probability model, we
first choose which subsets of \(\Omega\) count as valid *events* (the \(\sigma\)-algebra \(\mathcal{F}\)),
and then assign probabilities to those events (a probability measure \(\mathbb{P}\) with total size
\(1\)).

A crucial conceptual point from the lecture notes: *an event is simply a measurable set in a
probability space; it is not a different kind of mathematical object.* Moreover, the underlying set
\(\Omega\) does not have to be a subset of Euclidean space \(\mathbb{R}^d\): its elements can be die
outcomes, infinite sequences of coin tosses, or continuous functions (paths) on a time interval.

* Sets, subsets, and the power set

** Intuition

In measure theory we work on three levels of hierarchy at the same time:
1. *Level 0 (Points / Outcomes):* Individual elements \(\omega\) of the underlying set \(\Omega\).
2. *Level 1 (Subsets / Events):* Subsets \(A \subseteq \Omega\), which group points together.
3. *Level 2 (Collections / Families of subsets):* Sets whose elements are themselves subsets of
   \(\Omega\), such as the power set \(2^\Omega\) or a \(\sigma\)-algebra \(\mathcal{F} \subseteq 2^\Omega\).

Keeping these three levels straight prevents almost all beginner confusion. Think of \(\Omega\) as a
box of marbles, a subset \(A \subseteq \Omega\) as a small bag containing some of those marbles, and
a collection \(\mathcal{F} \subseteq 2^\Omega\) as a catalogue of permissible bags.

#+begin_example
Level 0 (point):         w  (a single outcome, e.g. H)
                         |  is an element of (in)
                         v
Level 1 (subset):       {w} (a set of outcomes, e.g. {H} subseteq Omega)
                         |  is an element of (in)
                         v
Level 2 (collection):   2^Omega = { emptyset, {H}, {T}, {H,T} }
#+end_example

** Definitions and notation

Let \(\Omega\) be an arbitrary set. Its elements need not be numbers, and no notion of distance or
open set is assumed on \(\Omega\). In a probability model, \(\Omega\) is called the *sample space*,
and its elements are called *outcomes* or *sample points*.

We carefully distinguish *membership* (\(\in\)) from *inclusion* (\(\subseteq\)):
- \(\omega \in A\) means that \(\omega\) is an *element* of \(A\) (\(\omega \notin A\) means it is not).
- \(A \subseteq B\) means that \(A\) is a *subset* of \(B\): every element of \(A\) also belongs to
  \(B\). Note that equality \(A = B\) is allowed in the notation \(A \subseteq B\).
- The *empty set*, denoted by \(\emptyset\), has no elements and is a subset of every set
  (\(\emptyset \subseteq A\) for all \(A\)).
- A set containing exactly one element, such as \(\{\omega\}\), is called a *singleton*. The curly
  braces matter: \(\omega\) is an element (Level 0), whereas \(\{\omega\}\) is the set containing
  that single element (Level 1).

#+begin_quote
*Definition (Power set).* The *power set* of \(\Omega\), denoted by \(2^\Omega\), is the collection
of all subsets of \(\Omega\):
\[
2^\Omega := \{A : A \subseteq \Omega\}.
\]
(Here the colon \(:\) is read as "such that," and \(:=\) means "is defined by.")
#+end_quote

By the power-set axiom of set theory, \(2^\Omega\) is a legitimate set (we are collecting subsets of
a fixed set \(\Omega\), not forming a contradictory "set of all sets"). In particular, since
\(\emptyset \subseteq \Omega\) and \(\Omega \subseteq \Omega\), both \(\emptyset\) and \(\Omega\) are
*elements* of \(2^\Omega\):
\[
\emptyset \in 2^\Omega \quad \text{and} \quad \Omega \in 2^\Omega.
\]

Why the notation \(2^\Omega\)? If \(\Omega\) is a finite set with \(n\) elements, then building a
subset \(A \subseteq \Omega\) requires making \(n\) independent binary choices: for each of the \(n\)
elements, we either *include* it in \(A\) or *omit* it. This yields \(2 \times 2 \times \cdots \times 2 = 2^n\)
possible subsets. For an infinite set \(\Omega\), the notation \(2^\Omega\) is kept by analogy to
mean the collection of all subsets of \(\Omega\).

We use the words *collection* or *family* for a "set of sets." Thus writing
\(\mathcal{F} \subseteq 2^\Omega\) means that \(\mathcal{F}\) is a collection of subsets of \(\Omega\):
every member \(A \in \mathcal{F}\) is a subset \(A \subseteq \Omega\).

** Examples and non-examples

*Example (A single coin toss).* Take \(\Omega = \{\mathrm{H}, \mathrm{T}\}\), where \(\mathrm{H}\) and
\(\mathrm{T}\) stand for heads and tails. There are \(n = 2\) outcomes, so the power set has
\(2^2 = 4\) elements:
\[
2^\Omega = \bigl\{\emptyset,\, \{\mathrm{H}\},\, \{\mathrm{T}\},\, \{\mathrm{H}, \mathrm{T}\}\bigr\}.
\]
Compare the three statements:
\[
\mathrm{H} \in \Omega, \qquad \{\mathrm{H}\} \subseteq \Omega, \qquad \{\mathrm{H}\} \in 2^\Omega.
\]
All three are true! The same object \(\{\mathrm{H}\}\) is viewed as a *subset* of \(\Omega\) when we
write \(\{\mathrm{H}\} \subseteq \Omega\), and as a single *element* of the collection \(2^\Omega\)
when we write \(\{\mathrm{H}\} \in 2^\Omega\).

Now consider the smaller collection \(\mathcal{F} = \{\emptyset, \Omega\} \subseteq 2^\Omega\), which
contains only two of the four subsets of \(\Omega\). Notice that
\[
\{\mathrm{H}\} \subseteq \Omega \quad \text{but} \quad \{\mathrm{H}\} \notin \mathcal{F}.
\]
Being a subset of \(\Omega\) and belonging to a chosen collection \(\mathcal{F}\) are completely
different requirements.

*Additional worked check (Common brace traps).* Let \(\Omega = \{1, 2\}\).
- Is \(1 \subseteq \Omega\)? *No:* \(1\) is an element of \(\Omega\), not a subset. We write
  \(1 \in \Omega\) or \(\{1\} \subseteq \Omega\).
- Is \(\emptyset \subseteq 2^\Omega\)? *Yes!* The empty set is a subset of *every* set, so
  \(\emptyset \subseteq 2^\Omega\) (this represents the empty collection of subsets, containing no
  subsets at all). At the same time, \(\emptyset \in 2^\Omega\) is *also* true, because \(\emptyset\)
  is a subset of \(\Omega\) and therefore one of the members of \(2^\Omega\).
- Is \(\{\emptyset\} = \emptyset\)? *No:* \(\emptyset\) has \(0\) elements, while \(\{\emptyset\}\)
  is a collection containing \(1\) element (namely, the empty set).

* Set operations and sequences

** Intuition

Set operations are the exact translation of logical connectives ("or", "and", "not") into the
language of subsets. If \(A\) and \(B\) are events in a sample space \(\Omega\), then:
- "at least one of \(A\) or \(B\) occurs" corresponds to the *union* \(A \cup B\);
- "both \(A\) and \(B\) occur" corresponds to the *intersection* \(A \cap B\);
- "\(A\) occurs, but \(B\) does not" corresponds to the *difference* \(A \setminus B\);
- "\(A\) does not occur" corresponds to the *complement* \(A^c = \Omega \setminus A\).

When we pass from finitely many operations to infinitely many (sequences of sets \(A_1, A_2, \dots\)),
"exists \(n\) such that \(A_n\) occurs" becomes the countable union \(\bigcup_{n=1}^\infty A_n\), and
"for all \(n\), \(A_n\) occurs" becomes the countable intersection \(\bigcap_{n=1}^\infty A_n\).

#+begin_example
+------------------------+   +------------------------+
| Omega                  |   | Omega                  |
|   +-------+            |   |   +-------+            |
|   | A   +-+-----+      |   |   | A   +-+-----+      |
|   |#####|#|#####|      |   |   |     |#|     |      |
|   +-----+-+   B |      |   |   +-----+-+   B |      |
|         +-------+      |   |         +-------+      |
+------------------------+   +------------------------+
   Union: A \cup B              Intersection: A \cap B

+------------------------+   +------------------------+
| Omega                  |   | Omega##################|

As Figure 1.1 in the lecture notes emphasises, these Venn diagrams are *schematic pictures of
membership*: the sets \(A, B\) need not be geometric regions in the plane, and the drawn areas on the
page do not specify measures or probabilities.

** Definitions

For sets \(A, B \subseteq \Omega\):
- The *union* \(A \cup B := \{\omega \in \Omega : \omega \in A \text{ or } \omega \in B\}\) consists
  of the elements belonging to at least one of them (inclusive "or").
- The *intersection* \(A \cap B := \{\omega \in \Omega : \omega \in A \text{ and } \omega \in B\}\)
  consists of the elements belonging to both.
- The *difference* \(A \setminus B := \{\omega \in A : \omega \notin B\}\) consists of the elements
  of \(A\) that do not belong to \(B\).
- The *complement* of \(A\) is \(A^c := \Omega \setminus A = \{\omega \in \Omega : \omega \notin A\}\).
  A complement is *always* taken relative to the specified underlying set \(\Omega\).

*Sequences of sets.* Throughout these notes, \(\mathbb{N} = \{1, 2, 3, \dots\}\) denotes the positive
integers. A *sequence of sets* is a list \(A_1, A_2, \dots\) indexed by \(\mathbb{N}\); repetitions
(such as \(A_1 = A_2\)) are completely allowed.
- Its *countable union* is
  \[
  \bigcup_{n=1}^\infty A_n := \{\omega \in \Omega : \omega \in A_n \text{ for at least one } n \in \mathbb{N}\}.
  \]
- Its *countable intersection* is
  \[
  \bigcap_{n=1}^\infty A_n := \{\omega \in \Omega : \omega \in A_n \text{ for every } n \in \mathbb{N}\}.
  \]
- *Convention:* A union over an empty index set is understood to be \(\emptyset\).

*Disjointness.*
- Two sets \(A\) and \(B\) are *disjoint* if their intersection is empty: \(A \cap B = \emptyset\).
- A sequence of sets \(A_1, A_2, \dots\) is *pairwise disjoint* if
  \[
  A_m \cap A_n = \emptyset \quad \text{whenever } m \ne n.
  \]
  (Warning: "pairwise disjoint" is much stronger than saying \(\bigcap_{n=1}^\infty A_n = \emptyset\)!
  Pairwise disjoint means *no two* sets in the list share any element.)

*Countability convention.*
#+begin_quote
*Definition (Countable and uncountable sets).* A set is called *countable* if it is finite or its
elements can be listed in a sequence indexed by \(\mathbb{N}\). A set that is not countable is
*uncountable*.
#+end_quote
In particular, *countable includes finite* throughout the entire course. (Some authors say "at most
countable" for this and reserve "countable" for countably infinite; Kuusi's lecture notes use
"countable" to mean finite or countably infinite.)

** Examples and non-examples

*Example (Basic operations).* Take \(\Omega = \{1, 2, 3\}\), \(A = \{1, 2\}\), and \(B = \{2, 3\}\).
Then:
\[
A \cup B = \{1, 2, 3\}, \qquad A \cap B = \{2\}, \qquad A \setminus B = \{1\}, \qquad A^c = \{3\}.
\]
Notice that set difference need not be symmetric: here \(B \setminus A = \{3\}\), which is different
from \(A \setminus B = \{1\}\). Also notice the useful identity
\[
A \setminus B = A \cap B^c,
\]
since an element of \(\Omega\) is in \(A\) and not in \(B\) if and only if it belongs to both \(A\)
and \(\Omega \setminus B = B^c\).

*Example (Pairwise disjoint vs. overlapping sequences).* In \(\Omega = \mathbb{N}\):
- The singletons \(A_n = \{n\}\) (\(\{1\}, \{2\}, \{3\}, \dots\)) are *pairwise disjoint* (since
  \(\{m\} \cap \{n\} = \emptyset\) for \(m \ne n\)) and have union \(\bigcup_{n=1}^\infty \{n\} = \mathbb{N}\).
- The initial segments \(B_n = \{1, \dots, n\}\) have the exact same union
  \(\bigcup_{n=1}^\infty B_n = \mathbb{N}\), but they are *not* pairwise disjoint (in fact,
  \(B_1 \subseteq B_2 \subseteq B_3 \subseteq \cdots\), so \(B_m \cap B_n = B_{\min(m,n)} \ne \emptyset\)).

* Which sets are measurable?

** Intuition

Which subsets of \(\Omega\) should belong to our collection \(\mathcal{F}\) of measurable sets (events)?
Think about what we naturally do with events in probability and analysis:
1. The whole sample space \(\Omega\) ("something happens") must be a valid event.
2. If \(A\) is a valid event, we must be able to ask whether \(A\) did *not* happen — so the
   complement \(A^c = \Omega \setminus A\) must also be in \(\mathcal{F}\).
3. In analysis and probability we constantly take limits (e.g., "does the random walk ever return to
   \(0\)?", which is the union over \(n = 1, 2, \dots\) of the event "returns at step \(n\)"). Thus,
   if \(A_1, A_2, \dots\) are valid events, their countable union \(\bigcup_{n=1}^\infty A_n\) must
   also be in \(\mathcal{F}\).

A collection \(\mathcal{F} \subseteq 2^\Omega\) satisfying these three closure properties is called a
*\(\sigma\)-algebra*. The letter \(\sigma\) (Greek \(s\), for German *Summe* / union) reminds us that
\(\mathcal{F}\) is closed under *countable* unions, not merely finite unions.

** Definition of a \(\sigma\)-algebra and basic consequences

#+begin_quote
*Definition 1.1 (\(\sigma\)-algebra and measurable space).* Let \(\Omega\) be a set. A collection
\(\mathcal{F} \subseteq 2^\Omega\) is a *\(\sigma\)-algebra* (or *sigma-algebra*) if it satisfies the
following conditions:
- (i) The set \(\Omega\) belongs to \(\mathcal{F}\).
- (ii) If \(A \in \mathcal{F}\), then \(\Omega \setminus A\) belongs to \(\mathcal{F}\).
- (iii) If \(A_n \in \mathcal{F}\) for every \(n \in \mathbb{N}\), then \(\bigcup_{n=1}^\infty A_n \in \mathcal{F}\).

The pair \((\Omega, \mathcal{F})\) is a *measurable space*, and the members of \(\mathcal{F}\) are its
*measurable sets*.
#+end_quote

Let us unpack several immediate consequences of Definition 1.1 that we use constantly without
having to build them into the axioms:

1. *The empty set belongs to \(\mathcal{F}\):* By (i), \(\Omega \in \mathcal{F}\). Applying (ii) to
   \(A = \Omega\) gives \(\emptyset = \Omega \setminus \Omega \in \mathcal{F}\).
2. *Closure under finite unions:* Suppose \(A_1, \dots, A_k \in \mathcal{F}\). Definition 1.1(iii)
   asks for an infinite sequence indexed by \(\mathbb{N}\), not a finite list! How do we bridge this?
   Since we already know \(\emptyset \in \mathcal{F}\), we simply pad the finite list with empty sets:
   define \(A_n := \emptyset\) for all \(n > k\). Then \(A_n \in \mathcal{F}\) for every \(n \in \mathbb{N}\),
   and (iii) tells us that
   \[
   A_1 \cup \cdots \cup A_k = A_1 \cup \cdots \cup A_k \cup \emptyset \cup \emptyset \cup \cdots = \bigcup_{n=1}^\infty A_n \in \mathcal{F}.
   \]
3. *Closure under countable (and finite) intersections:* Suppose \(A_n \in \mathcal{F}\) for every
   \(n \in \mathbb{N}\). Why is \(\bigcap_{n=1}^\infty A_n \in \mathcal{F}\)? This follows from
   *De Morgan's identity*:
   \[
   \bigcap_{n=1}^\infty A_n = \left(\bigcup_{n=1}^\infty A_n^c\right)^c.
   \]
   *Proof of De Morgan's identity:* Let \(\omega \in \Omega\). By definition, \(\omega \in \bigcap_{n=1}^\infty A_n\)
   if and only if \(\omega\) belongs to *every* \(A_n\); this holds if and only if \(\omega\) belongs
   to *none* of their complements \(A_n^c\), i.e., \(\omega \notin \bigcup_{n=1}^\infty A_n^c\), which
   means \(\omega \in \bigl(\bigcup_{n=1}^\infty A_n^c\bigr)^c\). \(\square\)

   Now check measurability on the right-hand side of De Morgan's identity: since each
   \(A_n \in \mathcal{F}\), property (ii) gives \(A_n^c \in \mathcal{F}\) for every \(n \in \mathbb{N}\).
   Property (iii) then gives \(\bigcup_{n=1}^\infty A_n^c \in \mathcal{F}\). Finally, applying
   property (ii) one more time gives \(\bigl(\bigcup_{n=1}^\infty A_n^c\bigr)^c \in \mathcal{F}\).
   (Finite intersections follow too, either by De Morgan or by padding with \(A_n = \Omega\) for
   \(n > k\).)
4. *Closure under set differences:* If \(A, B \in \mathcal{F}\), then \(B^c \in \mathcal{F}\) by (ii),
   and hence the difference
   \[
   A \setminus B = A \cap B^c
   \]
   belongs to \(\mathcal{F}\) as the intersection of two measurable sets.

** Two extreme \(\sigma\)-algebras on any set \(\Omega\)

On *any* set \(\Omega\), there are always two extreme examples of \(\sigma\)-algebras:
- *The largest \(\sigma\)-algebra (the power set):* \(\mathcal{F} = 2^\Omega\). It contains \(\Omega\),
  and complements and countable unions of subsets of \(\Omega\) are still subsets of \(\Omega\). Every
  subset of \(\Omega\) is measurable with respect to \(2^\Omega\).
- *The smallest \(\sigma\)-algebra (the trivial \(\sigma\)-algebra):* \(\mathcal{F} = \{\emptyset, \Omega\}\).
  It contains \(\Omega\), the complements are \(\Omega^c = \emptyset\) and \(\emptyset^c = \Omega\),
  and any union of \(\emptyset\)'s and \(\Omega\)'s is either \(\emptyset\) or \(\Omega\).

This immediately highlights a central lesson: *measurability is a condition relative to a chosen
\(\sigma\)-algebra \(\mathcal{F}\), not an intrinsic property of a subset \(A \subseteq \Omega\).*

** Examples from the notes and additional worked examples

#+begin_quote
*Example 1.2 (Two \(\sigma\)-algebras for a die).* For a single die roll, take
\(\Omega = \{1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6\}\) and let \(D = \{2, 4, 6\}\) be the set of even outcomes (so
\(D^c = \{1, 3, 5\}\) is the set of odd outcomes). Consider the four-element collection
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{F} = \{\emptyset, D, D^c, \Omega\}. \tag{1.1}
\end{equation}
Then \(\mathcal{F}\) is a \(\sigma\)-algebra on \(\Omega\). With respect to \(\mathcal{F}\), the set
\(D = \{2, 4, 6\}\) is measurable, whereas \(\{6\}\) is *not* measurable. With the larger
\(\sigma\)-algebra \(2^\Omega\) (which has \(2^6 = 64\) sets), every subset — including \(\{6\}\) — is
measurable.
#+end_quote

*Step-by-step verification of Example 1.2:*
- Condition (i): \(\Omega \in \mathcal{F}\) by inspection.
- Condition (ii): The complements of the four members \(\emptyset, D, D^c, \Omega\) are
  \(\Omega, D^c, D, \emptyset\), all of which belong to \(\mathcal{F}\).
- Condition (iii): Why is \(\mathcal{F}\) closed under countable unions \(\bigcup_{n=1}^\infty A_n\)?
  Notice that \(\Omega\) is partitioned into two non-overlapping "blocks" (atoms), \(D = \{2, 4, 6\}\)
  and \(D^c = \{1, 3, 5\}\). Every member of \(\mathcal{F}\) is built by taking none, one, or both of
  these two blocks. Therefore, taking the union of any sequence of sets from \(\mathcal{F}\) simply
  combines whichever of the two blocks \(D\) and \(D^c\) appear in the sequence, producing again one
  of the four sets \(\emptyset, D, D^c, \Omega \in \mathcal{F}\).

#+caption: Figure 1.2: Coarse vs. Fine sigma-Algebras (Parity vs. Power Set)

   +-----------------------+   +-----------------------+

#+end_example

As the lecture notes point out, \(\{6\} \notin \mathcal{F}\) is already a genuine example of a
*nonmeasurable set* (relative to the \(\sigma\)-algebra (1.1)). It does not require the Axiom of
Choice or any exotic construction! Nor does it mean that \(\{6\}\) can never be measured — it is
measurable in the richer model \((\Omega, 2^\Omega)\). Later (in Chapter 11) we will encounter
failures of measurability on \(\mathbb{R}\) that cannot be fixed merely by enlarging the
\(\sigma\)-algebra while keeping a translation-invariant measure.

*Additional Example A (The countable-cocountable \(\sigma\)-algebra).* Let \(\Omega\) be an uncountable
set (for instance, \(\Omega = \mathbb{R}\)), and define
\[
\mathcal{F} := \{A \subseteq \Omega : A \text{ is countable or } A^c \text{ is countable}\}.
\]
(A set whose complement \(A^c\) is countable is often called *cocountable*.) Let us check that
\(\mathcal{F}\) is a \(\sigma\)-algebra:
- (i) \(\Omega^c = \emptyset\) is finite, hence countable, so \(\Omega \in \mathcal{F}\).
- (ii) If \(A \in \mathcal{F}\), then either \(A = (A^c)^c\) is countable or \(A^c\) is countable; in
  either case \(A^c \in \mathcal{F}\).
- (iii) Let \(A_n \in \mathcal{F}\) for \(n \in \mathbb{N}\), and set \(U = \bigcup_{n=1}^\infty A_n\).
  - *Case 1:* Every \(A_n\) is countable. Since a countable union of countable sets is countable
    (Chapter 0), \(U\) is countable, so \(U \in \mathcal{F}\).
  - *Case 2:* At least one set, say \(A_{n_0}\), has a countable complement \(A_{n_0}^c\). Since
    \(A_{n_0} \subseteq U\), taking complements reverses the inclusion:
    \(U^c \subseteq A_{n_0}^c\). A subset of a countable set is countable, so \(U^c\) is countable,
    and again \(U \in \mathcal{F}\).
Thus \(\mathcal{F}\) is a \(\sigma\)-algebra! Note that on \(\Omega = \mathbb{R}\), the interval
\([0, 1]\) is uncountable and its complement \((-\infty, 0) \cup (1, \infty)\) is also uncountable,
so \([0, 1] \notin \mathcal{F}\).

*Non-example B (Closed under finite unions, but NOT countable unions).* Let \(\Omega = \mathbb{N}\)
and consider the *finite-cofinite collection*
\[
\mathcal{A} := \{A \subseteq \mathbb{N} : A \text{ is finite or } A^c \text{ is finite}\}.
\]
Here \(\mathbb{N} \in \mathcal{A}\), \(\mathcal{A}\) is closed under complements, and \(\mathcal{A}\)
is closed under *finite* unions (such a collection is called an *algebra* of sets; see Chapter 5).
However, \(\mathcal{A}\) is *not* a \(\sigma\)-algebra! Indeed, for each \(n \in \mathbb{N}\), the
singleton \(A_n = \{2n\}\) is finite, so \(A_n \in \mathcal{A}\). Yet their countable union is the set
of all even positive integers:
\[
E = \bigcup_{n=1}^\infty \{2n\} = \{2, 4, 6, 8, \dots\}.
\]
Both \(E\) and \(E^c = \{1, 3, 5, 7, \dots\}\) are infinite, so \(E \notin \mathcal{A}\).

* Generated sigma-algebras

** Intuition

In almost every problem on an infinite space like \(\mathbb{R}\), it is impossible to write down an
explicit list of all measurable sets one by one. Instead, we start with a simple collection
\(\mathcal{A}\) of basic sets that we *definitely want* to be measurable — for instance, all open sets
in \(\mathbb{R}\), or all open intervals \((a, b)\), or all half-lines \((-\infty, a]\). We then throw
in everything else that is forced upon us by taking complements and countable unions over and over
again.

The result is the *smallest \(\sigma\)-algebra containing \(\mathcal{A}\)*, denoted by
\(\sigma(\mathcal{A})\) and called the *\(\sigma\)-algebra generated by \(\mathcal{A}\)*. How do we
know there is a single "smallest" one, rather than several incomparable minimal ones? By a classic
"top-down" trick that appears throughout mathematics (for span in linear algebra, convex hull in
geometry, or subgroup generated by a set): we take the *intersection of all \(\sigma\)-algebras on
\(\Omega\) that contain \(\mathcal{A}\)*.

** Definition and existence of \(\sigma(\mathcal{A})\)

#+begin_quote
*Definition 1.3 (Generated \(\sigma\)-algebra).* For a collection \(\mathcal{A} \subseteq 2^\Omega\),
the *\(\sigma\)-algebra generated by \(\mathcal{A}\)*, denoted by \(\sigma(\mathcal{A})\), is the
smallest \(\sigma\)-algebra containing \(\mathcal{A}\).
#+end_quote

Let us be crystal clear about the words in Definition 1.3:
- "containing \(\mathcal{A}\)" means \(\mathcal{A} \subseteq \sigma(\mathcal{A})\) (every set
  \(A \in \mathcal{A}\) belongs to \(\sigma(\mathcal{A})\));
- "smallest" refers to inclusion of collections: if \(\mathcal{G}\) is *any* \(\sigma\)-algebra on
  \(\Omega\) with \(\mathcal{A} \subseteq \mathcal{G}\), then \(\sigma(\mathcal{A}) \subseteq \mathcal{G}\).

*Proof that \(\sigma(\mathcal{A})\) always exists and is unique.*
Consider the family of all candidate \(\sigma\)-algebras on \(\Omega\) that contain \(\mathcal{A}\):
\[
\mathfrak{S} := \{\mathcal{G} \subseteq 2^\Omega : \mathcal{G} \text{ is a } \sigma\text{-algebra on } \Omega \text{ and } \mathcal{A} \subseteq \mathcal{G}\}.
\]
1. *Non-emptiness:* There is at least one such candidate in \(\mathfrak{S}\), namely the power set
   \(\mathcal{G} = 2^\Omega\) (since \(2^\Omega\) is a \(\sigma\)-algebra and \(\mathcal{A} \subseteq 2^\Omega\)).
2. *Intersection:* Define a collection of subsets of \(\Omega\) by keeping a subset \(E \subseteq \Omega\)
   precisely when \(E\) belongs to *every* candidate \(\mathcal{G} \in \mathfrak{S}\):
   \[
   \sigma(\mathcal{A}) := \bigcap_{\mathcal{G} \in \mathfrak{S}} \mathcal{G} = \{E \subseteq \Omega : E \in \mathcal{G} \text{ for all } \mathcal{G} \in \mathfrak{S}\}.
   \]
3. *Verify the \(\sigma\)-algebra axioms for \(\sigma(\mathcal{A})\):*
   - *Contains \(\Omega\):* Every candidate \(\mathcal{G} \in \mathfrak{S}\) is a \(\sigma\)-algebra,
     so \(\Omega \in \mathcal{G}\) for all \(\mathcal{G} \in \mathfrak{S}\). Hence \(\Omega \in \sigma(\mathcal{A})\).
   - *Closed under complements:* If \(E \in \sigma(\mathcal{A})\), then for every candidate
     \(\mathcal{G} \in \mathfrak{S}\) we have \(E \in \mathcal{G}\), and since \(\mathcal{G}\) is a
     \(\sigma\)-algebra, \(E^c \in \mathcal{G}\). Because this holds for every \(\mathcal{G} \in \mathfrak{S}\),
     we have \(E^c \in \sigma(\mathcal{A})\).
   - *Closed under countable unions:* If \(E_n \in \sigma(\mathcal{A})\) for every \(n \in \mathbb{N}\),
     then for each candidate \(\mathcal{G} \in \mathfrak{S}\), every \(E_n\) belongs to \(\mathcal{G}\),
     and since \(\mathcal{G}\) is a \(\sigma\)-algebra, \(\bigcup_{n=1}^\infty E_n \in \mathcal{G}\).
     Because this holds for every candidate \(\mathcal{G} \in \mathfrak{S}\), we have
     \(\bigcup_{n=1}^\infty E_n \in \sigma(\mathcal{A})\).
4. *Verify containment and minimality:* Since every \(\mathcal{G} \in \mathfrak{S}\) contains
   \(\mathcal{A}\), their intersection \(\sigma(\mathcal{A})\) contains \(\mathcal{A}\). And if
   \(\mathcal{G}_0\) is any \(\sigma\)-algebra containing \(\mathcal{A}\), then \(\mathcal{G}_0\) is
   one of the members of \(\mathfrak{S}\), so the intersection over all \(\mathcal{G} \in \mathfrak{S}\)
   is automatically a subcollection of \(\mathcal{G}_0\): \(\sigma(\mathcal{A}) \subseteq \mathcal{G}_0\). \(\square\)

*Example (Generating the parity \(\sigma\)-algebra).* In the die example (\(\Omega = \{1,\dots,6\}\),
\(D = \{2, 4, 6\}\)), what is \(\sigma(\{D\})\)? Any \(\sigma\)-algebra containing \(D\) must contain
\(D^c\), \(\emptyset\), and \(\Omega\), so \(\{\emptyset, D, D^c, \Omega\} \subseteq \sigma(\{D\})\).
On the other hand, Example 1.2 already showed that \(\mathcal{F} = \{\emptyset, D, D^c, \Omega\}\) *is*
a \(\sigma\)-algebra containing \(\{D\}\). Hence \(\sigma(\{D\}) = \{\emptyset, D, D^c, \Omega\}\):
requiring \(D\) to be measurable forces \(D^c, \emptyset, \Omega\) and nothing further.

** The Borel \(\sigma\)-algebra on \(\mathbb{R}\)

The most important generated \(\sigma\)-algebra in analysis and probability is the Borel
\(\sigma\)-algebra on the real line.

#+begin_quote
*Definition (Borel \(\sigma\)-algebra on \(\mathbb{R}\) and on subspaces).*
- On \(\mathbb{R}\), the \(\sigma\)-algebra generated by the collection of all open subsets of
  \(\mathbb{R}\) is called the *Borel \(\sigma\)-algebra* and is denoted by \(\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R})\):
  \[
  \mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}) := \sigma(\{U \subseteq \mathbb{R} : U \text{ is open}\}).
  \]
  Its members are called *Borel sets*.
- For any subset \(S \subseteq \mathbb{R}\), we define
  \[
  \mathcal{B}(S) := \{S \cap B : B \in \mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R})\};
  \]
  these are the *Borel sets of the subspace \(S\)* (often called the *trace \(\sigma\)-algebra* on \(S\)).
#+end_quote

What sets belong to \(\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R})\)?
- Every *open set* \(U \subseteq \mathbb{R}\) belongs to \(\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R})\) by definition.
- Every *closed set* \(F \subseteq \mathbb{R}\) belongs to \(\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R})\), because a set
  is closed if and only if its complement \(F^c\) is open. In particular, every closed interval
  \([a, b]\), every closed half-line \((-\infty, a]\) or \([a, \infty)\), and every singleton
  \(\{x\} = [x, x]\) is a Borel set.
- Every *half-open interval* \((a, b] = (-\infty, b] \setminus (-\infty, a]\) or
  \([a, b) = [a, \infty) \cap (-\infty, b)\) is a Borel set.
- Every *countable set* (such as \(\mathbb{Z}\) or \(\mathbb{Q} = \bigcup_{q \in \mathbb{Q}} \{q\}\)) is
  a Borel set, being a countable union of singletons; its complement \(\mathbb{R} \setminus \mathbb{Q}\)
  (the irrationals) is therefore also a Borel set.

** Generating \(\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R})\) from countably many rational half-lines

The collection of all open subsets of \(\mathbb{R}\) is huge (uncountable). Yet in probability theory
we describe a random variable \(X\) via its cumulative distribution function \(F(a) = \mathbb{P}(X \le a)\),
which only probes half-lines \((-\infty, a]\). Even better, the lecture notes establish a fundamental
structural fact: *a countable collection of rational half-lines already generates \(\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R})\).*

#+begin_quote
*Proposition (Rational half-lines generate \(\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R})\)).* Let \(\mathbb{Q}\) denote
the set of rational numbers, and let
\[
\mathcal{A}_{\mathbb{Q}} := \{(-\infty, a] : a \in \mathbb{Q}\}.
\]
Then \(\sigma(\mathcal{A}_{\mathbb{Q}}) = \mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R})\).
#+end_quote

Let us unpack the lecture notes' proof step by step. Notice the standard *two-inclusion strategy* for
proving that two generated \(\sigma\)-algebras \(\sigma(\mathcal{A}_1)\) and \(\sigma(\mathcal{A}_2)\)
are equal:
- To show \(\sigma(\mathcal{A}_1) \subseteq \sigma(\mathcal{A}_2)\), it suffices to check that every
  generator in \(\mathcal{A}_1\) belongs to \(\sigma(\mathcal{A}_2)\) (then minimality of
  \(\sigma(\mathcal{A}_1)\) gives the inclusion!).
- Conversely, to show \(\sigma(\mathcal{A}_2) \subseteq \sigma(\mathcal{A}_1)\), check that every
  generator in \(\mathcal{A}_2\) belongs to \(\sigma(\mathcal{A}_1)\).

#+caption: Figure 1.3: Generating the Borel sigma-Algebra from Rational Half-Lines
#+begin_example
Roadmap of the reverse inclusion:
   Rational half-lines (-\infty, q]   (q \in \mathbb{Q})
        |
        |  Step 1: Countable intersection over rational q > t
        v
   Real half-lines     (-\infty, t]   (t \in \mathbb{R})
        |
        |  Step 2: Complement + union of (-\infty, b - 1/n]
        v
   Open intervals      (a, b)         (a < b in \mathbb{R})
        |
        |  Step 3: Countable union of rational intervals
        v
   Arbitrary open set  U \subseteq \mathbb{R}
#+end_example

*Unpacked Proof:*

*Direction 1: \(\sigma(\mathcal{A}_{\mathbb{Q}}) \subseteq \mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R})\).*
For every \(a \in \mathbb{Q}\), the half-line \((-\infty, a]\) is the complement of the open set
\((a, \infty)\), so \((-\infty, a] \in \mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R})\). Thus
\(\mathcal{A}_{\mathbb{Q}} \subseteq \mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R})\). Since \(\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R})\) is a
\(\sigma\)-algebra containing \(\mathcal{A}_{\mathbb{Q}}\), the minimality of
\(\sigma(\mathcal{A}_{\mathbb{Q}})\) immediately yields
\(\sigma(\mathcal{A}_{\mathbb{Q}}) \subseteq \mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R})\).

*Direction 2: \(\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}) \subseteq \sigma(\mathcal{A}_{\mathbb{Q}})\).*
To prove this, we must show that every open set \(U \subseteq \mathbb{R}\) belongs to
\(\sigma(\mathcal{A}_{\mathbb{Q}})\). We climb the ladder in three steps:

- *Step 1 (From rational half-lines to real half-lines):* Fix any real number \(t \in \mathbb{R}\). We
  claim that
  \[
  (-\infty, t] = \bigcap_{q \in \mathbb{Q},\, q > t} (-\infty, q].
  \]
  *Why does this set equality hold?*
  - (\(\subseteq\)): If \(x \le t\), then for every rational \(q > t\) we have \(x \le t < q\), so
    \(x \in (-\infty, q]\). Thus \(x\) belongs to the intersection on the right.
  - (\(\supseteq\)): Suppose \(x \notin (-\infty, t]\), which means \(x > t\). By the *density of the
    rationals* in \(\mathbb{R}\), there exists a rational number \(q_0 \in \mathbb{Q}\) strictly
    between \(t\) and \(x\): \(t < q_0 < x\). Then \(x \notin (-\infty, q_0]\), so \(x\) is excluded
    from the intersection on the right.
  Since \(\mathbb{Q}\) is countable, the index set \(\{q \in \mathbb{Q} : q > t\}\) is countable. Each
  set \((-\infty, q]\) in the intersection belongs to \(\mathcal{A}_{\mathbb{Q}} \subseteq \sigma(\mathcal{A}_{\mathbb{Q}})\),
  and \(\sigma(\mathcal{A}_{\mathbb{Q}})\) is closed under countable intersections, so
  \((-\infty, t] \in \sigma(\mathcal{A}_{\mathbb{Q}})\) for *every* \(t \in \mathbb{R}\).

- *Step 2 (From real half-lines to open intervals):* Fix any real numbers \(a < b\). We can write the
  open interval \((a, b)\) using real-endpoint half-lines as
  \[
  (a, b) = (-\infty, a]^c \cap \bigcup_{n=1}^\infty (-\infty, b - 1/n].
  \]
  *Why does this set equality hold?* First, \((-\infty, a]^c = (a, \infty)\) consists of all points
  \(x > a\). Second, a point \(x\) satisfies \(x < b\) if and only if \(b - x > 0\), which by the
  Archimedean property holds if and only if there exists \(n \in \mathbb{N}\) with \(1/n \le b - x\),
  i.e., \(x \le b - 1/n\). Thus \(\bigcup_{n=1}^\infty (-\infty, b - 1/n] = (-\infty, b)\).
  Intersecting \((a, \infty)\) with \((-\infty, b)\) gives \((a, b)\).

  Since \((-\infty, a]\) and every \((-\infty, b - 1/n]\) belong to \(\sigma(\mathcal{A}_{\mathbb{Q}})\)
  by Step 1, closure under complements, countable unions, and intersections shows that
  \((a, b) \in \sigma(\mathcal{A}_{\mathbb{Q}})\) for all \(a < b\).

- *Step 3 (From open intervals to arbitrary open sets):* Let \(U \subseteq \mathbb{R}\) be any open
  set. We claim that \(U\) is the union of all open intervals with *rational* endpoints that are
  contained in \(U\):
  \[
  U = \bigcup_{p, q \in \mathbb{Q},\, p < q,\, (p, q) \subseteq U} (p, q).
  \]
  *Why does this set equality hold?*
  - (\(\supseteq\)): Every interval \((p, q)\) participating in the union is explicitly required to
    satisfy \((p, q) \subseteq U\), so their union is contained in \(U\).
  - (\(\subseteq\)): Conversely, take any point \(x \in U\). Because \(U\) is open, there is some
    open interval \((x - \delta, x + \delta) \subseteq U\) around \(x\) (with \(\delta > 0\)). By the
    density of \(\mathbb{Q}\), we can pick rational numbers \(p \in (x - \delta, x)\) and
    \(q \in (x, x + \delta)\). Then \(p, q \in \mathbb{Q}\), \(p < x < q\), and
    \((p, q) \subseteq (x - \delta, x + \delta) \subseteq U\). Thus \(x \in (p, q)\) for one of the
    intervals in the union, so \(x\) belongs to the right-hand side!

  *Why is this union countable?* Because \(\mathbb{Q}\) is countable, we can list all rationals as
  \(r_1, r_2, r_3, \dots\), and then list all pairs of indices \((i, j)\) in order of increasing sum
  \(i + j\). Therefore there are only countably many rational pairs \((p, q)\), and the subcollection
  of pairs with \(p < q\) and \((p, q) \subseteq U\) is countable. Since each \((p, q) \in \sigma(\mathcal{A}_{\mathbb{Q}})\)
  by Step 2, this countable union belongs to \(\sigma(\mathcal{A}_{\mathbb{Q}})\). Hence
  \(U \in \sigma(\mathcal{A}_{\mathbb{Q}})\) for every open set \(U\), which proves
  \(\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}) \subseteq \sigma(\mathcal{A}_{\mathbb{Q}})\). \(\square\)

*(Remark on Step 3:* If we had tried to write \(U = \bigcup_{a,b \in \mathbb{R},\, (a,b) \subseteq U} (a,b)\)
using *all* real endpoints \(a, b \in \mathbb{R}\), the set equality would still be true, but the
union would be over an *uncountable* index set, and a \(\sigma\)-algebra is only guaranteed to be
closed under *countable* unions! Restricting to rational endpoints \(p, q \in \mathbb{Q}\) is the key
manoeuvre that makes the union countable.)

* Worked Exercises

** Exercise 1 (A union of \(\sigma\)-algebras need not be a \(\sigma\)-algebra)
Let \(\Omega = \{1, 2, 3\}\), and consider the two \(\sigma\)-algebras
\[
\mathcal{F}_1 = \bigl\{\emptyset, \{1\}, \{2, 3\}, \Omega\bigr\} \quad \text{and} \quad
\mathcal{F}_2 = \bigl\{\emptyset, \{2\}, \{1, 3\}, \Omega\bigr\}.
\]
1. Verify that \(\mathcal{F}_1 \cap \mathcal{F}_2\) is a \(\sigma\)-algebra.
2. Show that \(\mathcal{F}_1 \cup \mathcal{F}_2\) is *not* a \(\sigma\)-algebra, and find
   \(\sigma(\mathcal{F}_1 \cup \mathcal{F}_2)\).

** Solution
1. The common elements of \(\mathcal{F}_1\) and \(\mathcal{F}_2\) are only \(\emptyset\) and
   \(\Omega\), so \(\mathcal{F}_1 \cap \mathcal{F}_2 = \{\emptyset, \Omega\}\), which is the trivial
   \(\sigma\)-algebra on \(\Omega\). (Indeed, our existence proof for \(\sigma(\mathcal{A})\) showed
   that the intersection of *any* collection of \(\sigma\)-algebras on \(\Omega\) is always a
   \(\sigma\)-algebra.)
2. Listing the elements of the union gives
   \[
   \mathcal{F}_1 \cup \mathcal{F}_2 = \bigl\{\emptyset, \{1\}, \{2\}, \{1, 3\}, \{2, 3\}, \Omega\bigr\}.
   \]
   Both \(\{1\}\) and \(\{2\}\) belong to \(\mathcal{F}_1 \cup \mathcal{F}_2\), yet their union
   \(\{1\} \cup \{2\} = \{1, 2\}\) does *not* belong to \(\mathcal{F}_1 \cup \mathcal{F}_2\) (nor does
   its complement \(\{3\}\)). Therefore \(\mathcal{F}_1 \cup \mathcal{F}_2\) is not closed under
   unions and is not a \(\sigma\)-algebra.

   To find \(\sigma(\mathcal{F}_1 \cup \mathcal{F}_2)\), note that any \(\sigma\)-algebra containing
   \(\mathcal{F}_1 \cup \mathcal{F}_2\) contains \(\{1\}\), \(\{2\}\), and
   \(\{3\} = (\{1\} \cup \{2\})^c\), hence contains all singletons of \(\Omega = \{1,2,3\}\), and by
   taking unions contains all \(2^3 = 8\) subsets of \(\Omega\). Thus
   \(\sigma(\mathcal{F}_1 \cup \mathcal{F}_2) = 2^\Omega\).

** Exercise 2 (\(\sigma\)-algebra generated by a finite partition)
Let \(\Omega = \{1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6\}\) and let \(\mathcal{A} = \bigl\{\{1, 2\}, \{3, 4\}\bigr\}\).
Describe all sets in \(\sigma(\mathcal{A})\) explicitly, and state how many sets \(\sigma(\mathcal{A})\)
contains.

** Solution
Notice that \(B_1 := \{1, 2\}\) and \(B_2 := \{3, 4\}\) are disjoint subsets of \(\Omega\). Any
\(\sigma\)-algebra containing \(B_1\) and \(B_2\) must also contain the remaining piece
\[
B_3 := \Omega \setminus (B_1 \cup B_2) = \{5, 6\}.
\]
The three nonempty sets \(B_1, B_2, B_3\) are pairwise disjoint and partition \(\Omega\) into \(3\)
blocks: \(\Omega = B_1 \cup B_2 \cup B_3\). Taking unions of any subcollection of these \(3\) blocks
produces \(2^3 = 8\) subsets of \(\Omega\):
\[
\mathcal{G} = \bigl\{\emptyset,\, \{1,2\},\, \{3,4\},\, \{5,6\},\, \{1,2,3,4\},\, \{1,2,5,6\},\, \{3,4,5,6\},\, \Omega\bigr\}.
\]
Just as in Example 1.2, \(\mathcal{G}\) contains \(\Omega\), is closed under complements (the
complement of a union of blocks is the union of the remaining blocks), and is closed under countable
unions (any union of unions of blocks is still a union of blocks). Since \(\mathcal{A} \subseteq \mathcal{G}\)
and every set in \(\mathcal{G}\) was forced by \(\sigma\)-algebra operations on \(\mathcal{A}\), we
conclude that \(\sigma(\mathcal{A}) = \mathcal{G}\), which has \(8\) elements.

** Exercise 3 (\(\sigma\)-algebra generated by singletons on an uncountable space)
Let \(\Omega\) be an uncountable set (e.g., \(\Omega = \mathbb{R}\)), and let
\(\mathcal{A} = \bigl\{\{\omega\} : \omega \in \Omega\bigr\}\) be the collection of all singletons.
Prove that \(\sigma(\mathcal{A})\) is the countable-cocountable \(\sigma\)-algebra from Additional
Example A:
\[
\sigma(\mathcal{A}) = \mathcal{F} := \{A \subseteq \Omega : A \text{ is countable or } A^c \text{ is countable}\}.
\]

** Solution
We use the standard two-inclusion method:
- *Step 1 (\(\sigma(\mathcal{A}) \subseteq \mathcal{F}\)):* In Additional Example A we proved that
  \(\mathcal{F}\) is a \(\sigma\)-algebra on \(\Omega\). Every singleton \(\{\omega\} \in \mathcal{A}\)
  has \(1\) element, so it is finite and hence countable, which means \(\{\omega\} \in \mathcal{F}\).
  Thus \(\mathcal{A} \subseteq \mathcal{F}\). Since \(\mathcal{F}\) is a \(\sigma\)-algebra
  containing \(\mathcal{A}\), minimality of \(\sigma(\mathcal{A})\) gives
  \(\sigma(\mathcal{A}) \subseteq \mathcal{F}\).
- *Step 2 (\(\mathcal{F} \subseteq \sigma(\mathcal{A})\)):* Take any \(A \in \mathcal{F}\).
  - If \(A = \emptyset\), then \(A \in \sigma(\mathcal{A})\) automatically.
  - If \(A\) is nonempty and countable, we can list its elements as \(\omega_1, \omega_2, \dots\) (a
    finite or infinite sequence). Then \(A = \bigcup_{n} \{\omega_n\}\) is a countable union of
    singletons from \(\mathcal{A}\), so \(A \in \sigma(\mathcal{A})\).
  - If \(A^c\) is countable, then by the previous bullet \(A^c \in \sigma(\mathcal{A})\), and taking
    the complement gives \(A = (A^c)^c \in \sigma(\mathcal{A})\).
  Thus every member of \(\mathcal{F}\) belongs to \(\sigma(\mathcal{A})\), so
  \(\mathcal{F} \subseteq \sigma(\mathcal{A})\). Combining Steps 1 and 2 gives
  \(\sigma(\mathcal{A}) = \mathcal{F}\).

** Exercise 4 (Alternative generators for \(\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R})\))
Prove that the Borel \(\sigma\)-algebra \(\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R})\) is also generated by:
1. the collection \(\mathcal{C}_1 = \{[a, b] : a < b,\, a, b \in \mathbb{R}\}\) of all closed intervals;
2. the collection \(\mathcal{C}_2 = \{(a, \infty) : a \in \mathbb{Q}\}\) of open right half-lines with
   rational endpoints.

** Solution
1. *For \(\mathcal{C}_1\):* Every closed interval \([a, b]\) is closed in \(\mathbb{R}\) (its
   complement \((-\infty, a) \cup (b, \infty)\) is open), so \([a, b] \in \mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R})\).
   Thus \(\mathcal{C}_1 \subseteq \mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R})\), which implies
   \(\sigma(\mathcal{C}_1) \subseteq \mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R})\).
   Conversely, for any open interval \((a, b)\) with \(a < b\), choose \(N \in \mathbb{N}\) large
   enough that \(a + 1/N < b - 1/N\). Then
   \[
   (a, b) = \bigcup_{n=N}^\infty \left[a + \frac{1}{n},\, b - \frac{1}{n}\right] \in \sigma(\mathcal{C}_1).
   \]
   Since every open set \(U \subseteq \mathbb{R}\) is a countable union of open intervals \((p, q)\)
   with rational endpoints (Section 1.4, Step 3), every open set belongs to \(\sigma(\mathcal{C}_1)\),
   so \(\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}) \subseteq \sigma(\mathcal{C}_1)\).
2. *For \(\mathcal{C}_2\):* For every \(a \in \mathbb{Q}\), we have
   \((a, \infty) = (-\infty, a]^c\). Therefore a \(\sigma\)-algebra on \(\mathbb{R}\) contains
   \((a, \infty)\) if and only if it contains its complement \((-\infty, a]\). It follows immediately
   that \(\sigma(\mathcal{C}_2) = \sigma(\{(-\infty, a] : a \in \mathbb{Q}\}) = \mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R})\).

** Exercise 5 (Verifying that the subspace Borel sets \(\mathcal{B}(S)\) form a \(\sigma\)-algebra)
Let \(S \subseteq \mathbb{R}\) be an arbitrary subset of \(\mathbb{R}\) (note that \(S\) itself does
*not* even have to be a Borel set!). Show that
\[
\mathcal{B}(S) := \{S \cap B : B \in \mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R})\}
\]
is a \(\sigma\)-algebra on the underlying set \(S\).

** Solution
We check the three conditions of Definition 1.1 on the underlying space \(S\):
- (i) Since \(\mathbb{R} \in \mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R})\), we have
  \(S = S \cap \mathbb{R} \in \mathcal{B}(S)\).
- (ii) Suppose \(E \in \mathcal{B}(S)\). By definition, \(E = S \cap B\) for some
  \(B \in \mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R})\). The complement of \(E\) *relative to \(S\)* is
  \[
  S \setminus E = S \setminus (S \cap B) = S \cap (\mathbb{R} \setminus B).
  \]
  Since \(\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R})\) is a \(\sigma\)-algebra on \(\mathbb{R}\), we have
  \(\mathbb{R} \setminus B \in \mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R})\), and hence
  \(S \setminus E \in \mathcal{B}(S)\).
- (iii) Suppose \(E_n \in \mathcal{B}(S)\) for every \(n \in \mathbb{N}\). For each \(n\), pick a set
  \(B_n \in \mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R})\) such that \(E_n = S \cap B_n\). Then by the distributive law for
  intersections over unions,
  \[
  \bigcup_{n=1}^\infty E_n = \bigcup_{n=1}^\infty (S \cap B_n) = S \cap \left(\bigcup_{n=1}^\infty B_n\right).
  \]
  Since \(\bigcup_{n=1}^\infty B_n \in \mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R})\), it follows that
  \(\bigcup_{n=1}^\infty E_n \in \mathcal{B}(S)\). Thus \((S, \mathcal{B}(S))\) is a measurable space.

*Key takeaways*

- Keep the three levels distinct: outcomes \(\omega \in \Omega\), subsets \(A \subseteq \Omega\) (i.e.
  \(A \in 2^\Omega\)), and collections of subsets \(\mathcal{F} \subseteq 2^\Omega\).
- "Measurable set" always means \(A \in \mathcal{F}\) for a specified \(\sigma\)-algebra
  \(\mathcal{F}\). In a probability space, measurable sets are called *events*.
- To prove \(\sigma(\mathcal{A}_1) \subseteq \sigma(\mathcal{A}_2)\), you never need to construct sets
  by transfinite induction: simply verify that every generator \(A \in \mathcal{A}_1\) belongs to the
  \(\sigma\)-algebra \(\sigma(\mathcal{A}_2)\).
- On \(\mathbb{R}\), the density and countability of \(\mathbb{Q}\) allow us to pass from rational
  half-lines \((-\infty, q]\) to real half-lines \((-\infty, t]\), then to open intervals \((a, b)\),
  and finally to all open sets \(U \subseteq \mathbb{R}\) using only countable set operations.
