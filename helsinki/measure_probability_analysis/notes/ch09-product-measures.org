* Overview

** What problem does this chapter solve?

In multivariable calculus, we compute a double integral \(\iint_E f(s,t) \, ds \, dt\) by slicing the region \(E\) horizontally or vertically and integrating one variable at a time:
\[
\iint_E f(s, t) \, ds \, dt = \int \left( \int f(s, t) \, dt \right) ds = \int \left( \int f(s, t) \, ds \right) dt.
\]
We also take for granted that the area of a rectangle \(A \times B\) is \(\text{length}(A) \times \text{length}(B)\).

When we move from smooth functions on boxes in \(\mathbb{R}^2\) to *general measure spaces* \((S, \mathcal{S}, \mu)\) and \((T, \mathcal{T}, \nu)\)—including discrete spaces, probability spaces, and measurable sets \(E \subseteq S \times T\) that are not rectangles—three serious mathematical questions arise:

1. *Which subsets of \(S \times T\) are measurable, and what happens when we slice them?* A general region \(E \subseteq S \times T\) (such as a triangle or disk) is not a rectangle \(A \times B\). We define the *product \(\sigma\)-algebra* \(\mathcal{S} \otimes \mathcal{T}\) generated by measurable rectangles and show that every horizontal or vertical *slice* ("section") of a product-measurable set or function is itself measurable (Section 9.1).
2. *How do we define the measure \(\mu \otimes \nu\) of a non-rectangular set \(E \subseteq S \times T\)?* Knowing \((\mu \otimes \nu)(A \times B) = \mu(A)\nu(B)\) on rectangles does not yet define \((\mu \otimes \nu)(E)\) for a general set \(E \in \mathcal{S} \otimes \mathcal{T}\). Instead of running Carathéodory's outer-measure machine all over again, Theorem 9.2 uses a much more direct and illuminating route: *Cavalieri's principle*! We define the measure of \(E\) by integrating the measures of its slices, \(\int_S \nu(E_s) \, d\mu(s)\), and use Dynkin's \(\pi\)-\(\lambda\) theorem (Chapter 4) to show this is well-defined and equals \(\int_T \mu(E^t) \, d\nu(t)\) whenever \(\mu\) and \(\nu\) are \(\sigma\)-finite (Section 9.2).
3. *When is it legal to swap the order of integration \(\int_S \int_T f \, d\nu \, d\mu = \int_T \int_S f \, d\mu \, d\nu\)?*
   - For *nonnegative* product-measurable functions \(f \ge 0\), *Tonelli's theorem* (Theorem 9.4) says: *always* (assuming \(\sigma\)-finiteness), with no integrability check needed!
   - For *signed* product-measurable functions \(f : S \times T \to \mathbb{R}\), *Fubini's theorem* (Theorem 9.5) says: whenever \(f\) is *integrable* on the product space, i.e., \(\int_{S \times T} |f| \, d(\mu \otimes \nu) < \infty\). Both \(\sigma\)-finiteness and absolute integrability are genuinely needed, as classic counterexamples show (Examples 9.6 and 9.8).
   - Finally, we extend products to three or more factors and examine the subtle interaction between product measures and *completion*, including the construction of a non-Lebesgue-measurable *Vitali set* (Section 9.4).

** Probability motivation

To describe two random experiments performed together—or two random variables \(X\) taking values in \(S\) and \(Y\) taking values in \(T\)—we use ordered pairs \((s, t) \in S \times T\).
- If \(X \sim \mu\) and \(Y \sim \nu\) are *independent* (Chapter 13), the joint distribution of the pair \((X, Y)\) on \(S \times T\) is precisely the *product measure* \(\mu \otimes \nu\):
  \[
  \mathbb{P}(X \in A \text{ and } Y \in B) = \mathbb{P}((X, Y) \in A \times B) = \mu(A)\nu(B) = \mathbb{P}(X \in A)\mathbb{P}(Y \in B).
  \]
- Even for a single random variable \(X \ge 0\) on \((\Omega, \mathcal{F}, \mathbb{P})\), taking the product of \((\Omega, \mathcal{F}, \mathbb{P})\) with \(([0, \infty), \mathcal{B}([0, \infty)), \lambda)\) and applying Tonelli's theorem to \(\mathbf{1}_{\{\omega, t : 0 \le t < X(\omega)\}}\) immediately yields the famous *tail-integral (layer-cake) formula* for expectation:
  \[
  \mathbb{E}[X] = \int_\Omega \left( \int_0^\infty \mathbf{1}_{\{t < X(\omega)\}} \, dt \right) d\mathbb{P}(\omega) = \int_0^\infty \mathbb{P}(X > t) \, dt.
  \]
- When one space is discrete (counting measure on \(\mathbb{N}\)) and the other is a probability space, Tonelli and Fubini tell us exactly when we can interchange an expectation and an infinite sum: \(\mathbb{E}\big[\sum_{n=1}^\infty Z_n\big] = \sum_{n=1}^\infty \mathbb{E}[Z_n]\).

* Rectangles and sections

** Intuition

When we form the Cartesian product \(S \times T = \{(s, t) : s \in S, t \in T\}\) of two measurable spaces \((S, \mathcal{S})\) and \((T, \mathcal{T})\), the simplest subsets of \(S \times T\) are *measurable rectangles*
\[
A \times B = \{(s, t) \in S \times T : s \in A \text{ and } t \in B\}, \qquad A \in \mathcal{S},\; B \in \mathcal{T}.
\]
Beware of the word "rectangle": \(A\) and \(B\) do *not* have to be intervals! They can be any measurable sets in \(\mathcal{S}\) and \(\mathcal{T}\) (for example, \(A = \mathbb{Q} \cap [0,1]\) and \(B = [0,1/2] \cup [3/4, 1]\)).

Just as open intervals do not form a \(\sigma\)-algebra on \(\mathbb{R}\) (the complement of a rectangle in \(S \times T\) is a union of two or three rectangles, and a diagonal triangle is not a finite union of rectangles), the collection of measurable rectangles is *not* a \(\sigma\)-algebra. Instead, it is a *\(\pi\)-system* (closed under finite intersections), and we define the product \(\sigma\)-algebra \(\mathcal{S} \otimes \mathcal{T}\) to be the \(\sigma\)-algebra *generated* by all measurable rectangles.

To integrate over a general set \(E \subseteq S \times T\) one variable at a time, we slice \(E\) along a fixed first coordinate \(s \in S\) or along a fixed second coordinate \(t \in T\):

#+caption: Figure 9.1: Sections of a Measurable Set in a Product Space
#+begin_example

  |
t | - - - - - - - - - +---------------+ - -
  |                   |               |
  |        /          \               /     E^t = {s in S : (s,t) in E}
  |       |            |      E       |     (horizontal slice)
  |        \          /               \

Notice closely where the sections live:
- Fixing \(s \in S\), the *section at \(s\)* is \(E_s := \{t \in T : (s, t) \in E\}\), which is a subset of \(T\)!
- Fixing \(t \in T\), the *section at \(t\)* is \(E^t := \{s \in S : (s, t) \in E\}\), which is a subset of \(S\)!

** Definitions and Basic Properties

Let \((S, \mathcal{S})\) and \((T, \mathcal{T})\) be measurable spaces. Their *Cartesian product* \(S \times T\) consists of all ordered pairs \((s, t)\) with \(s \in S\) and \(t \in T\).

#+begin_quote
*Definition (Measurable rectangles and product \(\sigma\)-algebra).* A *measurable rectangle* is a set of the form \(A \times B\) with \(A \in \mathcal{S}\) and \(B \in \mathcal{T}\). The *product \(\sigma\)-algebra* on \(S \times T\) is defined by
\[
\mathcal{S} \otimes \mathcal{T} := \sigma\{A \times B : A \in \mathcal{S},\; B \in \mathcal{T}\}.
\]
Sets in \(\mathcal{S} \otimes \mathcal{T}\) are called *product measurable*.
#+end_quote

*Two fundamental facts about measurable rectangles and \(\mathcal{S} \otimes \mathcal{T}\):*
1. *Measurable rectangles form a \(\pi\)-system:* In a Cartesian product, intersections happen coordinate by coordinate:
   \[
   (A_1 \times B_1) \cap (A_2 \times B_2) = (A_1 \cap A_2) \times (B_1 \cap B_2).
   \]
   Since \(A_1 \cap A_2 \in \mathcal{S}\) and \(B_1 \cap B_2 \in \mathcal{T}\), the intersection of two measurable rectangles is again a measurable rectangle.
2. *Characterization via coordinate projections:* Let \(\pi_S : S \times T \to S\), \(\pi_S(s, t) = s\), and \(\pi_T : S \times T \to T\), \(\pi_T(s, t) = t\), be the two coordinate maps. Then \(\mathcal{S} \otimes \mathcal{T}\) is the *smallest \(\sigma\)-algebra on \(S \times T\) making both \(\pi_S\) and \(\pi_T\) measurable*.
   - *Why?* For any \(A \in \mathcal{S}\) and \(B \in \mathcal{T}\), the preimages under the coordinate maps are rectangles:
     \[
     \pi_S^{-1}(A) = A \times T, \qquad \pi_T^{-1}(B) = S \times B.
     \]
     Thus any \(\sigma\)-algebra containing all measurable rectangles makes \(\pi_S\) and \(\pi_T\) measurable. Conversely, any \(\sigma\)-algebra making \(\pi_S\) and \(\pi_T\) measurable must contain \(\pi_S^{-1}(A) = A \times T\) and \(\pi_T^{-1}(B) = S \times B\), and therefore also their intersection
     \[
     (A \times T) \cap (S \times B) = A \times B.
     \]

#+begin_quote
*Definition (Sections of sets and functions).* For a set \(E \subseteq S \times T\):
- Its *section at \(s \in S\)* is \(E_s := \{t \in T : (s, t) \in E\} \subseteq T\).
- Its *section at \(t \in T\)* is \(E^t := \{s \in S : (s, t) \in E\} \subseteq S\).

Similarly, for a function \(f : S \times T \to \mathbb{R}\) (or \([0, \infty]\)):
- Its *section at \(s \in S\)* is the one-variable function \(f_s : T \to \mathbb{R}\) defined by \(f_s(t) := f(s, t)\).
- Its *section at \(t \in T\)* is the one-variable function \(f^t : S \to \mathbb{R}\) defined by \(f^t(s) := f(s, t)\).
#+end_quote

*Proposition (Sections of product-measurable sets and functions are measurable).*
1. If \(E \in \mathcal{S} \otimes \mathcal{T}\), then \(E_s \in \mathcal{T}\) for every \(s \in S\), and \(E^t \in \mathcal{S}\) for every \(t \in T\).
2. If \(f : S \times T \to \mathbb{R}\) (or \([0, \infty]\)) is \(\mathcal{S} \otimes \mathcal{T}\)-measurable, then \(f_s\) is \(\mathcal{T}\)-measurable for every \(s \in S\), and \(f^t\) is \(\mathcal{S}\)-measurable for every \(t \in T\).

*Proof (via the "good sets" principle).*
Fix \(s \in S\) and consider the collection of "good sets"
\[
\mathcal{G} := \{E \subseteq S \times T : E_s \in \mathcal{T}\}.
\]
- *Rectangles are in \(\mathcal{G}\):* For a measurable rectangle \(E = A \times B\), what is its section \((A \times B)_s\)?
  \[
  (A \times B)_s = \{t \in T : (s, t) \in A \times B\} = \begin{cases} B & \text{if } s \in A, \\ \emptyset & \text{if } s \notin A. \end{cases}
  \]
  Since both \(B\) and \(\emptyset\) belong to \(\mathcal{T}\), every measurable rectangle is in \(\mathcal{G}\).
- *\(\mathcal{G}\) is a \(\sigma\)-algebra:* Taking a section at \(s\) commutes with all set operations! Specifically:
  \[
  (E^c)_s = \{t \in T : (s, t) \notin E\} = (E_s)^c, \qquad \left(\bigcup_{n=1}^\infty E_n\right)_s = \{t \in T : \exists n,\; (s, t) \in E_n\} = \bigcup_{n=1}^\infty (E_n)_s.
  \]
  Therefore, if \(E \in \mathcal{G}\), then \(E_s \in \mathcal{T} \implies (E^c)_s = (E_s)^c \in \mathcal{T} \implies E^c \in \mathcal{G}\); and if \(E_n \in \mathcal{G}\) for all \(n\), then \(\big(\bigcup_n E_n\big)_s = \bigcup_n (E_n)_s \in \mathcal{T} \implies \bigcup_n E_n \in \mathcal{G}\).
Since \(\mathcal{G}\) is a \(\sigma\)-algebra containing all measurable rectangles, it contains the generated \(\sigma\)-algebra \(\mathcal{S} \otimes \mathcal{T}\). By symmetry, \(E^t \in \mathcal{S}\) for every \(t \in T\).

Finally, if \(f\) is product measurable and \(B \subseteq \mathbb{R}\) is a Borel set, then \(E := f^{-1}(B) \in \mathcal{S} \otimes \mathcal{T}\). Notice that the preimage of \(B\) under the section function \(f_s\) is simply the section of the preimage \(f^{-1}(B)\):
\[
f_s^{-1}(B) = \{t \in T : f(s, t) \in B\} = \big(f^{-1}(B)\big)_s \in \mathcal{T}.
\]
Thus \(f_s\) is \(\mathcal{T}\)-measurable (and symmetrically \(f^t\) is \(\mathcal{S}\)-measurable). \(\blacksquare\)

** Examples from the notes

#+begin_quote
*Example 9.1 (A triangle).* In \([0, 1]^2\), the set \(E = \{(s, t) \in [0, 1]^2 : s < t\}\) is product measurable, since
\[
E = \bigcup_{q \in \mathbb{Q} \cap [0, 1]} \big([0, q) \times (q, 1]\big).
\]
For fixed \(s \in [0, 1]\), its vertical section is \(E_s = (s, 1]\), of length \(1 - s\). For fixed \(t \in [0, 1]\), its horizontal section is \(E^t = [0, t)\), of length \(t\). Integrating either section length gives \(1/2\):
\[
\int_0^1 (1 - s) \, ds = \frac{1}{2}, \qquad \int_0^1 t \, dt = \frac{1}{2}.
\]
The theorem below explains why both computations give the measure of the same set.
#+end_quote

*Unpacking Example 9.1:* Why does the countable union over \(q \in \mathbb{Q} \cap [0, 1]\) equal \(\{(s, t) : s < t\}\)? By density of \(\mathbb{Q}\) in \(\mathbb{R}\), we have \(s < t\) if and only if there exists a rational number \(q \in \mathbb{Q} \cap [0, 1]\) with \(s < q < t\), which means \((s, t) \in [0, q) \times (q, 1]\). Each set \([0, q) \times (q, 1]\) is a measurable rectangle in \(\mathcal{B}([0, 1]) \otimes \mathcal{B}([0, 1])\), and a countable union of measurable rectangles belongs to the product \(\sigma\)-algebra!

** Euclidean Borels: \(\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^m) \otimes \mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^n) = \mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^{m+n})\)

On Euclidean spaces, the product of the Borel \(\sigma\)-algebras is the Borel \(\sigma\)-algebra of the product:
\[
\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^m) \otimes \mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^n) = \mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^{m+n}).
\]
Let us verify both inclusions carefully:
- *(\(\subseteq\)) Product measurable implies Borel in \(\mathbb{R}^{m+n}\):* The coordinate projection maps \(\pi_1 : \mathbb{R}^m \times \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}^m\) and \(\pi_2 : \mathbb{R}^m \times \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}^n\) are continuous, hence Borel measurable (preimages of open sets are open, so preimages of Borel sets are Borel). Therefore, for any \(A \in \mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^m)\) and \(B \in \mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^n)\), the rectangle \(A \times B = \pi_1^{-1}(A) \cap \pi_2^{-1}(B)\) belongs to \(\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^{m+n})\). Since \(\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^{m+n})\) is a \(\sigma\)-algebra containing all Borel rectangles, it contains \(\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^m) \otimes \mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^n)\).
- *(\(\supseteq\)) Borel in \(\mathbb{R}^{m+n}\) implies product measurable:* Every open set \(U \subseteq \mathbb{R}^{m+n}\) can be written as a countable union of open boxes with rational coordinates, and each open box \(I_1 \times I_2\) (with \(I_1 \subseteq \mathbb{R}^m\) and \(I_2 \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n\) open boxes) is a measurable rectangle in \(\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^m) \otimes \mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^n)\). Thus \(\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^m) \otimes \mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^n)\) contains every open subset of \(\mathbb{R}^{m+n}\), and hence contains \(\sigma(\text{open sets}) = \mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^{m+n})\).

* Constructing the product measure

** Intuition

Given two measure spaces \((S, \mathcal{S}, \mu)\) and \((T, \mathcal{T}, \nu)\), we want a measure \(\mu \otimes \nu\) on \(\mathcal{S} \otimes \mathcal{T}\) that assigns to every measurable rectangle \(A \times B\) the product of its side measures:
\[
(\mu \otimes \nu)(A \times B) = \mu(A)\nu(B) \qquad (\text{with } 0 \cdot \infty = \infty \cdot 0 = 0).
\]
How do we define \((\mu \otimes \nu)(E)\) when \(E \in \mathcal{S} \otimes \mathcal{T}\) is *not* a rectangle?
Look back at Example 9.1 (the triangle): to find its area, we took the length \(\nu(E_s)\) of each vertical slice \(E_s\) and integrated over \(s \in S\):
\[
(\mu \otimes \nu)(E) = \int_S \nu(E_s) \, d\mu(s).
\]
This is *Cavalieri's principle* from geometry: the volume of a solid is the integral of its cross-sectional areas!

To turn Cavalieri's formula into a rigorous proof of existence and uniqueness of \(\mu \otimes \nu\), we have one technical hurdle to clear first: we know from Section 9.1 that the slice \(E_s \subseteq T\) is a \(\mathcal{T}\)-measurable set, so \(\nu(E_s)\) is a well-defined number in \([0, \infty]\) for each \(s\). *But is the function \(s \mapsto \nu(E_s)\) an \(\mathcal{S}\)-measurable function of \(s\)?* If it wasn't measurable, we wouldn't be allowed to integrate it with respect to \(\mu\)!

In Step 1 of Theorem 9.2 (when \(\mu\) and \(\nu\) are finite measures), we prove that \(s \mapsto \nu(E_s)\) is measurable for *every* \(E \in \mathcal{S} \otimes \mathcal{T}\) using *Dynkin's \(\pi\)-\(\lambda\) lemma* (Lemma 4.3). Notice why finiteness of \(\nu\) is needed right there: to show that the collection \(\mathcal{D}\) of sets with measurable section-measure functions is closed under complements, we write
\[
\nu\big((E^c)_s\big) = \nu(T \setminus E_s) = \nu(T) - \nu(E_s),
\]
which requires \(\nu(T) < \infty\) to avoid \(\infty - \infty\)! Once the finite case is settled, Step 2 extends everything to *\(\sigma\)-finite* measures by cutting \(S\) and \(T\) into countable partitions of finite-measure pieces \(S_i\) and \(T_j\) and summing over the cells \(S_i \times T_j\).

** The Product Measure Theorem

#+begin_quote
*Theorem 9.2 (Product measure).* Let \((S, \mathcal{S}, \mu)\) and \((T, \mathcal{T}, \nu)\) be \(\sigma\)-finite measure spaces. There is a unique measure \(\mu \otimes \nu\) on \(\mathcal{S} \otimes \mathcal{T}\) such that
\[
(\mu \otimes \nu)(A \times B) = \mu(A)\nu(B)
\]
for every measurable rectangle \(A \times B\), with \(0 \cdot \infty = \infty \cdot 0 = 0\). For every product-measurable set \(E \in \mathcal{S} \otimes \mathcal{T}\), the section-measure functions \(s \mapsto \nu(E_s)\) and \(t \mapsto \mu(E^t)\) are measurable functions (on \(S\) and \(T\) respectively) and
\[
(\mu \otimes \nu)(E) = \int_S \nu(E_s) \, d\mu(s) = \int_T \mu(E^t) \, d\nu(t). \tag{9.1}
\]
#+end_quote

** Proof of Theorem 9.2 (unpacked step by step)

*Step 1. Finite measures (\(\mu(S) < \infty\) and \(\nu(T) < \infty\)).*
Suppose first that both \(\mu\) and \(\nu\) are finite measures.

- *Step 1a: Show \(s \mapsto \nu(E_s)\) is measurable via Dynkin's lemma.* Let
  \[
  \mathcal{D} := \{E \in \mathcal{S} \otimes \mathcal{T} : \text{the function } s \mapsto \nu(E_s) \text{ is } \mathcal{S}\text{-measurable}\}.
  \]
  We check that \(\mathcal{D}\) contains the \(\pi\)-system \(\mathcal{P}\) of measurable rectangles and is a \(\lambda\)-system:
  1. *Rectangles belong to \(\mathcal{D}\):* For \(E = A \times B\) with \(A \in \mathcal{S}, B \in \mathcal{T}\), we saw in Section 9.1 that \(E_s = B\) if \(s \in A\) and \(E_s = \emptyset\) if \(s \notin A\). Thus
     \[
     \nu\big((A \times B)_s\big) = \nu(B)\mathbf{1}_A(s),
     \]
     which is a constant multiple of a measurable indicator function, hence \(\mathcal{S}\)-measurable. In particular, the whole space \(S \times T\) belongs to \(\mathcal{D}\).
  2. *Closed under complements:* If \(E \in \mathcal{D}\), then \((E^c)_s = T \setminus E_s\). Because \(\nu(T) < \infty\), we can legitimately subtract:
     \[
     \nu\big((E^c)_s\big) = \nu(T) - \nu(E_s).
     \]
     Since the constant \(\nu(T)\) and \(s \mapsto \nu(E_s)\) are measurable, their difference is measurable, so \(E^c \in \mathcal{D}\).
  3. *Closed under countable disjoint unions:* If \(E_1, E_2, \dots \in \mathcal{D}\) are pairwise disjoint in \(S \times T\), then for each fixed \(s \in S\) their sections \((E_1)_s, (E_2)_s, \dots\) are pairwise disjoint subsets of \(T\). By countable additivity of \(\nu\),
     \[
     \nu\left( \Big(\bigcup_{j=1}^\infty E_j\Big)_s \right) = \nu\left( \bigcup_{j=1}^\infty (E_j)_s \right) = \sum_{j=1}^\infty \nu\big((E_j)_s\big).
     \]
     As the pointwise limit of finite partial sums of measurable functions, this infinite sum is an \(\mathcal{S}\)-measurable function of \(s\). Thus \(\bigcup_{j=1}^\infty E_j \in \mathcal{D}\).
  Therefore \(\mathcal{D}\) is a \(\lambda\)-system containing the \(\pi\)-system \(\mathcal{P}\) of measurable rectangles. By Dynkin's \(\pi\)-\(\lambda\) lemma (Lemma 4.3), \(\mathcal{D} \supseteq \sigma(\mathcal{P}) = \mathcal{S} \otimes \mathcal{T}\), so \(\mathcal{D} = \mathcal{S} \otimes \mathcal{T}\).

- *Step 1b: Define \(\lambda_1(E) := \int_S \nu(E_s) \, d\mu(s)\) and check it is a measure.*
  1. For \(E = \emptyset\), \(\emptyset_s = \emptyset\) has \(\nu(\emptyset_s) = 0\), so \(\lambda_1(\emptyset) = \int_S 0 \, d\mu = 0\).
  2. For pairwise disjoint \(E_1, E_2, \dots \in \mathcal{S} \otimes \mathcal{T}\), we have \(\nu\big((\bigcup_{j=1}^\infty E_j)_s\big) = \sum_{j=1}^\infty \nu((E_j)_s)\). Applying the Monotone Convergence Theorem (Theorem 6.6) to the increasing partial sums \(\sum_{j=1}^N \nu((E_j)_s) \uparrow \sum_{j=1}^\infty \nu((E_j)_s)\) allows us to pass the sum through the integral:
     \[
     \lambda_1\left(\bigcup_{j=1}^\infty E_j\right) = \int_S \left( \sum_{j=1}^\infty \nu\big((E_j)_s\big) \right) d\mu(s) = \sum_{j=1}^\infty \int_S \nu\big((E_j)_s\big) \, d\mu(s) = \sum_{j=1}^\infty \lambda_1(E_j).
     \]
  3. On a measurable rectangle \(A \times B\),
     \[
     \lambda_1(A \times B) = \int_S \nu(B)\mathbf{1}_A(s) \, d\mu(s) = \nu(B)\mu(A) = \mu(A)\nu(B).
     \]

- *Step 1c: Uniqueness and equality of the two slice integrals in the finite case.*
  Since \(\lambda_1(S \times T) = \mu(S)\nu(T) < \infty\), any measure on \(\mathcal{S} \otimes \mathcal{T}\) agreeing with \(\lambda_1\) on the \(\pi\)-system of measurable rectangles must equal \(\lambda_1\) on all of \(\mathcal{S} \otimes \mathcal{T}\) by finite-measure uniqueness (Theorem 4.4).
  Now repeat Steps 1a–1b in the opposite order of coordinates: for every \(E \in \mathcal{S} \otimes \mathcal{T}\), \(t \mapsto \mu(E^t)\) is \(\mathcal{T}\)-measurable, and \(\lambda_2(E) := \int_T \mu(E^t) \, d\nu(t)\) is a finite measure on \(\mathcal{S} \otimes \mathcal{T}\) satisfying \(\lambda_2(A \times B) = \mu(A)\nu(B)\) on all measurable rectangles. By uniqueness, \(\lambda_1(E) = \lambda_2(E)\) for every \(E \in \mathcal{S} \otimes \mathcal{T}\)! This proves Theorem 9.2 and (9.1) when \(\mu\) and \(\nu\) are finite.

*Step 2. Construct the \(\sigma\)-finite product from finite pieces.*
Now suppose \((S, \mathcal{S}, \mu)\) and \((T, \mathcal{T}, \nu)\) are \(\sigma\)-finite.

- *Step 2a: Disjoint finite-measure partitions.* By \(\sigma\)-finiteness, \(S = \bigcup_{i=1}^\infty \tilde{S}_i\) with \(\mu(\tilde{S}_i) < \infty\). Making them disjoint via \(S_1 = \tilde{S}_1\) and \(S_i = \tilde{S}_i \setminus \bigcup_{k=1}^{i-1} \tilde{S}_k\) gives a countable measurable partition \(S = \bigsqcup_{i \ge 1} S_i\) with \(\mu(S_i) < \infty\), and similarly \(T = \bigsqcup_{j \ge 1} T_j\) with \(\nu(T_j) < \infty\).
  #+caption: Figure 9.3: Measurable Rectangle in Product Space

#+end_example
  On each piece \(S_i\), use the trace \(\sigma\)-algebra \(\mathcal{S}_i = \{A \cap S_i : A \in \mathcal{S}\}\) and the restricted finite measure \(\mu_i = \mu|_{S_i}\), and similarly \((T_j, \mathcal{T}_j, \nu_j)\). By Step 1, we have a finite product measure \(\mu_i \otimes \nu_j\) on \(\mathcal{S}_i \otimes \mathcal{T}_j\).
  For any \(E \in \mathcal{S} \otimes \mathcal{T}\), its intersection \(E \cap (S_i \times T_j)\) belongs to \(\mathcal{S}_i \otimes \mathcal{T}_j\) (the collection of \(E \subseteq S \times T\) with \(E \cap (S_i \times T_j) \in \mathcal{S}_i \otimes \mathcal{T}_j\) contains all measurable rectangles \((A \times B) \cap (S_i \times T_j) = (A \cap S_i) \times (B \cap T_j)\) and is preserved by \(\sigma\)-algebra operations).
  Define
  \[
  (\mu \otimes \nu)(E) := \sum_{i=1}^\infty \sum_{j=1}^\infty (\mu_i \otimes \nu_j)\big(E \cap (S_i \times T_j)\big).
  \]
  Since each summand is a measure in \(E\) and a countable sum of measures is a measure (by rearranging nonnegative double series), \(\mu \otimes \nu\) is a measure on \(\mathcal{S} \otimes \mathcal{T}\). On a rectangle \(A \times B\), countable additivity of \(\mu\) and \(\nu\) gives
  \[
  (\mu \otimes \nu)(A \times B) = \sum_{i=1}^\infty \sum_{j=1}^\infty \mu(A \cap S_i)\nu(B \cap T_j) = \left(\sum_{i=1}^\infty \mu(A \cap S_i)\right)\left(\sum_{j=1}^\infty \nu(B \cap T_j)\right) = \mu(A)\nu(B),
  \]
  even if one factor is \(0\) and the other is \(\infty\) (with \(0 \cdot \infty = 0\)).

- *Step 2b: Verify (9.1) in the \(\sigma\)-finite case.* For fixed \(i, j\), Step 1 shows that \(s \mapsto \nu(E_s \cap T_j)\) is \(\mathcal{S}_i\)-measurable on \(S_i\). Extend it by \(0\) outside \(S_i\), i.e., consider the function \(s \mapsto \mathbf{1}_{S_i}(s)\nu(E_s \cap T_j)\) on all of \(S\). Why is this extended function \(\mathcal{S}\)-measurable on \(S\)? For any Borel set \(B \subseteq [0, \infty]\), its preimage under \(\mathbf{1}_{S_i}(\cdot)\nu(E_\cdot \cap T_j)\) is either a measurable subset of \(S_i\) (if \(0 \notin B\)) or the union of that measurable subset with \(S \setminus S_i \in \mathcal{S}\) (if \(0 \in B\)).
  Now, for any fixed \(s \in S\), exactly one of the partition sets \(S_i\) contains \(s\), and \(T = \bigsqcup_{j=1}^\infty T_j\) partitions \(T\), so countable additivity of \(\nu\) gives
  \[
  \nu(E_s) = \sum_{j=1}^\infty \nu(E_s \cap T_j) = \sum_{i=1}^\infty \sum_{j=1}^\infty \mathbf{1}_{S_i}(s)\nu(E_s \cap T_j).
  \]
  Thus \(s \mapsto \nu(E_s)\) is a countable sum of nonnegative \(\mathcal{S}\)-measurable functions, hence \(\mathcal{S}\)-measurable! Applying the Monotone Convergence Theorem to the finite partial sums of this series, we can integrate term by term:
  \[
  \int_S \nu(E_s) \, d\mu(s) = \sum_{i=1}^\infty \sum_{j=1}^\infty \int_{S_i} \nu(E_s \cap T_j) \, d\mu_i(s) = \sum_{i=1}^\infty \sum_{j=1}^\infty (\mu_i \otimes \nu_j)\big(E \cap (S_i \times T_j)\big) = (\mu \otimes \nu)(E),
  \]
  where the middle equality is Step 1 applied to the cell \(S_i \times T_j\). Exchanging the roles of \(S\) and \(T\) proves the second equality in (9.1).

- *Step 2c: Global uniqueness.* Any competing measure \(\tilde{\lambda}\) on \(\mathcal{S} \otimes \mathcal{T}\) with \(\tilde{\lambda}(A \times B) = \mu(A)\nu(B)\) agrees with \(\mu_i \otimes \nu_j\) on rectangles inside each finite cell \(S_i \times T_j\) (which has finite measure \(\mu(S_i)\nu(T_j) < \infty\)), hence agrees with \(\mu_i \otimes \nu_j\) on all product-measurable subsets of \(S_i \times T_j\) by finite-measure uniqueness. Summing over the disjoint cells \(S_i \times T_j\) proves \(\tilde{\lambda}(E) = (\mu \otimes \nu)(E)\) for every \(E \in \mathcal{S} \otimes \mathcal{T}\). \(\blacksquare\)

* Tonelli and Fubini

** Intuition

Once Theorem 9.2 has established the slice formula (9.1) for *sets* (which is the case of indicator functions \(f = \mathbf{1}_E\)), upgrading from indicator functions to general functions follows the standard four-step "measure-theory staircase" from Chapter 6:
\[
\text{Indicators } \mathbf{1}_E \xrightarrow{\text{linearity}} \text{Nonnegative simple } s \xrightarrow{\text{MCT}} \text{Nonnegative } f \ge 0 \text{ (Tonelli)} \xrightarrow{f = f^+ - f^-} \text{Integrable signed } f \text{ (Fubini)}.
\]
Pay special attention to the *workflow* between Tonelli and Fubini in practice:
- *Tonelli's theorem* applies to any *nonnegative* product-measurable function \(f \ge 0\). You do *not* need to know in advance whether the integral is finite! In fact, computing one of the iterated integrals \(\int_S (\int_T |f| \, d\nu) \, d\mu\) by Tonelli is the standard way to *check* whether \(\int_{S \times T} |f| \, d(\mu \otimes \nu) < \infty\).
- *Fubini's theorem* applies to a *signed* product-measurable function \(f : S \times T \to \mathbb{R}\), *after* you know that \(\int_{S \times T} |f| \, d(\mu \otimes \nu) < \infty\). Also notice a subtle point in Fubini's statement: even when \(\int_{S \times T} |f| \, d(\mu \otimes \nu) < \infty\), the inner integral \(\int_T |f(s, t)| \, d\nu(t)\) is only guaranteed to be finite for *\(\mu\)-almost every* \(s \in S\) (just as in Example 9.3, where the slice at \(s = 0\) had infinite measure!). At that null set of exceptional \(s\)'s, \(\int_T f(s, t) \, d\nu(t)\) might be undefined (\(\infty - \infty\)), so we set the section integral to \(0\) on the null exceptional set before doing the outer integral.

** Tonelli's Theorem (Nonnegative Functions)

#+begin_quote
*Theorem 9.4 (Tonelli).* Under the \(\sigma\)-finiteness assumptions of Theorem 9.2, if \(f : S \times T \to [0, \infty]\) is product measurable (\(\mathcal{S} \otimes \mathcal{T}\)-measurable), then its section integrals
\[
s \mapsto \int_T f(s, t) \, d\nu(t) \qquad \text{and} \qquad t \mapsto \int_S f(s, t) \, d\mu(s)
\]
are measurable (with respect to \(\mathcal{S}\) and \(\mathcal{T}\) respectively), and
\[
\int_S \left( \int_T f(s, t) \, d\nu(t) \right) d\mu(s) = \int_{S \times T} f \, d(\mu \otimes \nu) = \int_T \left( \int_S f(s, t) \, d\mu(s) \right) d\nu(t). \tag{9.2}
\]
The common value lies in \([0, \infty]\) and may be infinite.
#+end_quote

** Proof of Theorem 9.4 (unpacked step by step)

1. *Indicators:* For \(f = \mathbf{1}_E\) with \(E \in \mathcal{S} \otimes \mathcal{T}\), we have \(\mathbf{1}_E(s, t) = \mathbf{1}_{E_s}(t) = \mathbf{1}_{E^t}(s)\), so \(\int_T \mathbf{1}_E(s, t) \, d\nu(t) = \nu(E_s)\) and \(\int_S \mathbf{1}_E(s, t) \, d\mu(s) = \mu(E^t)\). Thus the statement for indicators is literally (9.1) from Theorem 9.2!
2. *Nonnegative simple functions:* If \(f = \sum_{k=1}^m c_k \mathbf{1}_{E_k}\) with \(c_k \ge 0\) and \(E_k \in \mathcal{S} \otimes \mathcal{T}\), linearity of the integral (Proposition 6.7) and the fact that linear combinations of measurable functions are measurable immediately give the statement for \(f\).
3. *General nonnegative measurable functions:* Let \(f : S \times T \to [0, \infty]\) be \(\mathcal{S} \otimes \mathcal{T}\)-measurable. By Lemma 6.2, choose an increasing sequence of nonnegative product-measurable simple functions \(0 \le f_n \uparrow f\) pointwise on \(S \times T\).
   - For each fixed \(s \in S\), the section functions \((f_n)_s\) are nonnegative \(\mathcal{T}\)-measurable functions increasing pointwise on \(T\) to \(f_s\). By the Monotone Convergence Theorem (Theorem 6.6) on \((T, \mathcal{T}, \nu)\),
     \[
     \int_T f_n(s, t) \, d\nu(t) \uparrow \int_T f(s, t) \, d\nu(t) \qquad \text{as } n \to \infty.
     \]
   - Since each \(s \mapsto \int_T f_n(s, t) \, d\nu(t)\) is \(\mathcal{S}\)-measurable (by Step 2), its pointwise limit \(s \mapsto \int_T f(s, t) \, d\nu(t)\) is \(\mathcal{S}\)-measurable (Proposition 3.4).
   - Now apply the Monotone Convergence Theorem a second time, both to the outer integral on \((S, \mathcal{S}, \mu)\) and to the product integral on \((S \times T, \mathcal{S} \otimes \mathcal{T}, \mu \otimes \nu)\):
     \[
     \int_S \left( \int_T f(s, t) \, d\nu(t) \right) d\mu(s) = \lim_{n \to \infty} \int_S \left( \int_T f_n(s, t) \, d\nu(t) \right) d\mu(s) = \lim_{n \to \infty} \int_{S \times T} f_n \, d(\mu \otimes \nu) = \int_{S \times T} f \, d(\mu \otimes \nu).
     \]
   By symmetry between \(S\) and \(T\), the second equality in (9.2) holds by the exact same argument. \(\blacksquare\)

** Fubini's Theorem (Integrable Signed Functions)

#+begin_quote
*Theorem 9.5 (Fubini).* Under the \(\sigma\)-finiteness assumptions of Theorem 9.2, suppose that \(f : S \times T \to \mathbb{R}\) is product measurable and integrable on the product space:
\[
\int_{S \times T} |f| \, d(\mu \otimes \nu) < \infty.
\]
Then its sections \(f_s\) are \(\nu\)-integrable for \(\mu\)-almost every \(s \in S\), and its sections \(f^t\) are \(\mu\)-integrable for \(\nu\)-almost every \(t \in T\). Set each section integral to zero at the exceptional coordinates (where the section is not integrable). The resulting functions
\[
s \mapsto \int_T f(s, t) \, d\nu(t) \qquad \text{and} \qquad t \mapsto \int_S f(s, t) \, d\mu(s)
\]
are integrable (on \(S\) and \(T\) respectively), and the equalities in (9.2) hold for \(f\):
\[
\int_S \left( \int_T f(s, t) \, d\nu(t) \right) d\mu(s) = \int_{S \times T} f \, d(\mu \otimes \nu) = \int_T \left( \int_S f(s, t) \, d\mu(s) \right) d\nu(t).
\]
#+end_quote

** Proof of Theorem 9.5 (unpacked step by step)

1. *Find the exceptional null set of slices using Tonelli on \(|f|\):* Since \(|f| \ge 0\) is product measurable, Tonelli's theorem (Theorem 9.4) shows that the function
   \[
   H(s) := \int_T |f(s, t)| \, d\nu(t)
   \]
   is \(\mathcal{S}\)-measurable and satisfies
   \[
   \int_S H(s) \, d\mu(s) = \int_{S \times T} |f| \, d(\mu \otimes \nu) < \infty.
   \]
   Because \(H \ge 0\) has a finite integral over \(S\), the measurable set where it is infinite,
   \[
   N := \{s \in S : H(s) = \infty\},
   \]
   must be a \(\mu\)-null set (\(\mu(N) = 0\)). For every \(s \notin N\), we have \(\int_T |f_s| \, d\nu = H(s) < \infty\), which means the section \(f_s\) is \(\nu\)-integrable!
2. *Split into positive and negative parts \(f = f^+ - f^-\):* Both \(f^+\) and \(f^-\) are nonnegative product-measurable functions bounded by \(|f|\). By Tonelli's theorem applied separately to \(f^+\) and \(f^-\), their section integrals
   \[
   I^+(s) := \int_T f^+(s, t) \, d\nu(t), \qquad I^-(s) := \int_T f^-(s, t) \, d\nu(t)
   \]
   are \(\mathcal{S}\)-measurable and satisfy \(0 \le I^\pm(s) \le H(s) < \infty\) for all \(s \notin N\).
3. *Zero out on \(N\) and subtract safely:* Modify both \(I^+\) and \(I^-\) by setting them to \(0\) on the measurable null set \(N\) (i.e., replace \(I^\pm\) by \(I^\pm \mathbf{1}_{N^c}\)). Since both modified functions are finite everywhere on \(S\), their difference
   \[
   I(s) := I^+(s)\mathbf{1}_{N^c}(s) - I^-(s)\mathbf{1}_{N^c}(s) = \begin{cases} \int_T f(s, t) \, d\nu(t) & \text{if } s \notin N, \\ 0 & \text{if } s \in N \end{cases}
   \]
   is well-defined everywhere (never \(\infty - \infty\)!) and \(\mathcal{S}\)-measurable.
4. *Integrability and outer integral equality:* Since \(|I(s)| \le H(s)\mathbf{1}_{N^c}(s)\) and \(\int_S H \, d\mu < \infty\), the function \(I\) is \(\mu\)-integrable. Moreover, changing \(I^+\) and \(I^-\) on the \(\mu\)-null set \(N\) does not change their integrals over \(S\) (Lemma 6.8). Subtracting the two finite Tonelli identities for \(f^+\) and \(f^-\) yields
   \[
   \int_S I(s) \, d\mu(s) = \int_S I^+(s) \, d\mu(s) - \int_S I^-(s) \, d\mu(s) = \int_{S \times T} f^+ \, d(\mu \otimes \nu) - \int_{S \times T} f^- \, d(\mu \otimes \nu) = \int_{S \times T} f \, d(\mu \otimes \nu).
   \]
   Exchanging the roles of \(S\) and \(T\) gives the second equality and its corresponding \(\nu\)-null exceptional set. \(\blacksquare\)

** The Golden Workflow: Tonelli First, Fubini Second!

Whenever you are given a signed function \(f(s, t)\) on a product of \(\sigma\)-finite spaces and want to apply Fubini's theorem to swap \(\int_S \int_T f \, d\nu \, d\mu = \int_T \int_S f \, d\mu \, d\nu\), follow this two-step checklist:
1. *Step 1 (Tonelli on \(|f|\)):* Take the absolute value \(|f(s, t)| \ge 0\) and compute *either* iterated integral \(\int_S \big(\int_T |f(s, t)| \, d\nu(t)\big) d\mu(s)\) or \(\int_T \big(\int_S |f(s, t)| \, d\mu(s)\big) d\nu(t)\) (whichever direction is easier!). Because \(|f| \ge 0\), Tonelli's theorem guarantees that this iterated integral equals \(\int_{S \times T} |f| \, d(\mu \otimes \nu)\).
2. *Step 2 (Fubini on \(f\)):* If the number you got in Step 1 is *finite* (\(< \infty\)), then \(f \in L^1(\mu \otimes \nu)\), so Fubini's theorem applies and you are 100% authorized to swap the integrals of \(f\)! (If the number in Step 1 is \(\infty\), stop: Fubini does *not* apply!)

** Examples from the notes

#+begin_quote
*Example 9.6 (Double series and a failed exchange).* With counting measure in both coordinates on \(\mathbb{N} \times \mathbb{N}\), Tonelli says that a nonnegative double series (\(a_{mn} \ge 0\)) can be summed in either order:
\[
\sum_{m=1}^\infty \sum_{n=1}^\infty a_{mn} = \sum_{n=1}^\infty \sum_{m=1}^\infty a_{mn} \in [0, \infty].
\]
Fubini gives the same conclusion for an *absolutely summable* signed array (\(\sum_{m,n} |a_{mn}| < \infty\)). The absolute summability cannot simply be omitted! On \(\mathbb{N}^2\), put
\[
a_{mn} = \begin{cases} 1 & \text{if } m = n, \\ -1 & \text{if } n = m + 1, \\ 0 & \text{otherwise.} \end{cases}
\]
Let us look at the infinite matrix \((a_{mn})_{m,n \ge 1}\):
\[
\begin{pmatrix}
a_{11} & a_{12} & a_{13} & a_{14} & \cdots \\
a_{21} & a_{22} & a_{23} & a_{24} & \cdots \\
a_{31} & a_{32} & a_{33} & a_{34} & \cdots \\
\vdots & \vdots & \vdots & \vdots & \ddots
\end{pmatrix}
=
\begin{pmatrix}
1 & -1 & 0 & 0 & \cdots \\
0 & 1 & -1 & 0 & \cdots \\
0 & 0 & 1 & -1 & \cdots \\
\vdots & \vdots & \vdots & \vdots & \ddots
\end{pmatrix}
\begin{array}{l}
\to \text{Row 1 sum} = 1 - 1 = 0 \\
\to \text{Row 2 sum} = 1 - 1 = 0 \\
\to \text{Row 3 sum} = 1 - 1 = 0 \\
\end{array}
\]
- *Summing rows first:* Each row \(m\) has one \(+1\) and one \(-1\), so \(\sum_{n=1}^\infty a_{mn} = 0\). Therefore \(\sum_{m=1}^\infty \left(\sum_{n=1}^\infty a_{mn}\right) = \sum_{m=1}^\infty 0 = 0\).
- *Summing columns first:* Column \(n = 1\) has a single \(+1\) at the top, so \(\sum_{m=1}^\infty a_{m1} = 1\). Every other column \(n \ge 2\) has one \(-1\) and one \(+1\), so \(\sum_{m=1}^\infty a_{mn} = 0\). Therefore \(\sum_{n=1}^\infty \left(\sum_{m=1}^\infty a_{mn}\right) = 1 + 0 + 0 + \cdots = 1 \ne 0\)!

Why did Fubini not prevent this? Because the positive and negative parts both have infinite product integral: \(\sum_{m,n} a_{mn}^+ = 1 + 1 + 1 + \cdots = \infty\) and \(\sum_{m,n} a_{mn}^- = 1 + 1 + 1 + \cdots = \infty\) (so \(\sum_{m,n} |a_{mn}| = \infty\)). There is no signed product integral to which Fubini could apply.
#+end_quote

#+begin_quote
*Example 9.7 (Integrating over the triangle).* For the triangle \(E = \{(s, t) \in [0, 1]^2 : s < t\}\) in Example 9.1, consider the nonnegative function \(f(s, t) = t\,\mathbf{1}_{\{s < t\}}\). Since \(f \ge 0\) is product measurable, Tonelli's theorem gives
\[
\int_0^1 \left( \int_s^1 t \, dt \right) ds = \int_0^1 \left( \int_0^t t \, ds \right) dt = \int_0^1 t^2 \, dt = \frac{1}{3}.
\]
The integration limits record the same set \(E\) in two different ways:
- Fixing \(s \in [0, 1]\) first, \(t\) runs over the vertical section \(E_s = (s, 1]\), giving \(\int_0^1 \int_s^1 t \, dt \, ds = \int_0^1 \frac{1}{2}(1 - s^2) \, ds = \frac{1}{2}(1 - \frac{1}{3}) = \frac{1}{3}\).
- Fixing \(t \in [0, 1]\) first, \(s\) runs over the horizontal section \(E^t = [0, t)\), giving \(\int_0^1 \int_0^t t \, ds \, dt = \int_0^1 t^2 \, dt = \frac{1}{3}\).
Drawing the horizontal and vertical sections of the integration region is almost always the simplest way to find the new limits of integration when swapping integrals!
#+end_quote

#+begin_quote
*Example 9.8 (Why \(\sigma\)-finiteness appears).* On \(([0, 1], \mathcal{B}([0, 1]))\), let \(\mu = \lambda\) be Lebesgue measure and let \(\nu = \#\) be counting measure on Borel sets: a finite set has its number of points as measure, and an infinite set has measure \(\infty\). This counting measure on \([0, 1]\) is *not \(\sigma\)-finite*, since a countable union of finite sets is countable and cannot cover the uncountable interval \([0, 1]\).

Consider the diagonal \(\Delta = \{(s, t) \in [0, 1]^2 : s = t\}\). Since \(\Delta\) is closed in \([0, 1]^2\), it belongs to \(\mathcal{B}([0, 1]^2) = \mathcal{B}([0, 1]) \otimes \mathcal{B}([0, 1])\), so its indicator \(f(s, t) = \mathbf{1}_{\{s = t\}}\) is a nonnegative product-measurable function. Yet:
\[
\int_{[0, 1]} \left( \int_{[0, 1]} \mathbf{1}_{\{s = t\}}(s, t) \, d\#(t) \right) d\lambda(s) = \int_{[0, 1]} \#(\{s\}) \, d\lambda(s) = \int_{[0, 1]} 1 \, d\lambda(s) = 1,
\]
whereas
\[
\int_{[0, 1]} \left( \int_{[0, 1]} \mathbf{1}_{\{s = t\}}(s, t) \, d\lambda(s) \right) d\#(t) = \int_{[0, 1]} \lambda(\{t\}) \, d\#(t) = \int_{[0, 1]} 0 \, d\#(t) = 0!
\]
Each inner counting integral equals \(1\) (a singleton has \(1\) point), whereas each inner Lebesgue integral equals \(0\) (a singleton has length \(0\)). Thus even for a bounded nonnegative indicator function, equality of the two iterated integrals fails when \(\sigma\)-finiteness is dropped! Notice that we have not contradicted Theorem 9.4: we simply have a non-\(\sigma\)-finite factor \(\#\), outside the theorem's hypotheses.
#+end_quote

* More factors and completed measures

** Intuition

Three natural questions arise once we have product measures for two spaces:
1. *What about three or more spaces \(S_1 \times S_2 \times \cdots \times S_n\)?* Does grouping \(((S_1 \times S_2) \times S_3)\) vs. \((S_1 \times (S_2 \times S_3))\) change the \(\sigma\)-algebra or the product measure? No—uniqueness on rectangles shows that finite products are completely associative, so we can write \(\mu_1 \otimes \cdots \otimes \mu_n\) without parentheses and swap any of the \(n\) integrals via Tonelli/Fubini.
2. *If \((S, \mathcal{S}, \mu)\) and \((T, \mathcal{T}, \nu)\) are complete measure spaces (Chapter 5, Section 5.4), is their product \((S \times T, \mathcal{S} \otimes \mathcal{T}, \mu \otimes \nu)\) automatically complete?*
   *No!* Why not? Suppose \(A_0 \in \mathcal{S}\) is a non-empty \(\mu\)-null set (say \(A_0 = \{0\} \subseteq [0, 1]\) with Lebesgue measure), and let \(V \subseteq T = [0, 1]\) be a *non-measurable* subset of \(T\). Then the thin vertical strip \(A_0 \times V = \{0\} \times V\) sits inside the measurable rectangle \(\{0\} \times [0, 1]\), which has product measure \(0 \cdot 1 = 0\). So in the *completion* of the product space, \(\{0\} \times V\) is a subset of a null set and is therefore measurable! Yet \(\{0\} \times V\) *cannot* belong to the uncompleted product \(\sigma\)-algebra \(\mathcal{S} \otimes \mathcal{T}\), because by Section 9.1 every set in \(\mathcal{S} \otimes \mathcal{T}\) has measurable sections, whereas the section of \(\{0\} \times V\) at \(s = 0\) is \(V \notin \mathcal{T}\)!

   #+caption: Figure 9.5: Vitali Set Slice in Product Space Completion
#+begin_example
T = [0,1] ^
        1 |       |   |
          |   #   |   |   # = {0} x V  (where V is Vitali set)
          |   #   |   |   Inside null rectangle {0} x [0,1].
        0 +---+---+---+----> S = [0,1]
          0   s   1
In product sigma-algebra, {0} x V is not measurable!
In completion, {0} x V is measurable with measure 0.
#+end_example

3. *Can we still use Fubini's theorem if \(f\) is only measurable with respect to the completion of \(\mu \otimes \nu\)?* Yes—provided \(\mu\) and \(\nu\) are complete and \(\sigma\)-finite! The only twist is that the sections \(f_s\) are now measurable for *\(\mu\)-almost every* \(s\) (rather than *every* \(s\), because a bad slice like \(V\) at \(s = 0\) can happen on a \(\mu\)-null set!).

** Associativity for Three or More Factors

For three \(\sigma\)-finite measure spaces \((S_i, \mathcal{S}_i, \mu_i)\) (\(i = 1, 2, 3\)), consider the two ways of forming the triple product:
\[
(\mu_1 \otimes \mu_2) \otimes \mu_3 \quad \text{on } (\mathcal{S}_1 \otimes \mathcal{S}_2) \otimes \mathcal{S}_3, \qquad \text{and} \qquad \mu_1 \otimes (\mu_2 \otimes \mu_3) \quad \text{on } \mathcal{S}_1 \otimes (\mathcal{S}_2 \otimes \mathcal{S}_3).
\]
Under the natural identification of \(((s_1, s_2), s_3)\) with \((s_1, (s_2, s_3))\) and \((s_1, s_2, s_3)\):
- Both \(\sigma\)-algebras agree, because both are generated by the three-dimensional measurable rectangles \(A_1 \times A_2 \times A_3\) (\(A_i \in \mathcal{S}_i\))—equivalently, both are the smallest \(\sigma\)-algebra making all three coordinate maps measurable.
- Both product measures assign the exact same value \(\mu_1(A_1)\mu_2(A_2)\mu_3(A_3)\) to every rectangle \(A_1 \times A_2 \times A_3\). By uniqueness on finite-measure rectangle exhaustions (Theorem 4.4), the two measures agree on every set in \(\mathcal{S}_1 \otimes \mathcal{S}_2 \otimes \mathcal{S}_3\).

Repeating this argument inductively defines finite products \(\bigotimes_{i=1}^n \mu_i\) on \(\bigotimes_{i=1}^n \mathcal{S}_i\) for any \(n \ge 2\) without any dependence on parentheses, and Tonelli and Fubini apply successively to swap any order of the \(n\) integrals. (In particular, \(n\)-dimensional Borel–Lebesgue measure on \(\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^n) = \bigotimes_{i=1}^n \mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R})\) is the \(n\)-fold product \(\lambda^{\otimes n}\) of one-dimensional Lebesgue measure!)

** Why Product Measures Are Not Automatically Complete, and the Vitali Set Construction

As explained in the intuition above, take the *completed* Lebesgue measure space on \([0, 1]\) in both coordinates, and let \(V \subseteq [0, 1]\) be a subset that is *not* Lebesgue measurable. Then:
- The set \(E = \{0\} \times V\) is a subset of the product-measurable rectangle \(\{0\} \times [0, 1]\), which has product measure \(\lambda(\{0\})\lambda([0, 1]) = 0 \cdot 1 = 0\).
- Therefore, \(E = \{0\} \times V\) is measurable in the *completion* of the product measure (with measure \(0\)).
- However, \(E = \{0\} \times V\) is *not* measurable in the uncompleted product \(\sigma\)-algebra, because its section at \(s = 0\) is \(E_0 = V\), which is not Lebesgue measurable, whereas every section of a product-measurable set must be measurable (Section 9.1)!

*How do we know a non-Lebesgue-measurable set \(V \subseteq [0, 1]\) actually exists?*
Here is Giuseppe Vitali's classic 1905 construction (included in the lecture notes in case it was not covered in a prerequisite course):
1. *Rational-difference equivalence classes:* On \([0, 1]\), define a relation \(x \sim y\) by
   \[
   x \sim y \iff x - y \in \mathbb{Q}.
   \]
   This is an equivalence relation on \([0, 1]\): \(x - x = 0 \in \mathbb{Q}\) (reflexive); \(x - y \in \mathbb{Q} \implies y - x = -(x - y) \in \mathbb{Q}\) (symmetric); and \(x - y \in \mathbb{Q},\; y - z \in \mathbb{Q} \implies x - z = (x - y) + (y - z) \in \mathbb{Q}\) (transitive). It therefore partitions \([0, 1]\) into disjoint equivalence classes.
2. *Select one representative per class:* Using the Axiom of Choice, select *one* point from each equivalence class in \([0, 1]\), and let \(V \subseteq [0, 1]\) be the set of selected points.
3. *Countable rational translates of \(V\):* Consider the rational translates
   \[
   V + q := \{v + q : v \in V\} \qquad \text{for } q \in \mathbb{Q} \cap [-1, 1].
   \]
   We check three geometric properties of the family \(\{V + q : q \in \mathbb{Q} \cap [-1, 1]\}\):
   - *Pairwise disjoint:* Suppose \((V + q) \cap (V + r) \ne \emptyset\) for some \(q, r \in \mathbb{Q} \cap [-1, 1]\). Then there are \(v, w \in V\) with \(v + q = w + r\). Rearranging gives \(v - w = r - q \in \mathbb{Q}\), so \(v \sim w\). Since \(V\) contains only *one* element from each \(\sim\)-equivalence class, this forces \(v = w\), and then \(v + q = v + r\) forces \(q = r\)!
   - *Covers \([0, 1]\):* Every \(x \in [0, 1]\) belongs to the equivalence class of some representative \(v \in V \subseteq [0, 1]\). Then \(q := x - v\) is rational and lies in \([-1, 1]\) (since \(0 \le x, v \le 1\)), so \(x = v + q \in V + q\).
   - *Contained in \([-1, 2]\):* Since \(V \subseteq [0, 1]\) and \(q \in [-1, 1]\), every point \(v + q\) lies in \([0 - 1, 1 + 1] = [-1, 2]\).
   Summarizing the inclusions:
   \[
   [0, 1] \subseteq \bigsqcup_{q \in \mathbb{Q} \cap [-1, 1]} (V + q) \subseteq [-1, 2].
   \]
4. *Contradiction with countable additivity and translation invariance:* Suppose for contradiction that \(V\) were Lebesgue measurable, with measure \(a = \lambda(V) \in [0, 1]\). By translation invariance of Lebesgue measure, \(\lambda(V + q) = \lambda(V) = a\) for every \(q\). Since \(\mathbb{Q} \cap [-1, 1]\) is countably infinite, countable additivity of \(\lambda\) and monotonicity give
   \[
   1 = \lambda([0, 1]) \le \sum_{q \in \mathbb{Q} \cap [-1, 1]} \lambda(V + q) = \sum_{q \in \mathbb{Q} \cap [-1, 1]} a \le \lambda([-1, 2]) = 3.
   \]
   - If \(a = 0\), the middle sum is \(0\), contradicting \(1 \le 0\).
   - If \(a > 0\), the middle sum is \(a + a + a + \cdots = \infty\), contradicting \(\infty \le 3\).
   Both cases are impossible! Therefore \(V\) cannot be Lebesgue measurable. \(\blacksquare\)

** The Completed Version of Fubini's Theorem

Even though \(\mu \otimes \nu\) is not automatically complete, we can still prove a completed version of Fubini's theorem!

#+begin_quote
*Proposition (Completed Fubini).* Suppose \((S, \mathcal{S}, \mu)\) and \((T, \mathcal{T}, \nu)\) are *complete* and \(\sigma\)-finite measure spaces, and let \(f : S \times T \to \mathbb{R}\) be measurable and integrable with respect to the *completion* \(\overline{\mu \otimes \nu}\) of \(\mu \otimes \nu\). Then:
1. For \(\mu\)-almost every \(s \in S\), the section \(f_s\) is \(\mathcal{T}\)-measurable and \(\nu\)-integrable.
2. Setting the section integral \(\int_T f(s, t) \, d\nu(t)\) to \(0\) on the \(\mu\)-null set of exceptional \(s\), the resulting function of \(s\) is \(\mathcal{S}\)-measurable and \(\mu\)-integrable, and
   \[
   \int_S \left( \int_T f(s, t) \, d\nu(t) \right) d\mu(s) = \int_{S \times T} f \, d(\overline{\mu \otimes \nu}) = \int_T \left( \int_S f(s, t) \, d\mu(s) \right) d\nu(t).
   \]
#+end_quote

*Proof (unpacked step by step).*
- *Step 1: Replace \(f\) by a genuinely product-measurable function \(g\) outside a product-measurable null set \(N\).*
  Using positive and negative parts and Lemma 6.2, choose completed-measurable simple functions \(s_n \to f\) pointwise on \(S \times T\). Each \(s_n\) has finitely many level sets, which are measurable in the completion of \(\mathcal{S} \otimes \mathcal{T}\). By Proposition 5.14 (characterization of the completion), each such level set \(A\) differs from a set \(B \in \mathcal{S} \otimes \mathcal{T}\) by a subset of a null set in \(\mathcal{S} \otimes \mathcal{T}\).
  Let \(N\) be the union of these product-measurable null sets over all the finitely many level sets of \(s_n\) and over all \(n \in \mathbb{N}\). Since a countable union of product-measurable null sets is a product-measurable null set, we have \(N \in \mathcal{S} \otimes \mathcal{T}\) and \((\mu \otimes \nu)(N) = 0\).
  Now replace each level set in each \(s_n\) by its \(\mathcal{S} \otimes \mathcal{T}\)-counterpart, and set all the resulting simple functions to \(0\) on \(N\). These modified simple functions are genuinely \(\mathcal{S} \otimes \mathcal{T}\)-measurable and converge at every point of \(S \times T\) to the function
  \[
  g(s, t) := \begin{cases} f(s, t) & \text{if } (s, t) \notin N, \\ 0 & \text{if } (s, t) \in N. \end{cases}
  \]
  Thus \(g\) is \(\mathcal{S} \otimes \mathcal{T}\)-measurable, and since \(f = g\) outside the null set \(N\), \(g\) has the exact same absolute and signed integrals with respect to \(\mu \otimes \nu\) as \(f\) has with respect to \(\overline{\mu \otimes \nu}\).
- *Step 2: Control the slices of the null set \(N\) via Tonelli.*
  Since \(N \in \mathcal{S} \otimes \mathcal{T}\) has \((\mu \otimes \nu)(N) = 0\), Tonelli's theorem (or (9.1)) applied to \(\mathbf{1}_N\) gives
  \[
  \int_S \nu(N_s) \, d\mu(s) = (\mu \otimes \nu)(N) = 0.
  \]
  By Lemma 6.8, the nonnegative measurable function \(s \mapsto \nu(N_s)\) vanishes \(\mu\)-almost everywhere: there is a measurable \(\mu\)-null set \(M_1 \in \mathcal{S}\) such that \(\nu(N_s) = 0\) for all \(s \notin M_1\).
- *Step 3: Apply uncompleted Fubini to \(g\) and use completeness of \(\nu\).*
  By Fubini's theorem (Theorem 9.5) applied to the product-measurable function \(g\), there is another measurable \(\mu\)-null set \(M_2 \in \mathcal{S}\) such that \(g_s\) is \(\nu\)-integrable for all \(s \notin M_2\).
  Now take any \(s \notin M_1 \cup M_2\) (the complement of a \(\mu\)-null set!). For this \(s\):
  - The section \(g_s\) is \(\mathcal{T}\)-measurable and \(\nu\)-integrable;
  - The section \(f_s\) differs from \(g_s\) only on the slice \(N_s\), which is a \(\mathcal{T}\)-measurable \(\nu\)-null set (\(\nu(N_s) = 0\)) because \(s \notin M_1\)!
  - Since \((T, \mathcal{T}, \nu)\) is *complete*, any function \(f_s\) that agrees with a \(\mathcal{T}\)-measurable function \(g_s\) outside a \(\nu\)-null set is itself \(\mathcal{T}\)-measurable and has the exact same \(\nu\)-integral:
    \[
    \int_T f(s, t) \, d\nu(t) = \int_T g(s, t) \, d\nu(t) \qquad \text{for all } s \notin M_1 \cup M_2.
    \]
  Setting the section-integral function of \(f\) to \(0\) on the \(\mu\)-null set \(M_1 \cup M_2\), it agrees everywhere off \(M_1 \cup M_2\) with the section-integral function of \(g\), hence is \(\mathcal{S}\)-measurable and \(\mu\)-integrable with the same integral over \(S\). Fubini's theorem for \(g\) then yields the first identity for \(f\), and the exact same argument in the other coordinate (using completeness of \(\mu\)) gives the second identity. Notice that the conclusion holds for *almost every* section, not every section! \(\blacksquare\)

** Historical remark (from the notes)

Guido Fubini's 1907 paper reduced a two-variable Lebesgue integral to successive one-variable integrals, allowing unbounded integrable functions [13, p. 608]. In 1909, Leonida Tonelli proved that for a nonnegative measurable function on a rectangle, finiteness of an iterated integral implies integrability over the rectangle and equality of the integration orders [14, pp. 246–247]. His stated purpose was an integration-by-parts formula in two variables; the nonnegative criterion was an auxiliary proposition. Both papers concerned planar Lebesgue integration. Our abstract product-measure statements include settings not formulated in these passages.

Keep the completion distinction visible; later path-space examples will make the underlying \(\sigma\)-algebra even more important.

** Exercise 1
Let \(E \subseteq \mathbb{R}^2\) be the region bounded by \(s \ge 0\), \(t \ge 0\), and \(s + 2t \le 2\), and let \(f(s, t) = s e^t\).
(a) Write down the sections \(E_s\) (for \(s \in [0, 2]\)) and \(E^t\) (for \(t \in [0, 1]\)).
(b) Compute \(\iint_E s e^t \, d(\lambda \otimes \lambda)(s, t)\) in *both* orders of integration and verify that they agree.

** Solution
(a) The inequalities defining \(E\) are \(0 \le s \le 2\) and \(0 \le t \le 1 - s/2\) (or equivalently \(0 \le t \le 1\) and \(0 \le s \le 2 - 2t\)). Thus:
- For fixed \(s \in [0, 2]\), the vertical section is \(E_s = [0, 1 - s/2]\).
- For fixed \(t \in [0, 1]\), the horizontal section is \(E^t = [0, 2 - 2t]\).

(b) Since \(f(s, t) = s e^t \mathbf{1}_E(s, t) \ge 0\) on \(E\), Tonelli's theorem (Theorem 9.4) guarantees that both iterated integrals equal the product integral.
- *Integrating \(t\) first, then \(s\):*
  \[
  \int_0^2 \left( \int_0^{1 - s/2} s e^t \, dt \right) ds = \int_0^2 s \big(e^{1 - s/2} - 1\big) \, ds.
  \]
  Using integration by parts \(\int_0^2 s e^{1 - s/2} \, ds = \big[-2s e^{1 - s/2}\big]_0^2 + \int_0^2 2e^{1 - s/2} \, ds = -4 + \big[-4e^{1 - s/2}\big]_0^2 = -4 + (-4 + 4e) = 4e - 8\) and \(\int_0^2 s \, ds = 2\), we obtain
  \[
  (4e - 8) - 2 = 4e - 10.
  \]
- *Integrating \(s\) first, then \(t\) (notice how much easier this order is!):*
  \[
  \int_0^1 \left( \int_0^{2 - 2t} s e^t \, ds \right) dt = \int_0^1 \frac{(2 - 2t)^2}{2} e^t \, dt = 2 \int_0^1 (1 - t)^2 e^t \, dt.
  \]
  Substitute \(u = 1 - t\) (or integrate by parts twice: \(\int (1-t)^2 e^t \, dt = (1-t)^2 e^t + 2(1-t)e^t + 2e^t\)):
  \[
  2 \Big[ \big((1-t)^2 + 2(1-t) + 2\big)e^t \Big]_0^1 = 2 \big( 2e^1 - (1 + 2 + 2)e^0 \big) = 4e - 10.
  \]
Both orders give \(4e - 10\).

** Exercise 2 (The Layer-Cake / Tail-Integral Formula)
Let \((\Omega, \mathcal{F}, \mu)\) be a \(\sigma\)-finite measure space and \(f : \Omega \to [0, \infty]\) be an \(\mathcal{F}\)-measurable function.
(a) Show that the set \(G := \{(\omega, t) \in \Omega \times [0, \infty) : 0 \le t < f(\omega)\}\) (the region strictly under the graph of \(f\)) belongs to \(\mathcal{F} \otimes \mathcal{B}([0, \infty))\).
(b) Apply Tonelli's theorem to \(\mathbf{1}_G\) on \(\Omega \times [0, \infty)\) (with \(\mu \otimes \lambda\)) to prove that
\[
\int_\Omega f(\omega) \, d\mu(\omega) = \int_0^\infty \mu(\{\omega \in \Omega : f(\omega) > t\}) \, dt,
\]
and more generally, for any \(0 < p < \infty\),
\[
\int_\Omega f(\omega)^p \, d\mu(\omega) = \int_0^\infty p t^{p-1} \mu(\{f > t\}) \, dt.
\]

** Solution
(a) Both \((\omega, t) \mapsto f(\omega)\) (composition of \(f\) with the first coordinate projection \(\pi_\Omega\)) and \((\omega, t) \mapsto t\) (the second coordinate projection \(\pi_{[0, \infty)}\)) are \(\mathcal{F} \otimes \mathcal{B}([0, \infty))\)-measurable. By Chapter 3, the set where one measurable function exceeds another, \(G = \{(\omega, t) : f(\omega) - t > 0\}\), is product measurable! (Alternatively, just as in Example 9.1, \(G = \bigcup_{q \in \mathbb{Q} \cap [0, \infty)} \big(\{f > q\} \times [0, q)\big)\), a countable union of measurable rectangles.)

(b) Both \((\Omega, \mathcal{F}, \mu)\) and \(([0, \infty), \mathcal{B}([0, \infty)), \lambda)\) are \(\sigma\)-finite, and \(\mathbf{1}_G \ge 0\) is product measurable. Let us compute the two sections of \(G\):
- For fixed \(\omega \in \Omega\), the vertical section is \(G_\omega = \{t \ge 0 : t < f(\omega)\} = [0, f(\omega))\), which has Lebesgue measure \(\lambda(G_\omega) = f(\omega)\).
- For fixed \(t \in [0, \infty)\), the horizontal section is \(G^t = \{\omega \in \Omega : f(\omega) > t\} = \{f > t\}\), which has \(\mu\)-measure \(\mu(\{f > t\})\).
By Tonelli's theorem (or Theorem 9.2, (9.1)),
\[
\int_\Omega f(\omega) \, d\mu(\omega) = \int_\Omega \lambda(G_\omega) \, d\mu(\omega) = (\mu \otimes \lambda)(G) = \int_0^\infty \mu(G^t) \, dt = \int_0^\infty \mu(\{f > t\}) \, dt.
\]
For the \(p\)-th power formula (\(0 < p < \infty\)), apply Tonelli's theorem to the nonnegative product-measurable function \(h(\omega, t) := p t^{p-1} \mathbf{1}_G(\omega, t)\):
- Integrating \(t\) first gives \(\int_0^{f(\omega)} p t^{p-1} \, dt = f(\omega)^p\), and then integrating over \(\omega \in \Omega\) gives \(\int_\Omega f(\omega)^p \, d\mu(\omega)\).
- Integrating \(\omega\) first gives \(p t^{p-1} \int_\Omega \mathbf{1}_{\{f > t\}}(\omega) \, d\mu(\omega) = p t^{p-1} \mu(\{f > t\})\), and then integrating over \(t \in (0, \infty)\) gives \(\int_0^\infty p t^{p-1} \mu(\{f > t\}) \, dt\).
By Tonelli's theorem, the two expressions are equal!

** Exercise 3 (Evaluating a Non-Elementary Integral via Fubini)
Prove that for \(0 < a < b\),
\[
\int_0^\infty \frac{e^{-ax} - e^{-bx}}{x} \, dx = \ln\left(\frac{b}{a}\right)
\]
by rewriting the integrand itself as a definite integral \(\frac{e^{-ax} - e^{-bx}}{x} = \int_a^b e^{-xt} \, dt\) and applying Tonelli/Fubini on \((0, \infty) \times [a, b]\).

** Solution
For each \(x > 0\), the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus gives
\[
\int_a^b e^{-xt} \, dt = \left[ -\frac{e^{-xt}}{x} \right]_{t=a}^{t=b} = \frac{e^{-ax} - e^{-bx}}{x}.
\]
Consider the function \(f(x, t) = e^{-xt}\) on \((0, \infty) \times [a, b]\) equipped with \(\lambda \otimes \lambda\). Since \(f\) is continuous on \((0, \infty) \times [a, b]\), it is Borel measurable (hence product measurable), and since \(f(x, t) = e^{-xt} > 0\) is strictly positive, Tonelli's theorem (Theorem 9.4) applies directly without even needing a separate absolute-value step:
\[
\int_0^\infty \frac{e^{-ax} - e^{-bx}}{x} \, dx = \int_0^\infty \left( \int_a^b e^{-xt} \, dt \right) dx = \int_a^b \left( \int_0^\infty e^{-xt} \, dx \right) dt.
\]
For each fixed \(t \in [a, b]\) (recall \(a > 0\)), the inner integral is \(\int_0^\infty e^{-xt} \, dx = \left[-\frac{e^{-xt}}{t}\right]_{x=0}^{x=\infty} = \frac{1}{t}\). Integrating this over \(t \in [a, b]\) yields
\[
\int_a^b \frac{1}{t} \, dt = \ln(b) - \ln(a) = \ln\left(\frac{b}{a}\right).
\]

** Exercise 4 (A Continuous Counterexample Where Fubini Fails)
On \((0, 1) \times (0, 1)\) with Lebesgue measure \(\lambda \otimes \lambda\), consider the rational function
\[
f(s, t) = \frac{s^2 - t^2}{(s^2 + t^2)^2}.
\]
(a) Compute the two iterated integrals \(\int_0^1 \left(\int_0^1 f(s, t) \, dt\right) ds\) and \(\int_0^1 \left(\int_0^1 f(s, t) \, ds\right) dt\).
(b) Why did the two answers disagree? Deduce whether \(f\) belongs to \(L^1((0, 1)^2, \lambda \otimes \lambda)\).

** Solution
(a) Notice the partial-derivative antiderivative: for Fixed \(s > 0\),
\[
\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\left( \frac{t}{s^2 + t^2} \right) = \frac{1(s^2 + t^2) - t(2t)}{(s^2 + t^2)^2} = \frac{s^2 - t^2}{(s^2 + t^2)^2} = f(s, t).
\]
Therefore, for every fixed \(s \in (0, 1)\), the inner \(t\)-integral is
\[
\int_0^1 f(s, t) \, dt = \left[ \frac{t}{s^2 + t^2} \right]_{t=0}^{t=1} = \frac{1}{s^2 + 1},
\]
and integrating over \(s \in (0, 1)\) gives
\[
\int_0^1 \left( \int_0^1 f(s, t) \, dt \right) ds = \int_0^1 \frac{ds}{s^2 + 1} = [\arctan s]_0^1 = \frac{\pi}{4}.
\]
By antisymmetry \(f(s, t) = -f(t, s)\), swapping \(s\) and \(t\) negates the integral: for fixed \(t \in (0, 1)\), \(\int_0^1 f(s, t) \, ds = -\frac{1}{t^2 + 1}\), and integrating over \(t \in (0, 1)\) gives
\[
\int_0^1 \left( \int_0^1 f(s, t) \, ds \right) dt = -\int_0^1 \frac{dt}{t^2 + 1} = -\frac{\pi}{4} \ne \frac{\pi}{4}!
\]

(b) Both \((0, 1)\) factors are finite measure spaces (hence \(\sigma\)-finite) and \(f\) is continuous on \((0, 1)^2\) (hence product measurable). If \(\int_{(0,1)^2} |f| \, d(\lambda \otimes \lambda)\) were finite, Fubini's theorem (Theorem 9.5) would force the two iterated integrals to be equal! Since \(\frac{\pi}{4} \ne -\frac{\pi}{4}\), we conclude immediately that
\[
\int_{(0, 1)^2} |f(s, t)| \, d(\lambda \otimes \lambda)(s, t) = \infty, \qquad \text{so } f \notin L^1((0, 1)^2).
\]
(Indeed, one can also check directly by Tonelli that for \(s \in (0, 1)\), \(\int_0^1 |f(s, t)| \, dt = \int_0^s \frac{s^2 - t^2}{(s^2+t^2)^2} \, dt + \int_s^1 \frac{t^2 - s^2}{(s^2+t^2)^2} \, dt = \frac{1}{s} - \frac{1}{s^2 + 1}\), whose integral over \(s \in (0, 1)\) diverges like \(\int_0^1 \frac{ds}{s} = \infty\)!)

** Exercise 5 (Convolution of \(L^1(\mathbb{R})\) Functions)
Let \(f, g \in L^1(\mathbb{R}, \mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}), \lambda)\).
(a) Explain why \((x, y) \mapsto f(x - y)g(y)\) is \(\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^2)\)-measurable, and use Tonelli's theorem plus translation invariance of Lebesgue measure to show that
\[
\int_{\mathbb{R}} \left( \int_{\mathbb{R}} |f(x - y)g(y)| \, dy \right) dx = \|f\|_1 \|g\|_1 < \infty.
\]
(b) Deduce from Fubini's theorem that for \(\lambda\)-almost every \(x \in \mathbb{R}\), the *convolution*
\[
(f * g)(x) := \int_{\mathbb{R}} f(x - y)g(y) \, dy
\]
is well-defined (Setting \((f * g)(x) := 0\) on the null exceptional set), belongs to \(L^1(\mathbb{R})\), and satisfies \(\|f * g\|_1 \le \|f\|_1 \|g\|_1\) and \(\int_{\mathbb{R}} (f * g)(x) \, dx = \left(\int_{\mathbb{R}} f(x) \, dx\right)\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}} g(y) \, dy\right)\).

** Solution
(a) Choose Borel representatives for \(f\) and \(g\). The subtraction map \(\sigma : \mathbb{R}^2 \to \mathbb{R}\), \(\sigma(x, y) = x - y\), and the projection \(\pi_2(x, y) = y\) are continuous, hence \(\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^2)\)-measurable. Therefore the compositions \((x, y) \mapsto f(x - y) = (f \circ \sigma)(x, y)\) and \((x, y) \mapsto g(y) = (g \circ \pi_2)(x, y)\) are \(\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^2)\)-measurable, and so is their product \(H(x, y) := f(x - y)g(y)\). Since \(\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^2) = \mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}) \otimes \mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R})\), \(H\) is product measurable.

Applying Tonelli's theorem (Theorem 9.4) to \(|H(x, y)| = |f(x - y)|\,|g(y)| \ge 0\) and integrating \(x\) first,
\[
\int_{\mathbb{R}^2} |f(x - y)g(y)| \, d(\lambda \otimes \lambda)(x, y) = \int_{\mathbb{R}} |g(y)| \left( \int_{\mathbb{R}} |f(x - y)| \, dx \right) dy.
\]
For every fixed \(y \in \mathbb{R}\), translation invariance of Lebesgue measure gives \(\int_{\mathbb{R}} |f(x - y)| \, dx = \int_{\mathbb{R}} |f(u)| \, du = \|f\|_1\). Thus the outer integral is \(\|f\|_1 \int_{\mathbb{R}} |g(y)| \, dy = \|f\|_1 \|g\|_1 < \infty\). Tonelli now equates this to the other iterated integral \(\int_{\mathbb{R}} \big(\int_{\mathbb{R}} |f(x - y)g(y)| \, dy\big) dx\).

(b) Since \(\int_{\mathbb{R}^2} |H| \, d(\lambda \otimes \lambda) = \|f\|_1 \|g\|_1 < \infty\), Fubini's theorem (Theorem 9.5) applies to \(H(x, y) = f(x - y)g(y)\):
- For \(\lambda\)-almost every \(x \in \mathbb{R}\), \(y \mapsto f(x - y)g(y)\) is integrable, so \((f * g)(x) = \int_{\mathbb{R}} f(x - y)g(y) \, dy\) is finite a.e.
- Setting \((f * g)(x) = 0\) on the null exceptional set makes \(f * g\) Borel measurable and integrable on \(\mathbb{R}\), with
  \[
  \|f * g\|_1 = \int_{\mathbb{R}} \left| \int_{\mathbb{R}} f(x - y)g(y) \, dy \right| dx \le \int_{\mathbb{R}} \left( \int_{\mathbb{R}} |f(x - y)g(y)| \, dy \right) dx = \|f\|_1 \|g\|_1 < \infty,
  \]
  and swapping the signed integrals via Fubini gives
  \[
  \int_{\mathbb{R}} (f * g)(x) \, dx = \int_{\mathbb{R}} g(y) \left( \int_{\mathbb{R}} f(x - y) \, dx \right) dy = \left( \int_{\mathbb{R}} f(u) \, du \right) \left( \int_{\mathbb{R}} g(y) \, dy \right).
  \]

** Exercise 6
Let \((S, \mathcal{S}, \mu)\) and \((T, \mathcal{T}, \nu)\) be \(\sigma\)-finite measure spaces, and let \(E \in \mathcal{S} \otimes \mathcal{T}\). Show that the following three conditions are equivalent:
(i) \((\mu \otimes \nu)(E) = 0\);
(ii) \(\nu(E_s) = 0\) for \(\mu\)-almost every \(s \in S\);
(iii) \(\mu(E^t) = 0\) for \(\nu\)-almost every \(t \in T\).

Use this to prove that if \(f, g : S \times T \to \mathbb{R}\) are product measurable and \(f = g\) \((\mu \otimes \nu)\)-almost everywhere, then for \(\mu\)-almost every \(s \in S\), we have \(f_s = g_s\) \(\nu\)-almost everywhere.

** Solution
By Theorem 9.2, formula (9.1), the section-measure functions \(s \mapsto \nu(E_s) \ge 0\) and \(t \mapsto \mu(E^t) \ge 0\) are measurable and satisfy
\[
(\mu \otimes \nu)(E) = \int_S \nu(E_s) \, d\mu(s) = \int_T \mu(E^t) \, d\nu(t).
\]
By Lemma 6.8 (a nonnegative measurable function has integral zero if and only if it is zero almost everywhere), \(\int_S \nu(E_s) \, d\mu(s) = 0 \iff \nu(E_s) = 0\) for \(\mu\)-a.e. \(s \in S\), and \(\int_T \mu(E^t) \, d\nu(t) = 0 \iff \mu(E^t) = 0\) for \(\nu\)-a.e. \(t \in T\). Hence (i) \(\iff\) (ii) \(\iff\) (iii).

Now if \(f, g : S \times T \to \mathbb{R}\) are product measurable and \(f = g\) \((\mu \otimes \nu)\)-almost everywhere, let \(E := \{(s, t) \in S \times T : f(s, t) \ne g(s, t)\} \in \mathcal{S} \otimes \mathcal{T}\). By assumption, \((\mu \otimes \nu)(E) = 0\). By the equivalence (i) \(\iff\) (ii), for \(\mu\)-almost every \(s \in S\) we have
\[
0 = \nu(E_s) = \nu(\{t \in T : f(s, t) \ne g(s, t)\}) = \nu(\{f_s \ne g_s\}),
\]
which means \(f_s = g_s\) \(\nu\)-almost everywhere!

*Key takeaways*

- Rectangles \(A \times B\) form a \(\pi\)-system generating \(\mathcal{S} \otimes \mathcal{T}\); taking sections commutes with complements and unions, so sections of product-measurable sets and functions are always measurable.
- *Product measure* \(\mu \otimes \nu\) is constructed for finite measures by showing \(s \mapsto \nu(E_s)\) is measurable (via Dynkin's lemma, using \(\nu(T) < \infty\) for complements) and integrating over \(s\), then extended to \(\sigma\)-finite spaces by summing over a countable grid of finite cells \(S_i \times T_j\).
- *Tonelli's theorem* (\(f \ge 0\)) requires *no* integrability check; *Fubini's theorem* (signed \(f\)) requires \(\int_{S \times T} |f| \, d(\mu \otimes \nu) < \infty\), which you almost always verify first by applying Tonelli to \(|f|\).
- Dropping absolute integrability (Example 9.6) or dropping \(\sigma\)-finiteness (Example 9.8) destroys the equality of iterated integrals.
- Even when \(\mu\) and \(\nu\) are complete, \(\mu \otimes \nu\) need not be complete (because \(\{0\} \times V\) for a non-measurable Vitali set \(V\) is a subset of the null rectangle \(\{0\} \times [0,1]\) but has non-measurable section \(V\) at \(s=0\)). For completed measures, Fubini still holds, with section measurability and integrability holding for *almost every* coordinate.
