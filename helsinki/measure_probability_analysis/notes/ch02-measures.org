* Overview

** What problem does this chapter solve?

In Chapter 1 we built a *measurable space* \((\Omega, \mathcal{F})\), which specifies *which* subsets
of \(\Omega\) are eligible to have a size. Now we want to assign an actual numerical size \(\mu(A)\)
to each measurable set \(A \in \mathcal{F}\) — whether that size represents length, area, volume,
mass, counting the number of elements, or the probability of an event.

Importantly, choosing the \(\sigma\)-algebra \(\mathcal{F}\) and choosing the measure \(\mu\) are
*two separate steps*: the exact same measurable space \((\Omega, \mathcal{F})\) can carry many
different measures (for example, a fair die and a loaded die live on the same measurable space, and
so do Lebesgue length and a Gaussian distribution on \((\mathbb{R}, \mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}))\)).

What rules should a notion of "size" obey?
1. The empty set has size zero: \(\mu(\emptyset) = 0\).
2. Sizes are nonnegative, though possibly infinite: \(\mu(A) \in [0, \infty]\).
3. *Countable additivity:* If we break a measurable set into a sequence of pairwise disjoint
   measurable pieces \(A_1, A_2, \dots\), the size of the whole union equals the sum of the sizes of
   the pieces:
   \[
   \mu\left(\bigcup_{n=1}^\infty A_n\right) = \sum_{n=1}^\infty \mu(A_n).
   \]

Why require *countable* additivity rather than just finite additivity (\(\mu(A \cup B) = \mu(A) + \mu(B)\)
for disjoint \(A, B\))? Because countable additivity is the engine that lets measure theory interact
with *limits*. As we prove in Section 2.2, countable additivity guarantees that when a sequence of
measurable sets \(A_n\) grows up to a limit set \(A\) (or shrinks down to \(A\) from a set of finite
measure), the measures \(\mu(A_n)\) converge to \(\mu(A)\). Without countable additivity, calculus-style
limit theorems for integrals and probabilities would collapse.

** Probability motivation

When a measure \(\mu\) assigns total size \(1\) to the entire sample space (\(\mu(\Omega) = 1\)), we
call it a *probability measure* and usually denote it by \(\mathbb{P}\). The triple
\((\Omega, \mathcal{F}, \mathbb{P})\) is a *probability space*. Everything we prove for general
measures immediately applies to probability measures — and often simplifies because
\(\mathbb{P}(A) \le \mathbb{P}(\Omega) = 1 < \infty\) for every event \(A \in \mathcal{F}\), so
infinite-measure traps (like \(\infty - \infty\)) never arise!

At the end of the chapter we also meet one of the most important concepts in modern probability and
analysis: *null sets* (sets of measure zero) and properties that hold *almost everywhere* (or *almost
surely* in probability), meaning they hold outside a negligible set of measure zero.

* Definition and first examples of measures

** Intuition

Imagine pouring sand (mass) onto a tabletop \(\Omega\). If \(A_1, A_2, \dots\) are non-overlapping
regions on the table, the total mass of sand inside the combined region \(\bigcup_{n=1}^\infty A_n\)
is simply the sum \(\sum_{n=1}^\infty \mu(A_n)\) of the masses in each individual region.

Because the total length of the real line \(\mathbb{R}\) or the total number of integers in
\(\mathbb{N}\) is infinite, we must allow measures to take the value \(\infty\). Before stating the
definition of a measure, let us make sure our rules for arithmetic and infinite series in
\([0, \infty]\) are completely unambiguous.

** Arithmetic in \([0, \infty]\) and the definition of a measure

We write \([0, \infty] := [0, \infty) \cup \{\infty\}\) for the nonnegative real numbers together with
\(\infty\), ordered so that \(a < \infty\) for every finite \(a \in [0, \infty)\).
- *Addition:* For every \(a \in [0, \infty]\), we set
  \[
  a + \infty = \infty + a = \infty.
  \]
  Notice that since all numbers in \([0, \infty]\) are nonnegative, *we never encounter the
  indeterminate form \(\infty - \infty\) when adding*.
- *Nonnegative infinite series:* Given any sequence of values \(a_n \in [0, \infty]\), the partial
  sums \(S_N := \sum_{n=1}^N a_n\) form an increasing sequence in \([0, \infty]\). We define the
  infinite series by
  \[
  \sum_{n=1}^\infty a_n := \sup_{N \in \mathbb{N}} \sum_{n=1}^N a_n \in [0, \infty].
  \]
  Thus a nonnegative series *always has a well-defined value in \([0, \infty]\)*: it is a finite real
  number if the partial sums are bounded, and \(\infty\) if any \(a_n = \infty\) or if the partial
  sums grow without bound. By convention, an empty sum (a sum with no terms) has value \(0\).

#+begin_quote
*Definition 2.1 (Measure, measure space, and probability space).* Let \((\Omega, \mathcal{F})\) be a
measurable space. A *measure* on this space is a function \(\mu : \mathcal{F} \to [0, \infty]\) such
that \(\mu(\emptyset) = 0\) and, for every sequence of pairwise disjoint sets \(A_n \in \mathcal{F}\),
\begin{equation}
\mu\left(\bigcup_{n=1}^\infty A_n\right) = \sum_{n=1}^\infty \mu(A_n). \tag{2.1}
\end{equation}
This identity is called *countable additivity*. The triple \((\Omega, \mathcal{F}, \mu)\) is a
*measure space*.

If \(\mu(\Omega) = 1\), the measure is a *probability measure*; we usually denote it by
\(\mathbb{P}\). The triple \((\Omega, \mathcal{F}, \mathbb{P})\) is then a *probability space*. Its
points are *outcomes* or *sample points*, and its measurable sets are *events*.
#+end_quote

Let us note two immediate points about Definition 2.1:
1. *Both sides of (2.1) live in \([0, \infty]\):* No subtraction of infinite quantities is involved.
   Because \(\bigcup_{n=1}^\infty A_n \in \mathcal{F}\) (by the \(\sigma\)-algebra axiom), the left
   side is well-defined; because \(\mu(A_n) \ge 0\), the series on the right is well-defined in
   \([0, \infty]\).
2. *Finite additivity is included automatically:* Suppose \(A_1, \dots, A_k \in \mathcal{F}\) is a
   *finite* list of pairwise disjoint measurable sets. How do we get
   \(\mu(A_1 \cup \cdots \cup A_k) = \mu(A_1) + \cdots + \mu(A_k)\)? Just as in Chapter 1, we append
   empty sets: set \(A_n := \emptyset\) for all \(n > k\). Since \(A_n \cap A_m = \emptyset\) still
   holds for all \(n \ne m\) and \(\mu(\emptyset) = 0\), countable additivity (2.1) gives
   \[
   \mu(A_1 \cup \cdots \cup A_k) = \mu\left(\bigcup_{n=1}^\infty A_n\right)
   = \sum_{n=1}^k \mu(A_n) + \sum_{n=k+1}^\infty 0 = \sum_{n=1}^k \mu(A_n).
   \]
   (Note why we had to assume \(\mu(\emptyset) = 0\) explicitly in Definition 2.1: if \(\mu\) were identically
   \(\infty\) on *every* set including \(\emptyset\), then \(\infty = \infty + \infty + \cdots\) would
   still hold in \([0, \infty]\)! Requiring \(\mu(\emptyset) = 0\) rules out that degenerate case.)

#+begin_quote
*Remark 2.2 (Events and observations).* If the outcome of an experiment is \(\omega \in \Omega\), we
say that an event \(A \in \mathcal{F}\) *occurs* when \(\omega \in A\). For the die
(\(\Omega = \{1,\dots,6\}\)), the event \(D = \{2, 4, 6\}\) occurs exactly when the outcome is even.
Recording only the parity of the roll suffices to answer whether \(D\) occurred, but does not
determine whether the outcome was \(6\).

This observational interpretation helps explain *why* we might choose a particular \(\sigma\)-algebra
\(\mathcal{F}\). However, it is *not* a mathematical definition of measurability, nor does it imply
that every measurable event in an infinite model can be decided from finitely many observations. The
mathematical definition of an event remains simply membership in \(\mathcal{F}\).
#+end_quote

** Core examples of measures

#+begin_quote
*Example 2.3 (Assigning weights to outcomes; counting, Dirac, and Lebesgue measures; \(\sigma\)-finiteness).*
On a finite or countable set \(\Omega\), choose nonnegative weights \(p_\omega \ge 0\) for each
\(\omega \in \Omega\) and set
\begin{equation}
\mu(A) = \sum_{\omega \in A} p_\omega, \qquad A \subseteq \Omega. \tag{2.2}
\end{equation}
Then \(\mu\) is a measure on \((\Omega, 2^\Omega)\).
#+end_quote

Because (2.2) sums over an arbitrary subset \(A \subseteq \Omega\) (which does not come with a pre-assigned
order), let us carefully unpack how the lecture notes define \(\sum_{\omega \in A} p_\omega\) and
prove countable additivity using only elementary supremum arguments:

1. *How is \(\sum_{\omega \in A} p_\omega\) defined?* By definition, it is the supremum of the finite
   sums over all finite subsets \(F \subseteq A\):
   \[
   \mu(A) = \sum_{\omega \in A} p_\omega := \sup\left\{\sum_{\omega \in F} p_\omega : F \subseteq A,\ F \text{ finite}\right\} \in [0, \infty].
   \]
   For \(A = \emptyset\), the only finite subset is \(F = \emptyset\), whose empty sum is \(0\), so
   \(\mu(\emptyset) = 0\).
2. *Why does this match any sequential listing when \(A\) is countably infinite?* Suppose \(A\) is
   countably infinite and we list its elements without repetitions as \(\omega_1, \omega_2, \dots\).
   - Every partial sum \(\sum_{k=1}^N p_{\omega_k}\) is the sum over the finite subset
     \(F_N = \{\omega_1, \dots, \omega_N\} \subseteq A\), so
     \(\sup_N \sum_{k=1}^N p_{\omega_k} \le \mu(A)\).
   - Conversely, if \(F \subseteq A\) is any finite subset, its finitely many elements must all
     appear somewhere in the listing \(\omega_1, \omega_2, \dots\), so \(F \subseteq \{\omega_1, \dots, \omega_N\}\)
     for some large enough \(N\). Since \(p_\omega \ge 0\), we have
     \(\sum_{\omega \in F} p_\omega \le \sum_{k=1}^N p_{\omega_k}\). Taking the supremum over all
     finite \(F \subseteq A\) gives \(\mu(A) \le \sup_N \sum_{k=1}^N p_{\omega_k}\).
   In particular, *the sum \(\sum_{\omega \in A} p_\omega\) does not depend on the order in which we
   list the elements of \(A\)!*
3. *Step-by-step proof of countable additivity for (2.2):* Let \(A_1, A_2, \dots \subseteq \Omega\)
   be pairwise disjoint, and write \(A = \bigcup_{j=1}^\infty A_j\). We prove the two inequalities
   \(\mu(A) \le \sum_{j=1}^\infty \mu(A_j)\) and \(\sum_{j=1}^\infty \mu(A_j) \le \mu(A)\):
   - *(\(\le\)):* Take any finite subset \(F \subseteq \bigcup_{j=1}^\infty A_j\). Because \(F\) has
     only finitely many elements and the \(A_j\) are disjoint, \(F\) can meet only finitely many of
     the sets \(A_j\), say \(A_1, \dots, A_M\). For each \(j \in \{1, \dots, M\}\), the slice
     \(F \cap A_j\) is a finite subset of \(A_j\), so \(\sum_{\omega \in F \cap A_j} p_\omega \le \mu(A_j)\).
     Summing over \(j = 1, \dots, M\) gives
     \[
     \sum_{\omega \in F} p_\omega = \sum_{j=1}^M \sum_{\omega \in F \cap A_j} p_\omega
     \le \sum_{j=1}^M \mu(A_j) \le \sum_{j=1}^\infty \mu(A_j).
     \]
     Taking the supremum over all finite subsets \(F \subseteq \bigcup_{j=1}^\infty A_j\) yields
     \(\mu\bigl(\bigcup_{j=1}^\infty A_j\bigr) \le \sum_{j=1}^\infty \mu(A_j)\).
   - *(\(\ge\)):* Fix any integer \(N \ge 1\), and for each \(1 \le j \le N\) pick an arbitrary
     finite subset \(F_j \subseteq A_j\). Since \(A_1, \dots, A_N\) are pairwise disjoint, the
     \(F_j\) are pairwise disjoint and their union \(F := \bigcup_{j=1}^N F_j\) is a finite subset of
     \(\bigcup_{j=1}^\infty A_j\). Hence
     \[
     \sum_{j=1}^N \sum_{\omega \in F_j} p_\omega = \sum_{\omega \in F} p_\omega
     \le \mu\left(\bigcup_{j=1}^\infty A_j\right).
     \]
     Taking the supremum independently over each of the finitely many choices \(F_1 \subseteq A_1, \dots, F_N \subseteq A_N\)
     gives \(\sum_{j=1}^N \mu(A_j) \le \mu\bigl(\bigcup_{j=1}^\infty A_j\bigr)\). Finally, taking the
     supremum over \(N \in \mathbb{N}\) gives \(\sum_{j=1}^\infty \mu(A_j) \le \mu\bigl(\bigcup_{j=1}^\infty A_j\bigr)\). \(\square\)

Let us survey the key examples and concepts introduced right after (2.2) in Example 2.3:

- *Discrete probability measures (Fair vs. loaded die):* The weighted measure (2.2) is a probability
  measure when \(\sum_{\omega \in \Omega} p_\omega = 1\). For a *fair die* on
  \(\Omega = \{1, \dots, 6\}\), each weight is \(p_\omega = \frac{1}{6}\), and for
  \(D = \{2, 4, 6\}\) we have \(\mathbb{P}(D) = \frac{1}{6} + \frac{1}{6} + \frac{1}{6} = \frac{1}{2}\).
  Choosing unequal weights (with \(\sum_{\omega=1}^6 p_\omega = 1\)) gives a *different* probability
  measure on the *exact same* measurable space. Thus *fairness is an assumption about the measure,
  not about the \(\sigma\)-algebra*.
- *Counting measure:* For any set \(\Omega\), *counting measure* assigns to each set \(A\) its number
  of elements \(\#(A)\) when \(A\) is finite, and \(\infty\) when \(A\) is infinite. On
  \(\Omega = \mathbb{N}\), counting measure is simply (2.2) with weight \(p_\omega = 1\) at every
  point, and its total mass is \(\mu(\mathbb{N}) = \infty\).
- *Dirac measure (Point mass):* For *any* measurable space \((\Omega, \mathcal{F})\) and any fixed
  point \(x \in \Omega\), the *Dirac measure at \(x\)*, denoted by \(\delta_x\), is defined by
  \[
  \delta_x(A) := \begin{cases} 1 & \text{if } x \in A, \\ 0 & \text{if } x \notin A, \end{cases} \qquad A \in \mathcal{F}.
  \]
  Why is \(\delta_x\) a probability measure? First, \(x \notin \emptyset\), so \(\delta_x(\emptyset) = 0\),
  and \(x \in \Omega\), so \(\delta_x(\Omega) = 1\). Second, if \(A_1, A_2, \dots \in \mathcal{F}\)
  are pairwise disjoint, then the point \(x\) can belong to *at most one* of the sets \(A_n\):
  - If \(x \in A_{n_0}\) for some (unique) \(n_0\), then \(x \in \bigcup_{n=1}^\infty A_n\), so both
    \(\delta_x\bigl(\bigcup_{n=1}^\infty A_n\bigr)\) and \(\sum_{n=1}^\infty \delta_x(A_n)\) equal \(1\).
  - If \(x \notin A_n\) for all \(n\), then \(x \notin \bigcup_{n=1}^\infty A_n\), so both sides equal \(0\).
  Notice a subtle point remarked in the lecture notes: *defining \(\delta_x\) requires no assumption
  that the singleton \(\{x\}\) is measurable!* It simply evaluates whether \(x \in A\) for sets
  \(A\) that belong to \(\mathcal{F}\).
- *Lebesgue measure \(\lambda\):* We denote Lebesgue measure by \(\lambda\) (constructed rigorously
  in Chapter 5; it extends the length \(\lambda([a, b]) = b - a\) of intervals to all Borel sets). On
  \((\mathbb{R}, \mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}))\), its total mass is \(\lambda(\mathbb{R}) = \infty\). On
  the unit interval \(([0, 1], \mathcal{B}([0, 1]))\), the restriction \(A \mapsto \lambda(A)\) has
  total mass \(\lambda([0, 1]) = 1 - 0 = 1\), so it is a *probability measure* (the continuous
  uniform distribution on \([0, 1]\)).
- *Finite and \(\sigma\)-finite measures:*
  #+begin_quote
  *Definition (Finite and \(\sigma\)-finite measures).* A measure \(\mu\) on \((\Omega, \mathcal{F})\) is:
  - *finite* if \(\mu(\Omega) < \infty\);
  - *\(\sigma\)-finite* (sigma-finite) if \(\Omega\) can be written as a countable union of measurable
    sets of finite measure:
    \[
    \Omega = \bigcup_{n=1}^\infty E_n \quad \text{with } E_n \in \mathcal{F} \text{ and } \mu(E_n) < \infty \text{ for every } n \in \mathbb{N}.
    \]
  #+end_quote
  - *Examples of \(\sigma\)-finite measures:*
    1. Every finite measure (and in particular every probability measure) is \(\sigma\)-finite: just
       take \(E_1 = \Omega, E_2 = \emptyset, \dots\)
    2. Counting measure on \(\mathbb{N}\) has \(\mu(\mathbb{N}) = \infty\), so it is not finite, but
       it *is* \(\sigma\)-finite because \(\mathbb{N} = \bigcup_{n=1}^\infty \{n\}\) and each
       singleton has \(\mu(\{n\}) = 1 < \infty\).
    3. Lebesgue measure \(\lambda\) on \(\mathbb{R}\) has \(\lambda(\mathbb{R}) = \infty\), but it
       *is* \(\sigma\)-finite because \(\mathbb{R} = \bigcup_{n=1}^\infty [-n, n]\) and each bounded
       interval has \(\lambda([-n, n]) = 2n < \infty\).
  - *Non-example (A measure that is NOT \(\sigma\)-finite):* Consider counting measure \(\#\) on the
    uncountable space \((\mathbb{R}, \mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}))\). If \(E \subseteq \mathbb{R}\) has
    finite counting measure \(\#(E) < \infty\), then \(E\) is a finite set. A countable union of
    finite sets \(\bigcup_{n=1}^\infty E_n\) is at most countable, so it can *never* cover the
    uncountable set \(\mathbb{R}\)! Thus counting measure on \(\mathbb{R}\) is not \(\sigma\)-finite.
  - *Normalizing finite measures:* If \(0 < \mu(\Omega) < \infty\), we can normalize \(\mu\) into a
    probability measure by setting \(\mathbb{P}(A) := \mu(A) / \mu(\Omega)\). An infinite measure
    (\(\mu(\Omega) = \infty\)) cannot be normalized this way.

#+begin_quote
*Example 2.4 (There is no uniform probability on \(\mathbb{N}\)).* There does not exist a probability
measure \(\mathbb{P}\) on \((\mathbb{N}, 2^{\mathbb{N}})\) that assigns the same probability to every
singleton \(\{n\}\).
#+end_quote

*Proof of Example 2.4:* Suppose for contradiction that \(\mathbb{P}(\{n\}) = c \in [0, 1]\) for every
\(n \in \mathbb{N}\). Since \(\mathbb{N} = \bigcup_{n=1}^\infty \{n\}\) is a countable union of
pairwise disjoint singletons, countable additivity requires
\[
1 = \mathbb{P}(\mathbb{N}) = \sum_{n=1}^\infty \mathbb{P}(\{n\}) = \sum_{n=1}^\infty c.
\]
- *Case 1 (\(c > 0\)):* By the Archimedean property, taking \(N > 1/c\) disjoint singletons already
  gives \(\mathbb{P}(\{1, \dots, N\}) = N c > 1\), and the infinite series sums to \(\infty \ne 1\).
- *Case 2 (\(c = 0\)):* Then \(\sum_{n=1}^\infty 0 = 0 \ne 1\).

Neither case is compatible with \(\mathbb{P}(\mathbb{N}) = 1\). Therefore, an informal instruction
like "choose a positive integer uniformly at random" does *not* define a countably additive
probability model! \(\square\)

* Two consequences of countable additivity

** Intuition

Countable additivity requires the sets \(A_n\) to be *pairwise disjoint*. What if we have sets that
*overlap*?
1. If \(A \subseteq B\), then \(B\) consists of \(A\) plus the extra non-overlapping collar
   \(B \setminus A\). Since the collar has nonnegative measure \(\mu(B \setminus A) \ge 0\), the larger
   set \(B\) must have measure at least \(\mu(A)\). This is *monotonicity*.
2. If \(A_1, A_2, \dots\) is an arbitrary sequence of measurable sets (possibly overlapping), how can
   we relate \(\mu\bigl(\bigcup_{n=1}^\infty A_n\bigr)\) to \(\sum_{n=1}^\infty \mu(A_n)\)? We use a
   fundamental technique that recurs throughout the course: *disjointification* (removing the overlaps).
   We keep all of \(A_1\), then from \(A_2\) keep only the part not already in \(A_1\), then from
   \(A_3\) keep only the part not already in \(A_1 \cup A_2\), and so on. This slices the union into
   pairwise disjoint pieces \(B_n \subseteq A_n\) without losing or double-counting a single point!

** Monotonicity and the union bound

#+begin_quote
*Lemma 2.5 (Monotonicity and the union bound).* Let \((\Omega, \mathcal{F}, \mu)\) be a measure space.
If \(A, B \in \mathcal{F}\) and \(A \subseteq B\), then \(\mu(A) \le \mu(B)\). For every sequence
\(A_n \in \mathcal{F}\), we have
\begin{equation}
\mu\left(\bigcup_{n=1}^\infty A_n\right) \le \sum_{n=1}^\infty \mu(A_n). \tag{2.3}
\end{equation}
#+end_quote

*(Terminology:* Inequality (2.3) is called *countable subadditivity* in measure theory, and *Boole's
inequality* or the *union bound* in probability theory.)

** Unpacked proof of Lemma 2.5

*Step 1. Monotonicity.* Suppose \(A, B \in \mathcal{F}\) with \(A \subseteq B\). Then we can split
\(B\) into two disjoint measurable sets:
\[
B = A \cup (B \setminus A), \qquad A \cap (B \setminus A) = \emptyset.
\]
By finite additivity (obtained from countable additivity (2.1) by appending empty sets),
\[
\mu(B) = \mu(A) + \mu(B \setminus A).
\]
Since \(\mu(B \setminus A) \ge 0\) in \([0, \infty]\), adding it to \(\mu(A)\) gives
\[
\mu(B) = \mu(A) + \mu(B \setminus A) \ge \mu(A).
\]
Notice that we did *not* subtract \(\mu(A)\) from both sides, so this argument remains 100% valid
even when \(\mu(A) = \infty\) or \(\mu(B) = \infty\)!

*(Useful side remark — the excision / subtraction formula:* If \(A \subseteq B\) and we additionally
know that \(\mu(A) < \infty\), then because \(\mu(A)\) is a finite real number we *can* subtract it from
\(\mu(B) = \mu(A) + \mu(B \setminus A)\) to get
\[
\mu(B \setminus A) = \mu(B) - \mu(A) \qquad (\text{valid whenever } A \subseteq B \text{ and } \mu(A) < \infty).
\]
We will use this subtraction formula in Step 2 of Proposition 2.6 below.)

*Step 2. Remove the overlaps (Disjointification).* Let \(A_1, A_2, \dots \in \mathcal{F}\) be any
sequence of measurable sets. Define a new sequence \(B_1, B_2, \dots\) by setting
\[
B_1 := A_1 \quad \text{and} \quad B_n := A_n \setminus \bigcup_{j=1}^{n-1} A_j \quad \text{for } n \ge 2.
\]
Let us check four properties of the sets \(B_n\):
1. *Measurability:* Since \(\mathcal{F}\) is closed under finite unions and set differences, each
   \(B_n \in \mathcal{F}\).
2. *Containment:* By definition of set difference, \(B_n \subseteq A_n\) for every \(n \in \mathbb{N}\).
3. *Pairwise disjointness:* Take any \(m < n\). By definition, \(B_n\) excludes all elements of
   \(\bigcup_{j=1}^{n-1} A_j\). Since \(m \le n - 1\), we have
   \(B_m \subseteq A_m \subseteq \bigcup_{j=1}^{n-1} A_j\), so no element of \(B_m\) can belong to
   \(B_n\). Thus \(B_m \cap B_n = \emptyset\) whenever \(m \ne n\).
4. *Same union:* Why does \(\bigcup_{n=1}^\infty B_n = \bigcup_{n=1}^\infty A_n\)?
   - (\(\subseteq\)): Since \(B_n \subseteq A_n\) for every \(n\), we immediately have
     \(\bigcup_{n=1}^\infty B_n \subseteq \bigcup_{n=1}^\infty A_n\).
   - (\(\supseteq\)): Take any point \(\omega \in \bigcup_{n=1}^\infty A_n\). The set of indices
     \(\{k \in \mathbb{N} : \omega \in A_k\}\) is a nonempty subset of \(\mathbb{N}\), so by the
     well-ordering of \(\mathbb{N}\) there is a *first* index \(n \in \mathbb{N}\) such that
     \(\omega \in A_n\). Being the *first* such index means \(\omega \notin A_j\) for all
     \(1 \le j \le n - 1\), which is the exact definition of \(\omega \in B_n\)! Thus every point of
     \(\bigcup_{n=1}^\infty A_n\) belongs to a unique piece \(B_n\).

Now combine countable additivity (applied to the pairwise disjoint sequence \(B_n\)) with
monotonicity from Step 1 (\(\mu(B_n) \le \mu(A_n)\) since \(B_n \subseteq A_n\)):
\[
\mu\left(\bigcup_{n=1}^\infty A_n\right) = \mu\left(\bigcup_{n=1}^\infty B_n\right)
= \sum_{n=1}^\infty \mu(B_n) \le \sum_{n=1}^\infty \mu(A_n). \qquad \square
\]

*Probabilistic interpretation of the union bound:* If \(A_1, A_2, \dots\) are events in a probability
space, \(\mathbb{P}\bigl(\bigcup_{n=1}^\infty A_n\bigr)\) is the probability that *at least one* of
the events \(A_n\) occurs. Lemma 2.5 says this is at most \(\sum_{n=1}^\infty \mathbb{P}(A_n)\),
*with no independence assumption whatsoever*: overlaps between the events \(A_n\) can only cause the
right-hand sum \(\sum_{n=1}^\infty \mathbb{P}(A_n)\) to count the overlapping outcomes multiple times.

* Continuity of a measure

** Intuition

Suppose a sequence of measurable sets \(A_1, A_2, A_3, \dots\) approximates a limiting set \(A\) in a
monotone way:
- *Increasing sets (\(A_n \uparrow A\)):* \(A_1 \subseteq A_2 \subseteq A_3 \subseteq \cdots\) and
  \(A = \bigcup_{n=1}^\infty A_n\). Think of an expanding inkblot: every point of \(A\) enters at
  some stage \(n\) and remains inside from then on. We can slice the expanding inkblot into concentric
  "annuli" (rings) \(B_1 = A_1\) and \(B_n = A_n \setminus A_{n-1}\), which are pairwise disjoint!
  Applying countable additivity to the rings shows that \(\mu(A_n) \to \mu(A)\) *always*.
- *Decreasing sets (\(A_n \downarrow A\)):* \(A_1 \supseteq A_2 \supseteq A_3 \supseteq \cdots\) and
  \(A = \bigcap_{n=1}^\infty A_n\). Think of a puddle evaporating: a point belongs to the final puddle
  \(A\) precisely when it survives at *every* stage \(n\). If the initial puddle has finite size
  \(\mu(A_1) < \infty\), then taking complements inside \(A_1\) turns the shrinking sequence \(A_n\)
  into an expanding sequence \(A_1 \setminus A_n \uparrow A_1 \setminus A\), and subtracting from
  \(\mu(A_1)\) shows \(\mu(A_n) \to \mu(A)\).

** Monotone sequences of sets and Proposition 2.6

Let us record the exact notation from the lecture notes:
- A sequence of sets \((A_n)_{n=1}^\infty\) is *increasing* if \(A_n \subseteq A_{n+1}\) for every
  \(n \in \mathbb{N}\). We write \(A_n \uparrow A\) if \((A_n)\) is increasing and
  \(\bigcup_{n=1}^\infty A_n = A\).
- A sequence of sets \((A_n)_{n=1}^\infty\) is *decreasing* if \(A_{n+1} \subseteq A_n\) for every
  \(n \in \mathbb{N}\). We write \(A_n \downarrow A\) if \((A_n)\) is decreasing and
  \(\bigcap_{n=1}^\infty A_n = A\).

Note that the arrows \(\uparrow\) and \(\downarrow\) describe set inclusion and the limiting union or
intersection, not convergence of individual points in \(\Omega\). Successive sets are allowed to be
equal (\(A_n = A_{n+1}\)), and the limiting set \(A\) can be \(\emptyset\).

#+begin_quote
*Proposition 2.6 (Continuity of a measure).* Let \(A_n\) and \(A\) be measurable sets in a measure
space \((\Omega, \mathcal{F}, \mu)\).
- (i) *(Continuity from below)* If \(A_n \uparrow A\), then \(\mu(A_n) \to \mu(A)\) as \(n \to \infty\).
- (ii) *(Continuity from above)* If \(A_n \downarrow A\) and \(\mu(A_1) < \infty\), then
  \(\mu(A_n) \to \mu(A)\) as \(n \to \infty\).
#+end_quote

** Unpacked proof of Proposition 2.6

*Step 1. Increasing sets (Part (i)).* Suppose \(A_n \uparrow A\). We separate the "new ring" added at
each stage by defining
\[
B_1 := A_1 \quad \text{and} \quad B_j := A_j \setminus A_{j-1} \quad \text{for } j \ge 2.
\]
Because \(A_1 \subseteq A_2 \subseteq A_3 \subseteq \cdots\), notice that the disjointification
formula \(A_j \setminus \bigcup_{i=1}^{j-1} A_i\) from Lemma 2.5 simplifies directly to
\(A_j \setminus A_{j-1}\)! Thus:
1. The sets \(B_1, B_2, \dots \in \mathcal{F}\) are pairwise disjoint.
2. For each fixed \(n \in \mathbb{N}\), the union of the first \(n\) rings is telescope-like:
   \[
   \bigcup_{j=1}^n B_j = A_1 \cup (A_2 \setminus A_1) \cup \cdots \cup (A_n \setminus A_{n-1}) = A_n.
   \]
   By finite additivity, \(\sum_{j=1}^n \mu(B_j) = \mu(A_n)\).
3. The union of *all* the rings is \(\bigcup_{j=1}^\infty B_j = \bigcup_{n=1}^\infty A_n = A\).

Applying countable additivity to \(A = \bigcup_{j=1}^\infty B_j\) and using the definition of an
infinite series as the limit of its partial sums, we obtain
\[
\mu(A) = \sum_{j=1}^\infty \mu(B_j) = \lim_{n \to \infty} \sum_{j=1}^n \mu(B_j) = \lim_{n \to \infty} \mu(A_n).
\]
Notice that this holds whether the common value \(\mu(A)\) is finite or \(\infty\).

*Step 2. Decreasing sets (Part (ii)).* Suppose \(A_n \downarrow A\) and \(\mu(A_1) < \infty\). We
convert the decreasing sequence into an increasing sequence by taking complements *inside \(A_1\)*:
define \(C_n := A_1 \setminus A_n\) for \(n \in \mathbb{N}\).
1. Since \(A_{n+1} \subseteq A_n\), removing a smaller set from \(A_1\) leaves a larger set:
   \(C_n = A_1 \setminus A_n \subseteq A_1 \setminus A_{n+1} = C_{n+1}\).
2. By De Morgan's law relative to \(A_1\),
   \[
   \bigcup_{n=1}^\infty (A_1 \setminus A_n) = A_1 \setminus \bigcap_{n=1}^\infty A_n = A_1 \setminus A.
   \]
   Thus \(C_n \uparrow A_1 \setminus A\).
3. Applying Step 1 to the increasing sequence \(C_n \uparrow A_1 \setminus A\) gives
   \[
   \mu(A_1 \setminus A_n) \longrightarrow \mu(A_1 \setminus A) \quad \text{as } n \to \infty.
   \]
4. Now use the hypothesis \(\mu(A_1) < \infty\)! Because \(A_n \subseteq A_1\) and \(A \subseteq A_1\),
   monotonicity gives \(\mu(A_n) \le \mu(A_1) < \infty\) and \(\mu(A) \le \mu(A_1) < \infty\). By
   finite additivity on the disjoint decompositions \(A_1 = A_n \cup (A_1 \setminus A_n)\) and
   \(A_1 = A \cup (A_1 \setminus A)\), we have
   \[
   \mu(A_1) = \mu(A_n) + \mu(A_1 \setminus A_n) \quad \text{and} \quad \mu(A_1) = \mu(A) + \mu(A_1 \setminus A).
   \]
   Since all numbers in these equations are *finite*, we can subtract \(\mu(A_1 \setminus A_n)\) and
   \(\mu(A_1 \setminus A)\) to conclude:
   \[
   \mu(A_n) = \mu(A_1) - \mu(A_1 \setminus A_n) \longrightarrow \mu(A_1) - \mu(A_1 \setminus A) = \mu(A). \qquad \square
   \]

*(Bonus enhancement:* In Proposition 2.6(ii), what if \(\mu(A_1) = \infty\), wait — what if
\(\mu(A_N) < \infty\) for *some* later index \(N \ge 1\)? Then we can simply discard the first
\(N - 1\) terms and apply Proposition 2.6(ii) starting at \(A_N\), so \(\mu(A_n) \to \mu(A)\) still
holds whenever \(\mu(A_N) < \infty\) for at least one \(N\). What fails is when \(\mu(A_n) = \infty\)
for *all* \(n\), as Example 2.7 now demonstrates!)

** Examples from the notes: Finiteness, null sets, and "almost surely"

#+begin_quote
*Example 2.7 (Why finiteness is needed in continuity from above).* On \(\mathbb{N}\) with counting
measure \(\mu = \#\), consider the tail sets
\[
A_n := \{n, n+1, n+2, \dots\}, \qquad n \in \mathbb{N}.
\]
Clearly \(A_1 \supseteq A_2 \supseteq A_3 \supseteq \cdots\), and what is their intersection
\(\bigcap_{n=1}^\infty A_n\)? Any positive integer \(k \in \mathbb{N}\) fails to belong to
\(A_{k+1} = \{k+1, k+2, \dots\}\), so no integer belongs to all \(A_n\):
\[
A_n \downarrow \emptyset.
\]
However, every \(A_n\) contains infinitely many integers, so \(\mu(A_n) = \infty\) for all
\(n \in \mathbb{N}\), whereas \(\mu(\emptyset) = 0\):
\[
\lim_{n \to \infty} \mu(A_n) = \infty \ne 0 = \mu\left(\bigcap_{n=1}^\infty A_n\right).
\]
Where did Step 2 of the proof of Proposition 2.6 break down here? The sets
\(A_1 \setminus A_n = \{1, \dots, n-1\}\) do increase to \(A_1 \setminus \emptyset = \mathbb{N}\), and
\(\mu(A_1 \setminus A_n) = n - 1 \to \infty = \mu(\mathbb{N})\) is completely true! What breaks is
the subtraction \(\mu(A_1) - \mu(A_1 \setminus A)\), which would be \(\infty - \infty\).

Notice that on a *probability space* \((\Omega, \mathcal{F}, \mathbb{P})\), this obstruction can
*never* occur, because \(\mathbb{P}(A_1) \le \mathbb{P}(\Omega) = 1 < \infty\) is always finite!
#+end_quote

#+begin_quote
*Example 2.8 (A point can have probability zero).* On \([0, 1]\) equipped with Lebesgue probability
measure \(\mathbb{P} = \lambda|_{[0,1]}\), consider the shrinking intervals
\[
A_n := [0, 1/n], \qquad n \in \mathbb{N}.
\]
Here \(A_1 \supseteq A_2 \supseteq \cdots\) and \(\bigcap_{n=1}^\infty [0, 1/n] = \{0\}\), so
\(A_n \downarrow \{0\}\). Since \(\mathbb{P}(A_1) = 1 < \infty\), continuity from above
(Proposition 2.6(ii)) gives
\[
\mathbb{P}(\{0\}) = \lim_{n \to \infty} \mathbb{P}([0, 1/n]) = \lim_{n \to \infty} \frac{1}{n} = 0.
\]
By the same argument (using \([x, x + 1/n]\) or \([x - 1/n, x]\)), *every singleton \(\{x\} \subseteq [0, 1]\)
has probability zero*, even though when we pick a random point \(\omega \in [0, 1]\), *some* point is
selected!

Why is there no contradiction with additivity? Because
\[
[0, 1] = \bigcup_{x \in [0, 1]} \{x\}
\]
is an *uncountable* union of singletons, whereas countable additivity (2.1) applies only to
*countable* unions.
#+end_quote

This leads directly to the fundamental notions of *null sets*, *almost everywhere*, and *almost surely*:

#+begin_quote
*Definition (Null set, almost everywhere, and almost surely).* Let \((\Omega, \mathcal{F}, \mu)\) be a
measure space.
- A measurable set \(N \in \mathcal{F}\) with \(\mu(N) = 0\) is called a *null set*.
- A property or assertion \(P(\omega)\) about points \(\omega \in \Omega\) holds *almost everywhere*
  (abbreviated *a.e.* or *\(\mu\)-a.e.*) if it holds outside some measurable null set — that is, if
  there exists a measurable set \(N \in \mathcal{F}\) with \(\mu(N) = 0\) such that
  \[
  \{\omega \in \Omega : P(\omega) \text{ fails}\} \subseteq N.
  \]
- When \(\mu = \mathbb{P}\) is a probability measure, the exact same condition is said to hold
  *almost surely* (abbreviated *a.s.* or *\(\mathbb{P}\)-a.s.*).
#+end_quote

Let us highlight two crucial subtleties in this definition:
1. *"Almost surely" does NOT mean "for every outcome":* In Example 2.8 (\([0, 1]\) with Lebesgue
   measure), the singleton \(N = \{0\}\) is a Borel null set (\(\mathbb{P}(\{0\}) = 0\)). Thus the
   statement "the selected point \(\omega\) is nonzero" (\(\omega \ne 0\)) holds *almost surely*, even
   though \(\omega = 0\) belongs to the sample space \([0, 1]\)! Conversely, an event of probability
   \(0\) (such as picking \(0\), or picking a rational number since \(\mathbb{P}(\mathbb{Q} \cap [0,1]) = \sum_{q \in \mathbb{Q} \cap [0,1]} \mathbb{P}(\{q\}) = 0\))
   is not "logically impossible" as a subset of \(\Omega\); it simply has measure zero.
2. *Why does the definition say \(\{\omega : P(\omega) \text{ fails}\} \subseteq N\) rather than
   \(\mu(\{\omega : P(\omega) \text{ fails}\}) = 0\)?* Look closely at the lecture notes' wording:
   *"Its set of exceptions need only be contained in that null set; it need not itself be measurable."*
   Depending on the \(\sigma\)-algebra \(\mathcal{F}\), a subset \(E \subseteq N\) of a null set
   \(N \in \mathcal{F}\) might not belong to \(\mathcal{F}\) (so writing \(\mu(E)\) would not even
   make sense!). Requiring \(E \subseteq N\) for some *measurable* null set \(N \in \mathcal{F}\) avoids
   having to check whether the exception set \(E\) itself is measurable. (In Chapter 5 we will study
   *complete* measure spaces, where all subsets of null sets are adjoined to \(\mathcal{F}\).)

* Worked Exercises

** Exercise 1 (A countable union of null sets is a null set)
Let \((\Omega, \mathcal{F}, \mu)\) be a measure space.
1. Suppose \(N_1, N_2, \dots \in \mathcal{F}\) are null sets (\(\mu(N_k) = 0\) for all \(k \in \mathbb{N}\)).
   Prove that \(\bigcup_{k=1}^\infty N_k\) is also a null set.
2. Deduce that on \((\mathbb{R}, \mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}), \lambda)\), every countable subset
   \(C \subseteq \mathbb{R}\) (in particular \(\mathbb{Q}\)) satisfies \(\lambda(C) = 0\).
3. Suppose \(P_1(\omega), P_2(\omega), \dots\) is a sequence of assertions, each of which holds
   \(\mu\)-almost everywhere. Prove that the combined assertion "\(P_k(\omega)\) holds for *every*
   \(k \in \mathbb{N}\)" also holds \(\mu\)-almost everywhere.

** Solution
1. By monotonicity (\(\mu \ge 0\)) and the union bound (Lemma 2.5),
   \[
   0 \le \mu\left(\bigcup_{k=1}^\infty N_k\right) \le \sum_{k=1}^\infty \mu(N_k) = \sum_{k=1}^\infty 0 = 0.
   \]
   Hence \(\mu\bigl(\bigcup_{k=1}^\infty N_k\bigr) = 0\).
2. Just as in Example 2.8, for every \(x \in \mathbb{R}\) we have \([x, x + 1/n] \downarrow \{x\}\)
   with \(\lambda([x, x+1]) = 1 < \infty\), so Proposition 2.6(ii) gives \(\lambda(\{x\}) = \lim_{n\to\infty} 1/n = 0\).
   Any countable set \(C \subseteq \mathbb{R}\) is a countable union of singletons
   \(C = \bigcup_{x \in C} \{x\}\), so by part 1 we have \(\lambda(C) = 0\).
3. For each \(k \in \mathbb{N}\), let \(E_k := \{\omega \in \Omega : P_k(\omega) \text{ fails}\}\).
   Since \(P_k\) holds \(\mu\)-a.e., there exists a measurable null set \(N_k \in \mathcal{F}\) with
   \(\mu(N_k) = 0\) and \(E_k \subseteq N_k\). The set of points where "\(P_k(\omega)\) for all \(k\)"
   fails is \(\bigcup_{k=1}^\infty E_k \subseteq \bigcup_{k=1}^\infty N_k =: N\). By part 1,
   \(N \in \mathcal{F}\) and \(\mu(N) = 0\). Thus "\(P_k(\omega)\) holds for all \(k\)" holds
   \(\mu\)-almost everywhere! (Warning: this works for *countably* many assertions, not uncountably
   many!)

** Exercise 2 (Inclusion-exclusion and symmetric difference)
Let \((\Omega, \mathcal{F}, \mathbb{P})\) be a probability space, and let \(A, B \in \mathcal{F}\).
1. Prove the addition law \(\mathbb{P}(A \cup B) = \mathbb{P}(A) + \mathbb{P}(B) - \mathbb{P}(A \cap B)\).
2. If \(\mathbb{P}(A) = 1\), prove that \(\mathbb{P}(A \cap B) = \mathbb{P}(B)\) for every event
   \(B \in \mathcal{F}\).

** Solution
1. We decompose \(A \cup B\) and \(B\) into disjoint measurable pieces:
   \[
   A \cup B = A \cup (B \setminus A) \quad \text{and} \quad B = (A \cap B) \cup (B \setminus A).
   \]
   By finite additivity,
   \[
   \mathbb{P}(A \cup B) = \mathbb{P}(A) + \mathbb{P}(B \setminus A) \quad \text{and} \quad
   \mathbb{P}(B) = \mathbb{P}(A \cap B) + \mathbb{P}(B \setminus A).
   \]
   Since all probabilities lie in \([0, 1]\) (hence are finite!), we can subtract
   \(\mathbb{P}(A \cap B)\) from the second equation to get
   \(\mathbb{P}(B \setminus A) = \mathbb{P}(B) - \mathbb{P}(A \cap B)\), and substitute this into the
   first equation to obtain \(\mathbb{P}(A \cup B) = \mathbb{P}(A) + \mathbb{P}(B) - \mathbb{P}(A \cap B)\).
2. Suppose \(\mathbb{P}(A) = 1\). Since \(A \subseteq A \cup B \subseteq \Omega\), monotonicity gives
   \(1 = \mathbb{P}(A) \le \mathbb{P}(A \cup B) \le \mathbb{P}(\Omega) = 1\), so
   \(\mathbb{P}(A \cup B) = 1\). Substituting \(\mathbb{P}(A) = 1\) and \(\mathbb{P}(A \cup B) = 1\)
   into part 1 gives \(1 = 1 + \mathbb{P}(B) - \mathbb{P}(A \cap B)\), i.e.,
   \(\mathbb{P}(A \cap B) = \mathbb{P}(B)\).

** Exercise 3 (Using continuity from below and above on intervals)
Let \(\mu\) be a finite Borel measure on \((\mathbb{R}, \mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}))\) (so
\(\mu(\mathbb{R}) < \infty\)), and define \(F(t) := \mu((-\infty, t])\) for \(t \in \mathbb{R}\).
1. Using continuity from below (Proposition 2.6(i)), prove that
   \(\lim_{t \to \infty} F(t) = \mu(\mathbb{R})\) and that \(\mu((-\infty, b)) = \lim_{n \to \infty} F(b - 1/n)\)
   for every \(b \in \mathbb{R}\).
2. Using continuity from above (Proposition 2.6(ii)), prove that \(F\) is right-continuous along
   sequences \(b + 1/n\): \(\lim_{n \to \infty} F(b + 1/n) = F(b)\), and deduce that
   \[
   \mu(\{b\}) = F(b) - \lim_{n \to \infty} F(b - 1/n).
   \]

** Solution
1. Since \(F(t) = \mu((-\infty, t])\) is increasing in \(t\) by monotonicity (Lemma 2.5), to find
   \(\lim_{t \to \infty} F(t)\) it suffices to check the increasing sequence of sets
   \(A_n := (-\infty, n]\). We have \(A_n \uparrow \mathbb{R}\), so Proposition 2.6(i) gives
   \(F(n) = \mu(A_n) \to \mu(\mathbb{R})\).
   Similarly, for any fixed \(b \in \mathbb{R}\), the sets \(E_n := (-\infty, b - 1/n]\) form an
   increasing sequence with \(\bigcup_{n=1}^\infty (-\infty, b - 1/n] = (-\infty, b)\) (as checked in
   Chapter 1, Section 1.4). Thus \(E_n \uparrow (-\infty, b)\), and Proposition 2.6(i) gives
   \(\mu((-\infty, b)) = \lim_{n \to \infty} \mu(E_n) = \lim_{n \to \infty} F(b - 1/n)\).
2. For fixed \(b \in \mathbb{R}\), the sets \(D_n := (-\infty, b + 1/n]\) form a decreasing sequence
   with \(\bigcap_{n=1}^\infty (-\infty, b + 1/n] = (-\infty, b]\), so \(D_n \downarrow (-\infty, b]\).
   Since \(\mu\) is a finite measure, \(\mu(D_1) \le \mu(\mathbb{R}) < \infty\). Therefore
   Proposition 2.6(ii) applies and gives
   \[
   \lim_{n \to \infty} F(b + 1/n) = \lim_{n \to \infty} \mu(D_n) = \mu((-\infty, b]) = F(b).
   \]
   Finally, decompose \((-\infty, b]\) into the disjoint union \((-\infty, b) \cup \{b\}\). By finite
   additivity and finiteness of \(\mu\),
   \[
   \mu(\{b\}) = \mu((-\infty, b]) - \mu((-\infty, b)) = F(b) - \lim_{n \to \infty} F(b - 1/n).
   \]
   (In particular, \(\mu(\{b\}) = 0\) if and only if \(F\) is continuous at \(b\)! We will return to
   this systematically in Chapter 12.)

** Exercise 4 (Constructing a probability measure on \(\mathbb{N}\) via geometric weights)
While Example 2.4 showed that no *uniform* probability measure exists on \(\mathbb{N}\), show that
setting \(p_n := 2^{-n}\) for \(n \in \mathbb{N}\) in Example 2.3 defines a valid probability measure
\(\mathbb{P}\) on \((\mathbb{N}, 2^{\mathbb{N}})\), and compute \(\mathbb{P}(D)\) for the set
\(D = \{2, 4, 6, \dots\}\) of all even positive integers.

** Solution
By Example 2.3, \(\mathbb{P}(A) = \sum_{n \in A} 2^{-n}\) is a measure on \((\mathbb{N}, 2^{\mathbb{N}})\).
Its total mass is the geometric series
\[
\mathbb{P}(\mathbb{N}) = \sum_{n=1}^\infty 2^{-n} = \frac{1/2}{1 - 1/2} = 1,
\]
so \(\mathbb{P}\) is a probability measure. For the set of even positive integers
\(D = \{2k : k \in \mathbb{N}\}\), we have
\[
\mathbb{P}(D) = \sum_{k=1}^\infty p_{2k} = \sum_{k=1}^\infty 2^{-2k} = \sum_{k=1}^\infty 4^{-k}
= \frac{1/4}{1 - 1/4} = \frac{1}{3}.
\]

** Exercise 5 (Characterizing countable additivity via finite additivity + continuity)
Let \((\Omega, \mathcal{F})\) be a measurable space, and suppose \(\mu : \mathcal{F} \to [0, \infty)\)
is a *finitely additive* set function (meaning \(\mu(\emptyset) = 0\) and
\(\mu(A \cup B) = \mu(A) + \mu(B)\) whenever \(A, B \in \mathcal{F}\) are disjoint). Prove that the
following three statements are equivalent:
1. \(\mu\) is countably additive (i.e. \(\mu\) is a finite measure).
2. \(\mu\) is continuous from below: whenever \(E_n \uparrow E\) in \(\mathcal{F}\),
   \(\mu(E_n) \to \mu(E)\).
3. \(\mu\) is continuous from above at \(\emptyset\): whenever \(D_n \downarrow \emptyset\) in
   \(\mathcal{F}\), \(\mu(D_n) \to 0\).

** Solution
- *\((1 \Rightarrow 2)\):* This is Proposition 2.6(i).
- *\((2 \Rightarrow 3)\):* Suppose \(D_n \downarrow \emptyset\). Then \(E_n := \Omega \setminus D_n\)
  satisfies \(E_n \uparrow \Omega\). By finite additivity on \(\Omega = D_n \cup E_n\) (and finiteness
  \(\mu(\Omega) < \infty\)), we have \(\mu(D_n) = \mu(\Omega) - \mu(E_n) \to \mu(\Omega) - \mu(\Omega) = 0\).
- *\((3 \Rightarrow 1)\):* Let \(A_1, A_2, \dots \in \mathcal{F}\) be pairwise disjoint, and set
  \(A := \bigcup_{k=1}^\infty A_k\). For each \(n \in \mathbb{N}\), split \(A\) into the finite union
  \(\bigcup_{k=1}^n A_k\) and the tail \(D_n := \bigcup_{k=n+1}^\infty A_k\). By finite additivity,
  \[
  \mu(A) = \mu\left(\bigcup_{k=1}^n A_k\right) + \mu(D_n) = \sum_{k=1}^n \mu(A_k) + \mu(D_n).
  \]
  Because the \(A_k\) are pairwise disjoint, any point \(\omega \in A\) belongs to a unique \(A_{k_0}\),
  and therefore \(\omega \notin D_n\) for all \(n \ge k_0\). Thus \(D_n \downarrow \emptyset\). By
  (3), \(\lim_{n \to \infty} \mu(D_n) = 0\), so taking \(n \to \infty\) gives
  \(\mu(A) = \lim_{n \to \infty} \sum_{k=1}^n \mu(A_k) + 0 = \sum_{k=1}^\infty \mu(A_k)\).

*Key takeaways*

- A \(\sigma\)-algebra \(\mathcal{F}\) tells us *which* sets are measurable; a measure \(\mu\) tells
  us *how big* they are. The same \((\Omega, \mathcal{F})\) can carry many measures.
- Never subtract \(\infty - \infty\)! When proving inequalities for measures, decompose larger sets
  into disjoint pieces (\(B = A \cup (B \setminus A)\)) and only subtract \(\mu(A)\) when you have
  explicitly checked \(\mu(A) < \infty\).
- Whenever you are handed an overlapping sequence of sets \(A_1, A_2, \dots\), think of the
  *disjointification trick*: \(B_1 = A_1\) and \(B_n = A_n \setminus \bigcup_{j=1}^{n-1} A_j\).
- Countable additivity applies to *countable* unions, not uncountable ones: \([0, 1]\) has Lebesgue
  measure \(1\) even though every single point \(\{x\} \subseteq [0, 1]\) has Lebesgue measure \(0\).
