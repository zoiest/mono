* Overview

This chapter is *not* part of the original lecture notes. It collects the background that the
lecture notes use silently, so that a reader who has never seen measure theory can follow every
step of Chapters 1--16.

Measure theory is, at heart, a careful bookkeeping of *sets*: which sets we may measure, how big they
are, and how sizes behave when we take unions, intersections and limits of sets. Probability theory
uses exactly the same bookkeeping: events are sets of outcomes, and "the probability that at least
one of the events happens" is the size of a union. So the tools below are the vocabulary of the whole
course.

*Roadmap.*
- 0.1 Sets and the algebra of sets (unions, intersections, complements, De Morgan's laws, indexed families).
- 0.2 Functions, images and preimages --- and why preimages are the "good" operation.
- 0.3 Countable versus uncountable sets (\(\mathbb{N}\), \(\mathbb{Q}\) countable; \(\mathbb{R}\) uncountable).
- 0.4 The extended half-line \([0,\infty]\) and its arithmetic.
- 0.5 Supremum and infimum.
- 0.6 \(\limsup\) and \(\liminf\) of sequences of numbers and of sequences of sets.
- 0.7 Series of nonnegative terms (the order of summation does not matter).
- 0.8 Basic topology of \(\mathbb{R}\): open and closed sets.
- 0.9 Indicator functions.

*What you need from earlier chapters.* Nothing: this is the starting point. You should be comfortable
with calculus-level limits and with reading simple proofs.

* Sets and the algebra of sets

** Membership and inclusion

A *set* is a collection of objects, called its *elements*. We write \(\omega\in A\) for "\(\omega\) is an
element of \(A\)" and \(\omega\notin A\) for the opposite. We write \(A\subseteq B\) ("\(A\) is a subset of \(B\)")
if every element of \(A\) is also an element of \(B\); equality \(A=B\) is allowed. Two sets are equal
exactly when \(A\subseteq B\) and \(B\subseteq A\). This gives the standard way to prove that two sets are equal:

#+begin_quote
*Method (double inclusion).* To prove \(A=B\), take an arbitrary \(x\in A\) and show \(x\in B\); then take an
arbitrary \(x\in B\) and show \(x\in A\).
#+end_quote

The *empty set* \(\emptyset\) has no elements; it is a subset of every set (there is nothing to check).
A *singleton* \(\{\omega\}\) is a set with exactly one element. Note the difference: \(\omega\) is an element,
\(\{\omega\}\) is a set.

Throughout we fix an *underlying set* \(\Omega\) (in probability: the *sample space*, the set of all
possible outcomes) and consider subsets \(A,B,\dots\subseteq\Omega\).

The *power set* \(2^{\Omega}\) is the set of all subsets of \(\Omega\). If \(\Omega\) has \(n\) elements, then
\(2^\Omega\) has \(2^n\) elements (each element is either in or out of a subset).

*Example 0.1.* For \(\Omega=\{1,2,3\}\):
\[
2^{\Omega}=\bigl\{\emptyset,\{1\},\{2\},\{3\},\{1,2\},\{1,3\},\{2,3\},\{1,2,3\}\bigr\},
\]
which has \(2^3=8\) members. Note \(1\in\Omega\), \(\{1\}\subseteq\Omega\), \(\{1\}\in 2^\Omega\), but \(1\notin 2^\Omega\).

** Set operations

For \(A,B\subseteq\Omega\):
- *union* \(A\cup B=\{\omega:\omega\in A\text{ or }\omega\in B\}\) (at least one);
- *intersection* \(A\cap B=\{\omega:\omega\in A\text{ and }\omega\in B\}\);
- *difference* \(A\setminus B=\{\omega\in A:\omega\notin B\}\);
- *complement* \(A^c=\Omega\setminus A\) (always relative to the fixed \(\Omega\));
- *symmetric difference* \(A\,\triangle\,B=(A\setminus B)\cup(B\setminus A)\) (in exactly one of them).

\(A\) and \(B\) are *disjoint* if \(A\cap B=\emptyset\). Note \(A\setminus B=A\cap B^c\).

In probability language: if the outcome is \(\omega\), the event \(A\) "occurs" when \(\omega\in A\). Then
\(A\cup B\) = "\(A\) or \(B\) occurs", \(A\cap B\) = "both occur", \(A^c\) = "\(A\) does not occur".

*Example 0.2 (one die).* \(\Omega=\{1,\dots,6\}\), \(E=\{2,4,6\}\) (even), \(L=\{1,2,3\}\) (low). Then
\(E\cup L=\{1,2,3,4,6\}\), \(E\cap L=\{2\}\), \(E\setminus L=\{4,6\}\), \(L\setminus E=\{1,3\}\), \(E^c=\{1,3,5\}\),
\(E\,\triangle\, L=\{1,3,4,6\}\).

** Laws of set algebra

#+begin_quote
*Proposition 0.3 (basic laws).* For \(A,B,C\subseteq\Omega\):
1. Commutativity and associativity of \(\cup\) and \(\cap\).
2. Distributivity: \(A\cap(B\cup C)=(A\cap B)\cup(A\cap C)\) and \(A\cup(B\cap C)=(A\cup B)\cap(A\cup C)\).
3. *De Morgan's laws:* \((A\cup B)^c=A^c\cap B^c\) and \((A\cap B)^c=A^c\cup B^c\).
4. \((A^c)^c=A\), \(\emptyset^c=\Omega\), \(\Omega^c=\emptyset\).
#+end_quote

/Proof of the first De Morgan law./ \(\omega\in(A\cup B)^c\) iff \(\omega\) is not in \(A\cup B\) iff \(\omega\) is in
neither \(A\) nor \(B\) iff \(\omega\in A^c\) and \(\omega\in B^c\) iff \(\omega\in A^c\cap B^c\). The second law follows by
applying the first to \(A^c,B^c\) and taking complements. \(\square\)

In words: "not (at least one)" = "none"; "not (all)" = "at least one fails".

** Indexed families

Often we have infinitely many sets \(A_1,A_2,\dots\) (a *sequence of sets*), or more generally a
family \((A_i)_{i\in I}\) indexed by any set \(I\). We define
\[
\bigcup_{i\in I}A_i=\{\omega:\ \omega\in A_i\text{ for at least one } i\in I\},\qquad
\bigcap_{i\in I}A_i=\{\omega:\ \omega\in A_i\text{ for every } i\in I\}.
\]
Convention: a union over an empty index set is \(\emptyset\) (and an intersection over an empty index set
is \(\Omega\)). A sequence is *pairwise disjoint* if \(A_m\cap A_n=\emptyset\) whenever \(m\neq n\).

#+begin_quote
*Proposition 0.4 (De Morgan, general form).* For any family \((A_i)_{i\in I}\),
\[
\Bigl(\bigcup_{i\in I}A_i\Bigr)^c=\bigcap_{i\in I}A_i^c,\qquad
\Bigl(\bigcap_{i\in I}A_i\Bigr)^c=\bigcup_{i\in I}A_i^c .
\]
Also \(B\cap\bigcup_i A_i=\bigcup_i (B\cap A_i)\) and \(B\cup\bigcap_i A_i=\bigcap_i(B\cup A_i)\).
#+end_quote

The proof is identical to the two-set case: "\(\omega\) is in no \(A_i\)" means "\(\omega\) is in every \(A_i^c\)".

*Example 0.5 (unions and intersections of intervals).* Work in \(\Omega=\mathbb{R}\).
1. \(\bigcup_{n=1}^\infty[1/n,1]=(0,1]\). If \(0<x\le 1\), pick \(n\) with \(1/n\le x\); then \(x\in[1/n,1]\).
   Conversely each \([1/n,1]\subseteq(0,1]\). Note the point \(0\) is never reached: a union of closed
   intervals need not be closed.
2. \(\bigcap_{n=1}^\infty(-1/n,1/n)=\{0\}\). Clearly \(0\) is in every interval. If \(x\neq 0\), choose \(n\) with
   \(1/n<|x|\); then \(x\notin(-1/n,1/n)\). An intersection of open intervals need not be open.
3. \(\bigcap_{n=1}^\infty(0,1/n)=\emptyset\). No positive number is below every \(1/n\). Decreasing nonempty sets
   can have empty intersection.
4. \(\bigcup_{n=1}^\infty[0,n]=[0,\infty)\) and \(\bigcap_{n=1}^\infty[n,\infty)=\emptyset\).

*Example 0.6 (making a sequence disjoint).* Given any sets \(A_1,A_2,\dots\), define \(B_1=A_1\) and
\(B_n=A_n\setminus(A_1\cup\dots\cup A_{n-1})\). Then the \(B_n\) are pairwise disjoint, \(B_n\subseteq A_n\), and
\(\bigcup_n B_n=\bigcup_n A_n\): each point of the union is placed in \(B_n\) for the /first/ \(n\) with
\(\omega\in A_n\). This trick is used repeatedly in Chapter 2.

*Common confusions.*
- \(\in\) versus \(\subseteq\): \(\{2\}\subseteq\{1,2\}\) but \(\{2\}\notin\{1,2\}\).
- Complements depend on \(\Omega\): the complement of \(\{2,4,6\}\) is \(\{1,3,5\}\) inside \(\{1,\dots,6\}\), but
  would be different inside \(\mathbb{N}\).
- "Infinitely many operations" can produce sets of a different nature (Example 0.5): this is exactly why
  measure theory insists on *countable* operations.

* Functions, images and preimages

A *function* (or *map*) \(f:X\to Y\) assigns to every \(x\in X\) exactly one value \(f(x)\in Y\). \(X\) is the
*domain*, \(Y\) the *target* (codomain).

#+begin_quote
*Definition 0.7 (image and preimage).* For \(A\subseteq X\) and \(B\subseteq Y\):
\[
f(A)=\{f(x): x\in A\}\subseteq Y,\qquad f^{-1}(B)=\{x\in X: f(x)\in B\}\subseteq X .
\]
#+end_quote

The notation \(f^{-1}(B)\) does *not* require \(f\) to have an inverse function; it is defined for every \(f\)
and every \(B\subseteq Y\). It is the set of inputs that land in \(B\).

*Example 0.8.* Let \(f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}\), \(f(x)=x^2\).
- \(f^{-1}([0,4])=[-2,2]\), \(f^{-1}([-5,-1])=\emptyset\), \(f^{-1}(\{1\})=\{-1,1\}\).
- \(f([-1,2])=[0,4]\).

*Example 0.9 (die).* \(\Omega=\{1,\dots,6\}\), \(X(\omega)=\omega \bmod 2\) (\(1\) if odd, \(0\) if even). Then
\(X^{-1}(\{0\})=\{2,4,6\}\), \(X^{-1}(\{1\})=\{1,3,5\}\), \(X^{-1}(\{0,1\})=\Omega\), \(X^{-1}(\{7\})=\emptyset\).

** Preimages respect every set operation

#+begin_quote
*Proposition 0.10.* For any \(f:X\to Y\), any \(B,C\subseteq Y\) and any family \((B_i)_{i\in I}\) of subsets of \(Y\):
\[
f^{-1}\Bigl(\bigcup_i B_i\Bigr)=\bigcup_i f^{-1}(B_i),\quad
f^{-1}\Bigl(\bigcap_i B_i\Bigr)=\bigcap_i f^{-1}(B_i),\quad
f^{-1}(Y\setminus B)=X\setminus f^{-1}(B),
\]
and \(f^{-1}(Y)=X\), \(f^{-1}(\emptyset)=\emptyset\). In particular, if \(B\cap C=\emptyset\) then
\(f^{-1}(B)\cap f^{-1}(C)=\emptyset\).
#+end_quote

/Proof./ Each identity is a translation of the definition. For example, \(x\in f^{-1}(\bigcup_i B_i)\) iff
\(f(x)\in B_i\) for some \(i\) iff \(x\in f^{-1}(B_i)\) for some \(i\). And \(x\in f^{-1}(Y\setminus B)\) iff \(f(x)\notin B\)
iff \(x\notin f^{-1}(B)\). The others are similar. \(\square\)

** Images do not behave as well

#+begin_quote
*Proposition 0.11.* \(f(\bigcup_i A_i)=\bigcup_i f(A_i)\) always holds, but in general only
\(f(A\cap B)\subseteq f(A)\cap f(B)\), and \(f(X\setminus A)\) need not equal \(Y\setminus f(A)\).
#+end_quote

*Counterexample.* With \(f(x)=x^2\), \(A=[-1,0]\), \(B=[0,1]\): \(A\cap B=\{0\}\), so \(f(A\cap B)=\{0\}\), but
\(f(A)\cap f(B)=[0,1]\cap[0,1]=[0,1]\). Also \(f(\mathbb{R}\setminus A)=f((0,\infty))=(0,\infty)\), while
\(\mathbb{R}\setminus f(A)=\mathbb{R}\setminus[0,1]\). The trouble: two different inputs can share an output, and
some outputs are never hit.

*Why this matters.* Because preimages commute with complements and countable unions, the definition of
a *measurable function* (Chapter 3) is phrased with preimages: "the preimage of every measurable set is
measurable". With images, nothing would work.

#+begin_quote
*Proposition 0.12 (composition).* If \(f:X\to Y\) and \(g:Y\to Z\), then for \(C\subseteq Z\),
\((g\circ f)^{-1}(C)=f^{-1}\bigl(g^{-1}(C)\bigr)\).
#+end_quote

/Proof./ \(x\in(g\circ f)^{-1}(C)\) iff \(g(f(x))\in C\) iff \(f(x)\in g^{-1}(C)\) iff \(x\in f^{-1}(g^{-1}(C))\). \(\square\)

*Common confusions.*
- \(f^{-1}(f(A))\supseteq A\), and it can be strictly larger: with \(f(x)=x^2\), \(f^{-1}(f(\{1\}))=\{-1,1\}\).
- \(f(f^{-1}(B))\subseteq B\), and it can be strictly smaller: \(f(f^{-1}([-1,1]))=[0,1]\).

* Countable and uncountable sets

#+begin_quote
*Definition 0.13.* A set \(A\) is *countable* if it is finite or its elements can be listed in a sequence
\(a_1,a_2,a_3,\dots\) (equivalently: there is a surjection \(\mathbb{N}\to A\), or \(A=\emptyset\)). A set that is not
countable is *uncountable*. Here \(\mathbb{N}=\{1,2,3,\dots\}\).
#+end_quote

In these notes (as in the lecture notes) "countable" *includes* "finite".

*Example 0.14 (countable sets).*
1. \(\mathbb{N}\) itself; any subset of \(\mathbb{N}\) (list its elements in increasing order); in fact any subset of a
   countable set is countable.
2. \(\mathbb{Z}\): list it as \(0,1,-1,2,-2,3,-3,\dots\).
3. \(\mathbb{N}\times\mathbb{N}\): list pairs by increasing sum \(m+n\):
   \((1,1);\ (1,2),(2,1);\ (1,3),(2,2),(3,1);\ \dots\). Each sum-level is finite, so every pair appears.
#+caption: Figure 0.1: Pairing Function Enumerating $\mathbb{N} \times \mathbb{N}$

#+ATTR_HTML: :width 600px :align center
[[file:figures/fig00_pairing.png]]
4. \(\mathbb{Q}\): every rational is \(p/q\) with \(p\in\mathbb{Z}\), \(q\in\mathbb{N}\); the map \((p,q)\mapsto p/q\) from the countable
   set \(\mathbb{Z}\times\mathbb{N}\) onto \(\mathbb{Q}\) gives a listing (skip repetitions).
5. The set of all pairs \((p,q)\) of rationals, and the set of all intervals with rational endpoints.

#+begin_quote
*Proposition 0.15.* A countable union of countable sets is countable.
#+end_quote

/Proof sketch./ If \(A_m=\{a_{m,1},a_{m,2},\dots\}\) for \(m\in\mathbb{N}\), then \((m,n)\mapsto a_{m,n}\) maps the countable set
\(\mathbb{N}\times\mathbb{N}\) onto \(\bigcup_m A_m\). (Finite \(A_m\) can be padded by repetition; empty ones skipped.) \(\square\)

#+begin_quote
*Theorem 0.16 (Cantor).* The interval \([0,1)\), and hence \(\mathbb{R}\), is uncountable.
#+end_quote

/Proof sketch (diagonal argument)./ Suppose \(x_1,x_2,\dots\) were a list of all numbers in \([0,1)\). Write
each in decimal: \(x_n=0.d_{n1}d_{n2}d_{n3}\dots\). Build a new number \(y=0.e_1e_2e_3\dots\) by
\(e_n=5\) if \(d_{nn}\neq 5\) and \(e_n=4\) if \(d_{nn}=5\). Then \(y\in[0,1)\), but \(y\) differs from \(x_n\) in the
\(n\)-th digit for every \(n\). Because \(y\) uses only digits \(4\) and \(5\), it has a unique decimal expansion
(no trailing \(9\)'s or \(0\)'s ambiguity), so \(y\neq x_n\) for all \(n\): the list missed \(y\). Contradiction. \(\square\)

*Example 0.17 (infinite coin tossing is uncountable).* The set \(\{0,1\}^{\mathbb{N}}\) of all infinite 0--1
sequences is uncountable: the same diagonal trick (flip the \(n\)-th digit of the \(n\)-th sequence) produces
a sequence not in any proposed list. Equivalently, the power set \(2^{\mathbb{N}}\) is uncountable (identify a
subset \(S\subseteq\mathbb{N}\) with the sequence whose \(n\)-th digit is \(1\) iff \(n\in S\)). This space appears in
Chapter 4 as the sample space of infinitely many coin tosses.

*Why this matters.* Measures are *countably* additive. The interval \([0,1]\) is an uncountable union of
singletons, each of length zero, yet its length is one. There is no contradiction, precisely because
uncountable unions are not covered by the axioms (Chapter 2, Example 2.8).

* The extended half-line \([0,\infty]\)

Measures can be infinite (the length of \(\mathbb{R}\) is infinite). So we work in
\([0,\infty]=[0,\infty)\cup\{\infty\}\), where \(\infty\) is a new symbol, larger than every real number.

#+begin_quote
*Conventions in \([0,\infty]\).* For \(a\in[0,\infty]\):
- order: \(a\le\infty\);
- addition: \(a+\infty=\infty+a=\infty\);
- multiplication: \(a\cdot\infty=\infty\cdot a=\infty\) if \(a>0\), and \(0\cdot\infty=\infty\cdot 0=0\);
- \(\infty-\infty\) is *undefined*; \(\infty-a=\infty\) for finite \(a\) is allowed but we avoid subtraction whenever
  an infinite quantity could be involved.
#+end_quote

*Why \(0\cdot\infty=0\)?* In integration (Chapter 6), the integral of the function that is \(0\) on all of
\(\mathbb{R}\) should be \(0\), and \(0\cdot\lambda(\mathbb{R})=0\cdot\infty\). Similarly, the function equal to \(\infty\) on a set of length
\(0\) should have integral \(\infty\cdot 0=0\). The convention makes "area = height \(\times\) width" consistent.

*Why is \(\infty-\infty\) undefined?* Because cancellation fails: \(1+\infty=2+\infty\), but \(1\neq 2\). Any value we
assigned to \(\infty-\infty\) would contradict some reasonable identity. This is why, e.g., the proof of
continuity from above (Proposition 2.6) needs a finiteness assumption before subtracting.

*Facts that do hold.* Addition in \([0,\infty]\) is commutative and associative; if \(a\le b\) then \(a+c\le b+c\);
every subset of \([0,\infty]\) has a supremum in \([0,\infty]\) (Section 0.5); every increasing sequence in
\([0,\infty]\) converges in \([0,\infty]\) to its supremum.

*Common confusions.* \(a+c=b+c\) does *not* imply \(a=b\) if \(c=\infty\). \(\infty\) is not a real number; "\(x_n\to\infty\)"
in \([0,\infty]\) is genuine convergence to the element \(\infty\).

* Supremum and infimum

#+begin_quote
*Definition 0.18.* Let \(S\subseteq\mathbb{R}\). A number \(u\) is an *upper bound* of \(S\) if \(s\le u\) for all \(s\in S\). The
*supremum* \(\sup S\) is the least upper bound: an upper bound \(u\) such that no smaller number is an upper
bound. Similarly the *infimum* \(\inf S\) is the greatest lower bound.
#+end_quote

*Completeness axiom of \(\mathbb{R}\):* every nonempty set bounded above has a supremum in \(\mathbb{R}\). We extend:
\(\sup S=\infty\) if \(S\) is not bounded above, \(\inf S=-\infty\) if not bounded below. For subsets of \([0,\infty]\)
the supremum always exists in \([0,\infty]\), and we use the convention \(\sup\emptyset=0\) there (e.g. "a sum
with no terms is zero").

#+begin_quote
*Proposition 0.19 (\(\varepsilon\)-characterization).* For \(S\subseteq\mathbb{R}\) nonempty and bounded above, \(u=\sup S\) iff
(a) \(s\le u\) for all \(s\in S\), and (b) for every \(\varepsilon>0\) there is \(s\in S\) with \(s>u-\varepsilon\).
#+end_quote

(b) says that \(u-\varepsilon\) is not an upper bound, i.e. no smaller number works.

*Example 0.20.*
1. \(S=\{1-1/n:n\in\mathbb{N}\}\): \(\sup S=1\) (not attained, not a maximum), \(\inf S=\min S=0\).
2. \(S=(0,1)\): \(\sup S=1\), \(\inf S=0\), neither attained.
3. \(S=\mathbb{N}\): \(\sup S=\infty\), \(\inf S=1\).
4. \(S=\{x\in\mathbb{Q}: x^2<2\}\): \(\sup S=\sqrt2\notin\mathbb{Q}\) --- the supremum need not lie in \(S\), nor even in \(\mathbb{Q}\).

#+begin_quote
*Proposition 0.21 (monotone convergence for numbers).* An increasing sequence \(x_1\le x_2\le\cdots\) of
real numbers converges to \(\sup_n x_n\) (which may be \(\infty\)). A decreasing sequence converges to \(\inf_n x_n\).
#+end_quote

/Proof./ Let \(u=\sup_n x_n<\infty\) and \(\varepsilon>0\). By Proposition 0.19 there is \(N\) with \(x_N>u-\varepsilon\), and then
\(u-\varepsilon<x_N\le x_n\le u\) for all \(n\ge N\). If \(u=\infty\), for every \(M\) some \(x_N>M\), hence all later terms
exceed \(M\). \(\square\)

* \(\limsup\) and \(\liminf\)

** For sequences of numbers

A sequence need not converge, but we can always record its "eventual highest" and "eventual lowest"
levels. For a real sequence \((x_n)\) put \(s_n=\sup_{k\ge n}x_k\) (decreasing in \(n\)) and \(i_n=\inf_{k\ge n}x_k\)
(increasing in \(n\)).

#+begin_quote
*Definition 0.22.*
\[
\limsup_{n\to\infty}x_n=\inf_{n\ge1}\,\sup_{k\ge n}x_k=\lim_{n\to\infty}s_n,\qquad
\liminf_{n\to\infty}x_n=\sup_{n\ge1}\,\inf_{k\ge n}x_k=\lim_{n\to\infty}i_n ,
\]
with values in \([-\infty,\infty]\).
#+end_quote

Both always exist (monotone sequences converge, Proposition 0.21). Always \(\liminf x_n\le\limsup x_n\), and
\((x_n)\) converges to \(L\in[-\infty,\infty]\) iff \(\liminf x_n=\limsup x_n=L\).

*Example 0.23.*
1. \(x_n=(-1)^n\): \(s_n=1\), \(i_n=-1\), so \(\limsup=1\), \(\liminf=-1\): no limit.
2. \(x_n=(-1)^n(1+1/n)\): \(\limsup=1\), \(\liminf=-1\) (the values approach \(\pm1\) from outside).
3. \(x_n=1/n\): \(\limsup=\liminf=0\), the limit.
4. \(x_n=n\) for even \(n\), \(x_n=0\) for odd \(n\): \(\limsup=\infty\), \(\liminf=0\).

Interpretation: \(\limsup x_n\) is the largest value that the sequence approaches along some subsequence; for
every \(\varepsilon>0\), \(x_n>\limsup x_n-\varepsilon\) infinitely often, and \(x_n<\limsup x_n+\varepsilon\) eventually.

** For sequences of sets

#+begin_quote
*Definition 0.24.* For sets \(A_1,A_2,\dots\subseteq\Omega\):
\[
\limsup_{n\to\infty}A_n=\bigcap_{n=1}^\infty\bigcup_{k=n}^\infty A_k,\qquad
\liminf_{n\to\infty}A_n=\bigcup_{n=1}^\infty\bigcap_{k=n}^\infty A_k .
\]
#+end_quote

Unpack: \(\omega\in\limsup A_n\) iff for every \(n\) there is \(k\ge n\) with \(\omega\in A_k\) iff \(\omega\) belongs to
*infinitely many* \(A_k\) ("\(A_n\) infinitely often", abbreviated \(A_n\) i.o.). And \(\omega\in\liminf A_n\) iff there is
\(n\) with \(\omega\in A_k\) for all \(k\ge n\) iff \(\omega\) belongs to *all but finitely many* \(A_k\) ("\(A_n\) eventually").

Properties:
- \(\liminf A_n\subseteq\limsup A_n\) (eventually implies infinitely often).
- \((\limsup A_n)^c=\liminf A_n^c\) (by De Morgan: "not infinitely often in \(A_n\)" = "eventually in \(A_n^c\)").
- If \(A_n\) is increasing, both equal \(\bigcup_n A_n\); if decreasing, both equal \(\bigcap_n A_n\).
- \(\mathbf 1_{\limsup A_n}=\limsup\mathbf 1_{A_n}\) and \(\mathbf 1_{\liminf A_n}=\liminf\mathbf 1_{A_n}\) pointwise (Section 0.9).

*Example 0.25.*
1. Alternating sets: \(A_n=A\) for odd \(n\), \(A_n=B\) for even \(n\). Then \(\limsup A_n=A\cup B\),
   \(\liminf A_n=A\cap B\).
2. Coin tossing: let \(\Omega=\{0,1\}^{\mathbb{N}}\) and \(A_n=\{\omega:\omega_n=1\}\) (toss \(n\) is heads). Then \(\limsup A_n\) is the
   set of sequences with infinitely many ones, and \(\liminf A_n\) the set of sequences that are eventually
   all ones. (Chapter 4 shows the first has probability one for fair coins.)
3. \(A_n=[0,1/n]\subseteq\mathbb{R}\): decreasing, so \(\limsup A_n=\liminf A_n=\{0\}\).
4. \(A_n=\{n\}\subseteq\mathbb{N}\): every \(m\) lies in only one \(A_n\), so \(\limsup A_n=\liminf A_n=\emptyset\).

* Series of nonnegative terms

#+begin_quote
*Definition 0.26.* For \(a_n\in[0,\infty]\),
\(\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^\infty a_n:=\sup_{N\in\mathbb{N}}\sum_{n=1}^N a_n\in[0,\infty]\).
#+end_quote

Since the terms are \(\ge0\), the partial sums increase, so the supremum is also their limit. The sum is
*always defined* (possibly \(\infty\)); there are no convergence issues to worry about.

*Example 0.27.* \(\sum_{n\ge1}2^{-n}=1\) (partial sums \(1-2^{-N}\)); \(\sum_{n\ge1}1/n=\infty\) (harmonic series);
\(\sum_{n\ge1}1/n^2=\pi^2/6<\infty\); \(\sum_{n\ge1}0=0\); \(\sum_n a_n=\infty\) if some \(a_n=\infty\).

#+begin_quote
*Proposition 0.28 (order does not matter).* Let \(a_n\in[0,\infty]\) and let \(\pi:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{N}\) be a bijection
(a rearrangement). Then \(\sum_n a_{\pi(n)}=\sum_n a_n\). More generally, for a countable index set \(I\) and
\(a_i\ge0\), the value
\[
\sum_{i\in I}a_i:=\sup\Bigl\{\sum_{i\in F}a_i:\ F\subseteq I\text{ finite}\Bigr\}
\]
equals \(\sum_n a_{i_n}\) for any listing \(i_1,i_2,\dots\) of \(I\) without repetitions.
#+end_quote

/Proof./ Fix a listing. Every partial sum \(\sum_{n\le N}a_{i_n}\) is a sum over a finite set, so it is at most the
supremum over finite sets. Conversely, every finite \(F\subseteq I\) is contained in \(\{i_1,\dots,i_N\}\) for \(N\) large;
since terms are nonnegative, \(\sum_{i\in F}a_i\le\sum_{n\le N}a_{i_n}\). So both suprema coincide, and the value does
not depend on the listing. \(\square\)

*Contrast (signed terms).* For the alternating harmonic series \(1-\tfrac12+\tfrac13-\cdots=\ln2\), a rearrangement
can produce any real value (Riemann's rearrangement theorem). Nonnegativity is what makes order irrelevant.
This is why measures (which add nonnegative numbers) cause no ordering problems.

#+begin_quote
*Proposition 0.29 (double series, nonnegative terms).* If \(a_{m,n}\in[0,\infty]\), then
\(\sum_m\sum_n a_{m,n}=\sum_n\sum_m a_{m,n}=\sum_{(m,n)\in\mathbb{N}^2}a_{m,n}\).
#+end_quote

/Proof sketch./ Each iterated sum is a supremum of finite sums over rectangles \(\{1..M\}\times\{1..N\}\), and
every finite subset of \(\mathbb{N}^2\) lies in such a rectangle; apply Proposition 0.28. \(\square\)

* Basic topology of \(\mathbb{R}\)

#+begin_quote
*Definition 0.30.* A set \(U\subseteq\mathbb{R}\) is *open* if for every \(x\in U\) there is \(\varepsilon>0\) with
\((x-\varepsilon,x+\varepsilon)\subseteq U\). A set \(C\subseteq\mathbb{R}\) is *closed* if its complement is open. Equivalently, \(C\) is closed iff
it contains the limit of every convergent sequence of its points.
#+end_quote

*Examples.* Open: \((a,b)\), \((a,\infty)\), \(\mathbb{R}\), \(\emptyset\), \((0,1)\cup(2,3)\). Closed: \([a,b]\), \([a,\infty)\), \(\{x\}\),
\(\mathbb{Z}\), \(\mathbb{R}\), \(\emptyset\). Neither: \([0,1)\) (not open at \(0\); not closed since \(1-1/n\to1\notin[0,1)\)), \(\mathbb{Q}\) (every
interval contains irrationals and rationals). \(\mathbb{R}\) and \(\emptyset\) are both open and closed.

Facts: any union of open sets is open; a *finite* intersection of open sets is open; an infinite
intersection need not be (Example 0.5(2)). Dually for closed sets.

*Density of the rationals.* Between any two reals \(a<b\) there is a rational \(q\) with \(a<q<b\). (Choose \(n\)
with \(1/n<b-a\); some multiple \(m/n\) lands in \((a,b)\).)

#+begin_quote
*Theorem 0.31.* Every open set \(U\subseteq\mathbb{R}\) is a countable union of open intervals; in fact
\[
U=\bigcup\{(p,q):\ p,q\in\mathbb{Q},\ p<q,\ (p,q)\subseteq U\}.
\]
#+end_quote

/Proof./ "\(\supseteq\)": each interval in the union lies in \(U\). "\(\subseteq\)": let \(x\in U\). Openness gives \(\varepsilon>0\) with
\((x-\varepsilon,x+\varepsilon)\subseteq U\). By density pick rationals \(p\in(x-\varepsilon,x)\) and \(q\in(x,x+\varepsilon)\); then
\(x\in(p,q)\subseteq U\). The family of rational pairs is countable (Example 0.14(5)), so the union is countable. \(\square\)

(One can even take the intervals pairwise disjoint --- the "connected components" of \(U\) --- but we do not
need this.) This theorem is the reason the Borel \(\sigma\)-algebra can be generated by countably many sets
(Chapter 1, Section 1.4).

*Continuity via preimages.* \(f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}\) is continuous iff \(f^{-1}(U)\) is open for every open \(U\). This preimage
formulation is the model for the definition of measurable functions (Chapter 3).

* Indicator functions

#+begin_quote
*Definition 0.32.* For \(A\subseteq\Omega\), the *indicator function* \(\mathbf 1_A:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}\) is
\[
\mathbf 1_A(\omega)=\begin{cases}1,&\omega\in A,\\0,&\omega\notin A.\end{cases}
\]
#+end_quote

Indicators turn set operations into arithmetic:
\[
\begin{aligned}
\mathbf 1_{A\cap B}&=\mathbf 1_A\mathbf 1_B=\min(\mathbf 1_A,\mathbf 1_B), &
\mathbf 1_{A^c}&=1-\mathbf 1_A, \\
\mathbf 1_{A\cup B}&=\mathbf 1_A+\mathbf 1_B-\mathbf 1_{A\cap B}=\max(\mathbf 1_A,\mathbf 1_B), &
A\subseteq B&\iff \mathbf 1_A\le\mathbf 1_B .
\end{aligned}
\]
If \(A_1,A_2,\dots\) are pairwise disjoint, \(\mathbf 1_{\bigcup_n A_n}=\sum_n\mathbf 1_{A_n}\) (at each point at most one term
is \(1\)). For arbitrary sets, \(\sum_n\mathbf 1_{A_n}(\omega)\) *counts* how many \(A_n\) contain \(\omega\); thus
\(\omega\in\limsup A_n\) iff \(\sum_n\mathbf 1_{A_n}(\omega)=\infty\).

*Preimages of an indicator.* For \(B\subseteq\mathbb{R}\), \(\mathbf 1_A^{-1}(B)\) is \(\emptyset\), \(A\), \(A^c\) or \(\Omega\), according as \(B\)
contains neither, only \(1\), only \(0\), or both of the values \(0,1\).

*Example 0.33 (verifying a set identity with indicators).* To check De Morgan:
\(\mathbf 1_{(A\cup B)^c}=1-\max(\mathbf 1_A,\mathbf 1_B)=\min(1-\mathbf 1_A,1-\mathbf 1_B)=\mathbf 1_{A^c\cap B^c}\).

*Example 0.34 (probability preview).* For a fair die and \(E=\{2,4,6\}\), the random quantity \(\mathbf 1_E\) is
\(1\) if the roll is even and \(0\) otherwise; its average value is \(\tfrac16(0+1+0+1+0+1)=\tfrac12=P(E)\). In
general \(\mathbb E[\mathbf 1_A]=P(A)\) (Chapter 3).

* Worked Exercises

** Exercise 1
Show that \(A\setminus(B\cup C)=(A\setminus B)\cap(A\setminus C)\).

*** Solution
\(A\setminus(B\cup C)=A\cap(B\cup C)^c=A\cap B^c\cap C^c=(A\cap B^c)\cap(A\cap C^c)\), using De Morgan and \(A\cap A=A\).

** Exercise 2
Compute \(\bigcup_{n\ge1}[0,1-1/n]\) and \(\bigcap_{n\ge1}[0,1+1/n)\).

*** Solution
The union is \([0,1)\): every \(x\in[0,1)\) satisfies \(x\le 1-1/n\) for large \(n\), and \(1\) is never included.
The intersection is \([0,1]\): \(1<1+1/n\) for all \(n\), while any \(x>1\) fails \(x<1+1/n\) for large \(n\); and
negative numbers are never included.

** Exercise 3
Let \(f:\{1,\dots,6\}\to\mathbb{R}\), \(f(\omega)=(\omega-3)^2\). Find \(f^{-1}(\{1\})\), \(f^{-1}((-\infty,1])\), \(f(\{2,4\})\),
and check that \(f(\{2\}\cap\{4\})\neq f(\{2\})\cap f(\{4\})\).

*** Solution
Values: \(f(1)=4,f(2)=1,f(3)=0,f(4)=1,f(5)=4,f(6)=9\). So \(f^{-1}(\{1\})=\{2,4\}\),
\(f^{-1}((-\infty,1])=\{2,3,4\}\), \(f(\{2,4\})=\{1\}\). And \(f(\emptyset)=\emptyset\neq\{1\}=f(\{2\})\cap f(\{4\})\).

** Exercise 4
Show that the set of all finite subsets of \(\mathbb{N}\) is countable, whereas \(2^{\mathbb{N}}\) is not.

*** Solution
For each \(n\), the subsets of \(\{1,\dots,n\}\) form a finite family \(\mathcal F_n\) (with \(2^n\) members). Every finite
subset of \(\mathbb{N}\) lies in some \(\mathcal F_n\), so the family of finite subsets is \(\bigcup_n\mathcal F_n\), a countable union
of finite sets, hence countable (Proposition 0.15). For \(2^{\mathbb{N}}\) see Example 0.17: given a list
\(S_1,S_2,\dots\), the set \(T=\{n: n\notin S_n\}\) differs from every \(S_n\) at the element \(n\).

** Exercise 5
Let \(A_n=[0,1]\) for even \(n\) and \(A_n=[1,2]\) for odd \(n\). Find \(\limsup A_n\) and \(\liminf A_n\). Also compute
\(\limsup x_n\) and \(\liminf x_n\) for \(x_n=\sin(n\pi/2)\).

*** Solution
By Example 0.25(1), \(\limsup A_n=[0,2]\), \(\liminf A_n=\{1\}\). The sequence \(x_n\) is \(1,0,-1,0,1,0,-1,\dots\), so
\(\sup_{k\ge n}x_k=1\) and \(\inf_{k\ge n}x_k=-1\) for every \(n\): \(\limsup x_n=1\), \(\liminf x_n=-1\).

** Exercise 6
In \([0,\infty]\), evaluate (or explain why undefined): \(3+\infty\), \(0\cdot\infty\), \(\sum_{n\ge1}\infty\cdot 2^{-n}\),
\(\sum_{n\ge1}0\cdot\infty\), \(\infty-\infty\).

*** Solution
\(3+\infty=\infty\); \(0\cdot\infty=0\); each term \(\infty\cdot2^{-n}=\infty\), so the sum is \(\infty\); each term \(0\cdot\infty=0\), so the
sum is \(0\); \(\infty-\infty\) is undefined (cancellation fails: \(1+\infty=2+\infty\)).

** Exercise 7
Show that \(\mathbf 1_{A\triangle B}=|\mathbf 1_A-\mathbf 1_B|=\mathbf 1_A+\mathbf 1_B-2\mathbf 1_A\mathbf 1_B\).

*** Solution
Check the four cases \((\mathbf 1_A,\mathbf 1_B)\in\{(0,0),(0,1),(1,0),(1,1)\}\): all three expressions give \(0,1,1,0\)
respectively, which is exactly "in exactly one of \(A,B\)".

* Summary

#+caption: Toolkit cheat-sheet

| Concept                | Statement                                                                          | Why it matters                                             |
|------------------------+------------------------------------------------------------------------------------+------------------------------------------------------------|
| De Morgan              | \((\bigcup A_i)^c=\bigcap A_i^c\), \((\bigcap A_i)^c=\bigcup A_i^c\)                     | Turns unions into intersections; used in every chapter     |
| Preimage               | \(f^{-1}(B)=\{x: f(x)\in B\}\) commutes with all set operations                      | Basis of measurable functions                              |
| Image                  | \(f(A\cap B)\subseteq f(A)\cap f(B)\), equality may fail                             | Why we never define measurability by images                |

| Uncountable            | \(\mathbb{R}\), \([0,1]\), \(\{0,1\}^{\mathbb{N}}\) (diagonal argument)                               | Points of length zero can fill a set of length one         |
| \([0,\infty]\)         | \(a+\infty=\infty\), \(0\cdot\infty=0\), \(\infty-\infty\) undefined                               | Values of measures and integrals                           |
| sup / inf              | least upper / greatest lower bound; increasing sequences converge to sup           | Series, limits, outer measure                              |
| limsup of sets         | \(\bigcap_n\bigcup_{k\ge n}A_k\) = infinitely often                                    | Borel--Cantelli, strong law                                |
| liminf of sets         | \(\bigcup_n\bigcap_{k\ge n}A_k\) = eventually                                          | Complement of limsup of complements                        |
| Nonnegative series     | sum = sup of partial sums, order irrelevant                                        | Countable additivity is well-defined                       |
| Open sets of \(\mathbb{R}\)    | countable unions of rational open intervals                                        | Borel sets are countably generated                         |
| Indicator              | \(\mathbf 1_{A\cap B}=\mathbf 1_A\mathbf 1_B\), \(\mathbf 1_{A^c}=1-\mathbf 1_A\)                     | Events become random variables; \(\mathbb E\mathbf 1_A=P(A)\)  |

*Key takeaways.*
- Prove set identities by double inclusion or with indicators.
- Preimages are perfectly behaved; images are not.
- "Countable" includes finite; \(\mathbb{Q}\) is countable, \(\mathbb{R}\) is not.
- In \([0,\infty]\) we may add freely but must not subtract infinities; \(0\cdot\infty=0\).
- Nonnegative series always have a value in \([0,\infty]\), independent of the order of terms.
- \(\limsup A_n\) = "infinitely often", \(\liminf A_n\) = "eventually".
