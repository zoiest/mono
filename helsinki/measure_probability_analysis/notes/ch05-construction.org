* Overview

** What problem does this chapter solve?

Knowing from Chapter 4 that a measure is *unique* does not tell us that it *exists*. When we build a
measure or probability model, we can only write down explicit formulas for the sizes of "elementary"
sets:
- intervals \((a,b]\) on \(\mathbb R\) (where length is \(b-a\)),
- rectangles \(A \times B\) in a product space (where area is \(\mu(A)\nu(B)\)),
- cylinder events in \(\{0,1\}^{\mathbb N}\) determined by finitely many coin tosses.

These elementary sets (or finite disjoint unions of them) form an **algebra** \(\mathcal A\) — closed
under complements and *finite* unions, but not countable unions. How do we extend a size function
\(\mu_0\) from an algebra \(\mathcal A\) to the entire generated \(\sigma\)-algebra
\(\sigma(\mathcal A)\) without losing countable additivity?

This chapter carries out **Carathéodory's extension machine** in three clean stages, generalizing the
construction of Lebesgue measure on \(\mathbb R\) to an arbitrary abstract set \(\Omega\) with no geometry
required:
1. *Premeasure on an algebra (\(\mu_0\) on \(\mathcal A\)):* Check that the initial assignment \(\mu_0\)
   is countably additive whenever a countable disjoint union happens to land back inside \(\mathcal A\)
   (Definition 5.2, Proposition 5.5).
2. *Outer measure on all subsets (\(\mu^{\ast}\) on \(2^\Omega\)):* Cover any subset \(E \subseteq \Omega\) by
   countable families of sets from \(\mathcal A\) and take the infimum of their total sizes
   (Definition 5.6, Lemma 5.7). This defines \(\mu^*(E)\) for *every* subset of \(\Omega\), but \(\mu^*\)
   is only countably *subadditive*, not additive on all subsets (Example 5.8).
3. *Carathéodory measurability (\(\mathcal M\)):* Isolate the sets \(E \subseteq \Omega\) that cleanly split
   every test set \(T \subseteq \Omega\) additively:
   \[
   \mu^*(T) = \mu^*(T \cap E) + \mu^*(T \setminus E).
   \]
   Miraculously, the collection \(\mathcal M\) of such sets is a \(\sigma\)-algebra containing
   \(\mathcal A\) (hence containing \(\sigma(\mathcal A)\)), and \(\mu^*\) restricted to \(\mathcal M\)
   is a countably additive measure extending \(\mu_0\) (Theorems 5.9 and 5.10)!

Finally, in Section 5.5 we examine **completeness** of a measure space: adjoining all subsets of
measure-zero sets so that negligible modifications never accidentally take us outside the
\(\sigma\)-algebra (Proposition 5.14).

** Probability motivation

Every major probability space in modern probability theory is built by Theorem 5.10:
- The uniform distribution on \([0,1]\) (Lebesgue measure, Example 5.11);
- Product measures for independent random variables (Chapter 9);
- Infinite coin-tossing and general stochastic processes \((\Omega = \mathbb R^{\mathbb N})\) via cylinder
  algebras (Chapter 10);
- Probability laws on \(\mathbb R\) associated with arbitrary cumulative distribution functions
  (Lebesgue–Stieltjes measures, Chapter 12).

Even more importantly, Example 5.4 ("the finitely additive uniform lottery on \(\mathbb N\)") shows what
goes wrong when a naive prescription is only *finitely* additive on an algebra: if it fails the
continuity test \(A_n \downarrow \emptyset \implies \mu_0(A_n) \to 0\) (Proposition 5.5), no countably
additive probability measure can extend it!

* Algebras and premeasures

** Intuition

Before we have a \(\sigma\)-algebra, we start with a simpler family of sets closed under *finite* Boolean
operations (complements, finite unions, finite intersections, set differences). Such a family is called
an **algebra**. On an algebra \(\mathcal A\), we want to prescribe a size function \(\mu_0\) that has a
chance of extending to a genuine measure on \(\sigma(\mathcal A)\).

If a measure \(\mu\) on \(\sigma(\mathcal A)\) extends \(\mu_0\), then whenever pairwise disjoint sets
\(A_1, A_2, \dots \in \mathcal A\) happen to have their countable union
\(\bigcup_{n=1}^\infty A_n\) *already inside \(\mathcal A\)*, \(\mu_0\) must obey countable additivity on
that union! A function \(\mu_0\) on \(\mathcal A\) with this property is called a **premeasure**.

** Definitions and examples

#+begin_quote
*Definition 5.1 (Algebra of sets).* An *algebra* of subsets of \(\Omega\) is a collection
\(\mathcal A \subseteq 2^\Omega\) that:
1. contains \(\Omega\) (\(\Omega \in \mathcal A\)),
2. is closed under complements (\(A \in \mathcal A \implies A^c \in \mathcal A\)), and
3. is closed under finite unions (\(A, B \in \mathcal A \implies A \cup B \in \mathcal A\)).
#+end_quote

By De Morgan's laws, an algebra is automatically closed under finite intersections
(\(A \cap B = (A^c \cup B^c)^c \in \mathcal A\)) and set differences
(\(B \setminus A = B \cap A^c \in \mathcal A\)), and contains \(\emptyset = \Omega^c\). In particular,
**every algebra is a \(\pi\)-system**. Unlike a \(\sigma\)-algebra, an algebra need *not* contain
countable unions of its members.

*Concrete example (The finite/cofinite algebra on \(\mathbb N\)).* A subset \(A \subseteq \mathbb N\) is
called *cofinite* if its complement \(\mathbb N \setminus A\) is finite. Let
\[
\mathcal A := \{A \subseteq \mathbb N : A \text{ is finite or } A^c \text{ is finite}\}.
\]
- \(\mathbb N \in \mathcal A\) (since \(\mathbb N^c = \emptyset\) is finite), and \(\mathcal A\) is
  manifestly closed under complements.
- If \(A, B \in \mathcal A\): if both are finite, \(A \cup B\) is finite; if at least one is cofinite,
  then \((A \cup B)^c = A^c \cap B^c\) is a subset of a finite set, hence finite, so \(A \cup B\) is
  cofinite. Thus \(\mathcal A\) is an algebra!
- \(\mathcal A\) is *not* a \(\sigma\)-algebra: each singleton \(\{2k\} \in \mathcal A\) is finite, but
  their countable union \(E = \{2, 4, 6, \dots\}\) (the even integers) is neither finite nor cofinite, so
  \(E \notin \mathcal A\).
- Nevertheless, what is the generated \(\sigma\)-algebra \(\sigma(\mathcal A)\)? Every subset
  \(S \subseteq \mathbb N\) is a countable union of singletons \(S = \bigcup_{n \in S} \{n\}\), so
  \(\sigma(\mathcal A) = 2^{\mathbb N}\)!

#+begin_quote
*Definition 5.2 (Premeasure).* A *premeasure* on an algebra \(\mathcal A\) is a function
\(\mu_0 : \mathcal A \to [0, \infty]\) such that:
1. \(\mu_0(\emptyset) = 0\), and
2. *(Countable additivity within \(\mathcal A\))* Whenever \(A_1, A_2, \dots \in \mathcal A\) are pairwise
   disjoint and their union **also belongs to \(\mathcal A\)** (\(\bigcup_{n=1}^\infty A_n \in \mathcal A\)),
   \[
   \mu_0\Big(\bigcup_{n=1}^\infty A_n\Big) = \sum_{n=1}^\infty \mu_0(A_n).
   \]
#+end_quote

Notice why the condition "\(\bigcup_{n=1}^\infty A_n \in \mathcal A\)" is essential: \(\mu_0\) is only
defined on \(\mathcal A\), so if the union falls outside \(\mathcal A\), the left-hand side
\(\mu_0(\bigcup_{n=1}^\infty A_n)\) is not even defined yet!

*Immediate consequences of Definition 5.2:*
- *Finite additivity:* If \(A_1, \dots, A_m \in \mathcal A\) are pairwise disjoint, set
  \(A_{m+1} = A_{m+2} = \cdots = \emptyset\). Since \(\mathcal A\) is an algebra,
  \(\bigcup_{k=1}^m A_k \in \mathcal A\), and \(\mu_0(\emptyset) = 0\) gives
  \(\mu_0(\bigcup_{k=1}^m A_k) = \sum_{k=1}^m \mu_0(A_k)\).
- *Monotonicity:* If \(A, B \in \mathcal A\) with \(A \subseteq B\), then \(B \setminus A \in \mathcal A\)
  and \(B = A \sqcup (B \setminus A)\), so
  \(\mu_0(B) = \mu_0(A) + \mu_0(B \setminus A) \ge \mu_0(A)\).

#+begin_quote
*Example 5.3 (Assigning masses to a finite partition).* A *finite partition* of \(\Omega\) is a finite
family of nonempty, pairwise disjoint sets \(B_1, \dots, B_m\) whose union is \(\Omega\); its members
are called *blocks*. Let \(\mathcal A\) consist of all unions of these blocks:
\[
\mathcal A = \Big\{\bigcup_{j \in J} B_j : J \subseteq \{1, \dots, m\}\Big\}
\]
(where \(J = \emptyset\) gives the empty set \(\emptyset\)). Since the blocks are nonempty and disjoint,
every set in \(\mathcal A\) corresponds to a unique index set \(J \subseteq \{1, \dots, m\}\), so
\(\mathcal A\) has \(2^m\) sets.

Assign each block \(B_j\) a mass \(p_j \ge 0\) (with \(\sum_{j=1}^m p_j = 1\) for a probability model),
and prescribe
\[
\mu_0\Big(\bigcup_{j \in J} B_j\Big) := \sum_{j \in J} p_j \qquad (J \subseteq \{1, \dots, m\}).
\]
Disjoint unions of sets in \(\mathcal A\) correspond to disjoint index sets \(J\), so their masses add.
Because \(\mathcal A\) is finite, in any sequence of pairwise disjoint sets in \(\mathcal A\) at most
\(m\) sets can be nonempty; hence countable additivity holds automatically, and \(\mathcal A\) is already
a \(\sigma\)-algebra! What remains unspecified is how mass might be distributed *inside* an individual
block \(B_j\) if \(B_j\) contains more than one point.
#+end_quote

#+begin_quote
*Example 5.4 (A finitely additive "uniform lottery" on \(\mathbb N\) that is NOT a premeasure).* On the
finite/cofinite algebra \(\mathcal A\) of \(\mathbb N\), define \(\mu_0 : \mathcal A \to \{0, 1\}\) by
\[
\mu_0(A) := \begin{cases} 0, & \text{if } A \text{ is finite},\\ 1, & \text{if } A \text{ is cofinite}.\end{cases}
\]
- *\(\mu_0\) is finitely additive:* Let \(A_1, \dots, A_m \in \mathcal A\) be pairwise disjoint.
  - If all \(A_k\) are finite, their union is finite, and \(0 = 0 + \cdots + 0\).
  - Could two disjoint sets \(A_j, A_k\) both be cofinite? No! If \(A_j\) is cofinite, then
    \(A_k \subseteq A_j^c\) is contained in a finite set, so \(A_k\) must be finite. Thus at most one
    \(A_j\) is cofinite; the union is then cofinite, and both sides equal \(1\).
- *\(\mu_0\) is NOT a premeasure:* The singletons \(A_n = \{n\} \in \mathcal A\) are pairwise disjoint,
  and their countable union is \(\bigcup_{n=1}^\infty \{n\} = \mathbb N \in \mathcal A\). Yet
  \[
  1 = \mu_0(\mathbb N) \ne \sum_{n=1}^\infty \mu_0(\{n\}) = \sum_{n=1}^\infty 0 = 0!
  \]
Thus **finite additivity on an algebra does not guarantee a measure extension**. Notice how the same
obstruction appears in the decreasing tail sets \(T_n = \{n, n+1, \dots\} \in \mathcal A\): each \(T_n\)
is cofinite with \(\mu_0(T_n) = 1\), and \(T_n \downarrow \emptyset\), yet
\(\mu_0(T_n) = 1 \not\to 0\). Mass "escapes to infinity"!
#+end_quote

** The continuity test for a finite premeasure

Example 5.4 suggests that when \(\mu_0(\Omega) < \infty\), checking countable additivity on
\(\mathcal A\) is equivalent to checking that no mass can escape along a sequence of sets in
\(\mathcal A\) decreasing to \(\emptyset\).

#+begin_quote
*Proposition 5.5 (A continuity test for a finite premeasure).* Suppose that \(\mu_0 : \mathcal A \to [0, \infty)\)
is nonnegative and finitely additive on an algebra \(\mathcal A\), with \(\mu_0(\emptyset) = 0\) and
\(\mu_0(\Omega) < \infty\). Then \(\mu_0\) is a premeasure if and only if
\[
\mu_0(A_n) \longrightarrow 0 \quad \text{whenever } A_n \in \mathcal A \text{ and } A_n \downarrow \emptyset.
\]
#+end_quote

*Proof.*
- *(\(\implies\)) Suppose \(\mu_0\) is a premeasure and \(A_n \in \mathcal A\) with \(A_n \downarrow \emptyset\):*
  Write \(A_1\) as the telescoping disjoint union of differences \(A_k \setminus A_{k+1} \in \mathcal A\):
  \[
  A_1 = \bigsqcup_{k=1}^\infty (A_k \setminus A_{k+1}) \qquad \text{and more generally} \qquad
  A_n = \bigsqcup_{k=n}^\infty (A_k \setminus A_{k+1}).
  \]
  Since \(A_1 \in \mathcal A\) and \(\mu_0\) is a premeasure, the series
  \(\sum_{k=1}^\infty \mu_0(A_k \setminus A_{k+1}) = \mu_0(A_1) \le \mu_0(\Omega) < \infty\) converges!
  Because \(A_n \in \mathcal A\) is the union from index \(k = n\) onwards, \(\mu_0(A_n)\) is the tail
  \(\sum_{k=n}^\infty \mu_0(A_k \setminus A_{k+1})\) of a convergent series, which tends to \(0\) as
  \(n \to \infty\).
- *(\(\Longleftarrow\)) Suppose \(\mu_0(B_n) \to 0\) whenever \(B_n \in \mathcal A\) decreases to \(\emptyset\):*
  Let \(A_1, A_2, \dots \in \mathcal A\) be pairwise disjoint sets whose union
  \(A := \bigcup_{j=1}^\infty A_j\) belongs to \(\mathcal A\). For each \(n \ge 1\), consider the
  remainder after removing the first \(n\) pieces:
  \[
  B_n := A \setminus \bigcup_{j=1}^n A_j.
  \]
  Since \(\mathcal A\) is an algebra and \(A, A_1, \dots, A_n \in \mathcal A\), we have
  \(B_n \in \mathcal A\). Moreover, \(B_1 \supseteq B_2 \supseteq \cdots\) and
  \(\bigcap_{n=1}^\infty B_n = A \setminus \bigcup_{j=1}^\infty A_j = \emptyset\), so
  \(B_n \downarrow \emptyset\). By finite additivity of \(\mu_0\) on \(\mathcal A\),
  \[
  \mu_0(A) = \sum_{j=1}^n \mu_0(A_j) + \mu_0(B_n).
  \]
  Letting \(n \to \infty\) and using \(\mu_0(B_n) \to 0\) gives
  \(\mu_0(A) = \sum_{j=1}^\infty \mu_0(A_j)\). Thus \(\mu_0\) is a premeasure. \(\blacksquare\)

We will use Proposition 5.5 in Chapter 10 to prove that cylinder probabilities on infinite product
spaces form a premeasure, and in Chapter 12 to prove that interval lengths \(\mu_0((a,b]) = F(b) - F(a)\)
form a premeasure.

* Outer measure from coverings

** Intuition

Given a premeasure \(\mu_0\) on an algebra \(\mathcal A\), how do we assign a size to an *arbitrary*
subset \(E \subseteq \Omega\) that is not in \(\mathcal A\)? We approximate \(E\) from the outside! We
cover \(E\) by a countable sequence of elementary sets \(A_1, A_2, \dots \in \mathcal A\) (so
\(E \subseteq \bigcup_{n=1}^\infty A_n\)), add up their prescribed sizes \(\sum_{n=1}^\infty \mu_0(A_n)\),
and take the **infimum** over all possible countable covers of \(E\).

** Definitions and the covering lemma

#+begin_quote
*Definition 5.6 (Outer measure).* An *outer measure* on \(\Omega\) is a function
\(\mu^* : 2^\Omega \to [0, \infty]\) defined on **all** subsets of \(\Omega\) such that:
1. \(\mu^*(\emptyset) = 0\),
2. *(Monotonicity)* \(\mu^*(E) \le \mu^*(F)\) whenever \(E \subseteq F \subseteq \Omega\), and
3. *(Countable subadditivity)* For any sequence of subsets \(E_1, E_2, \dots \subseteq \Omega\),
   \[
   \mu^*\Big(\bigcup_{n=1}^\infty E_n\Big) \le \sum_{n=1}^\infty \mu^*(E_n).
   \]
#+end_quote

*(Warning: An outer measure is NOT required to be additive, even for two disjoint sets \(E_1, E_2\)!)*

Given a premeasure \(\mu_0\) on an algebra \(\mathcal A\), define \(\mu^* : 2^\Omega \to [0, \infty]\) by
\begin{equation}
\mu^*(E) := \inf\left\{\sum_{n=1}^\infty \mu_0(A_n) : A_n \in \mathcal A,\ E \subseteq \bigcup_{n=1}^\infty A_n\right\}. \tag{5.1}
\end{equation}
- *Why does at least one cover always exist?* Because \(\Omega \in \mathcal A\), so
  \(A_1 = \Omega, A_2 = \emptyset, \dots\) covers every \(E \subseteq \Omega\).
- *Are finite covers allowed?* Yes: any finite cover \(A_1, \dots, A_m\) can be padded with
  \(A_{m+1} = A_{m+2} = \cdots = \emptyset\), which adds \(\mu_0(\emptyset) = 0\) to the sum.

#+begin_quote
*Lemma 5.7.* The function \(\mu^*\) defined in (5.1) is an outer measure on \(\Omega\), and it agrees with
\(\mu_0\) on \(\mathcal A\) (that is, \(\mu^*(A) = \mu_0(A)\) for every \(A \in \mathcal A\)).
#+end_quote

*Proof.*

*Step 1. Check the three outer-measure properties (Definition 5.6).*
- *Vanishing at \(\emptyset\):* Covering \(\emptyset\) with \(A_n = \emptyset\) for all \(n\) gives
  \(0 \le \mu^*(\emptyset) \le \sum_{n=1}^\infty \mu_0(\emptyset) = 0\).
- *Monotonicity:* If \(E \subseteq F\), then every cover \(F \subseteq \bigcup_{n=1}^\infty A_n\) is
  automatically a cover of \(E\) as well. Thus the set of cover sums for \(E\) contains the set of cover
  sums for \(F\), so taking infima reverses the inclusion: \(\mu^*(E) \le \mu^*(F)\).
- *Countable subadditivity (the \(\varepsilon/2^j\) trick):* Let \(E_1, E_2, \dots \subseteq \Omega\). We
  want to prove \(\mu^*(\bigcup_{j=1}^\infty E_j) \le \sum_{j=1}^\infty \mu^*(E_j)\).
  If \(\sum_{j=1}^\infty \mu^*(E_j) = \infty\), the right-hand side is \(\infty\) and the inequality holds
  trivially. So assume \(\sum_{j=1}^\infty \mu^*(E_j) < \infty\); in particular, \(\mu^*(E_j) < \infty\)
  for every \(j\).
  Fix any \(\varepsilon > 0\). For each \(j \ge 1\), by the definition of \(\mu^*(E_j)\) as an infimum,
  we can choose a countable cover \(E_j \subseteq \bigcup_{n=1}^\infty A_{j,n}\) with
  \(A_{j,n} \in \mathcal A\) whose total size exceeds \(\mu^*(E_j)\) by at most \(2^{-j}\varepsilon\):
  \[
  \sum_{n=1}^\infty \mu_0(A_{j,n}) \le \mu^*(E_j) + 2^{-j}\varepsilon.
  \]
  Since \(\mathbb N \times \mathbb N\) is countable, the double family \(\{A_{j,n}\}_{j,n \ge 1}\) forms a
  single countable cover of \(\bigcup_{j=1}^\infty E_j\) by sets in \(\mathcal A\)! Therefore,
  \(\mu^*(\bigcup_{j=1}^\infty E_j)\) is at most the sum of \(\mu_0(A_{j,n})\) over all \(j, n \ge 1\):
  \[
  \mu^*\Big(\bigcup_{j=1}^\infty E_j\Big) \le \sum_{j=1}^\infty \sum_{n=1}^\infty \mu_0(A_{j,n})
  \le \sum_{j=1}^\infty \big(\mu^*(E_j) + 2^{-j}\varepsilon\big)
  = \sum_{j=1}^\infty \mu^*(E_j) + \varepsilon.
  \]
  (All terms are in \([0, \infty]\), so summing a double series in any order is valid.) Since
  \(\varepsilon > 0\) was arbitrary, letting \(\varepsilon \downarrow 0\) gives
  \(\mu^*(\bigcup_{j=1}^\infty E_j) \le \sum_{j=1}^\infty \mu^*(E_j)\).

*Step 2. Show that \(\mu^*(A) = \mu_0(A)\) for every \(A \in \mathcal A\).*
- *(\(\le\)) Trivial cover:* If \(A \in \mathcal A\), then \(A_1 = A, A_2 = \emptyset, \dots\) is a valid
  cover of \(A\) by sets in \(\mathcal A\), so \(\mu^*(A) \le \mu_0(A) + 0 + \cdots = \mu_0(A)\).
- *(\(\ge\)) Any cover is at least as big as \(\mu_0(A)\):* Let \(A \subseteq \bigcup_{n=1}^\infty A_n\)
  be *any* cover of \(A\) by sets \(A_n \in \mathcal A\). Disjointify and clip to \(A\) by defining
  \[
  B_n := A \cap \Big(A_n \setminus \bigcup_{j < n} A_j\Big) \qquad (n \ge 1).
  \]
  Since \(\mathcal A\) is an algebra, each \(B_n \in \mathcal A\). Furthermore, the sets \(B_n\) are
  pairwise disjoint and their union is \(\bigsqcup_{n=1}^\infty B_n = A \cap \bigcup_{n=1}^\infty A_n = A \in \mathcal A\)!
  Because \(\mu_0\) is a **premeasure** on \(\mathcal A\) (and \(B_n \subseteq A_n\) implies
  \(\mu_0(B_n) \le \mu_0(A_n)\)), we get
  \[
  \mu_0(A) = \sum_{n=1}^\infty \mu_0(B_n) \le \sum_{n=1}^\infty \mu_0(A_n).
  \]
  Taking the infimum over all covers \(\{A_n\}\) gives \(\mu_0(A) \le \mu^*(A)\). Combining both
  inequalities yields \(\mu^*(A) = \mu_0(A)\). \(\blacksquare\)

*Important observation:* Step 2 is the **exact place** where the premeasure hypothesis (countable
additivity of \(\mu_0\) on \(\mathcal A\)) is used! If you apply formula (5.1) to the failed uniform
lottery of Example 5.4, covering \(\mathbb N\) by the singletons \(\{n\}\) gives
\(\mu^*(\mathbb N) = \sum_{n=1}^\infty \mu_0(\{n\}) = 0 \ne 1 = \mu_0(\mathbb N)\), so \(\mu^*\) fails to
recover \(\mu_0\)!

#+begin_quote
*Example 5.8 (Outer measure sees whole blocks and is NOT additive on all sets).* Let
\(\Omega = \{1, 2, 3, 4\}\) and let \(\mathcal A = \{\emptyset, \{1, 2\}, \{3, 4\}, \Omega\}\) be the
algebra generated by the two blocks \(B_1 = \{1, 2\}\) and \(B_2 = \{3, 4\}\), with premeasure
\[
\mu_0(\emptyset) = 0, \qquad \mu_0(\{1, 2\}) = \frac12, \qquad \mu_0(\{3, 4\}) = \frac12, \qquad \mu_0(\Omega) = 1.
\]
What is the outer measure \(\mu^*(\{1\})\) of the singleton \(\{1\} \notin \mathcal A\)?
Every cover of \(\{1\}\) by sets in \(\mathcal A\) must include either \(\{1, 2\}\) or \(\Omega\), because
those are the only sets in \(\mathcal A\) that contain the point \(1\). The cheapest such cover is
\(A_1 = \{1, 2\}\) itself, which has mass \(\frac12\). The exact same reasoning applies to \(\{2\}\).
Therefore:
\[
\mu^*(\{1\}) = \frac12, \qquad \mu^*(\{2\}) = \frac12, \qquad \text{and} \qquad \mu^*(\{1, 2\}) = \frac12.
\]
In particular, on the disjoint sets \(\{1\}\) and \(\{2\}\),
\[
\mu^*\big(\{1\} \cup \{2\}\big) = \frac12 < \frac12 + \frac12 = \mu^*(\{1\}) + \mu^*(\{2\})!
\]
Outer measure is strictly subadditive here: because \(\mathcal A\) cannot distinguish \(1\) from \(2\),
covering \(\{1\}\) and \(\{2\}\) separately pays for the whole block \(\{1, 2\}\) twice.
#+end_quote

* Which sets can split every test set?

** Intuition

In Example 5.8, why did \(\{1\}\) misbehave while the block \(\{1, 2\}\) behaved nicely? Because when we
use \(E = \{1\}\) to split the test set \(T = \{1, 2\}\) into \(T \cap E = \{1\}\) and
\(T \setminus E = \{2\}\), the outer measures of the two pieces add up to
\(\frac12 + \frac12 = 1 > \frac12 = \mu^*(T)\). Intuitively, \(E = \{1\}\) cuts right through the middle
of an indivisible block of \(\mathcal A\), causing double-counting!

Constantin Carathéodory's brilliant idea (1914) was to define a set \(E \subseteq \Omega\) to be
*measurable* if it never causes double-counting when splitting *any* test set \(T \subseteq \Omega\).

** Carathéodory's criterion and the measurable-set theorem

For *any* outer measure \(\mu^*\), countable ( hence finite) subadditivity applied to the union
\(T = (T \cap E) \cup (T \setminus E)\) always gives one inequality for free:
\[
\mu^*(T) \le \mu^*(T \cap E) + \mu^*(T \setminus E) \quad \text{for all } T, E \subseteq \Omega.
\]
We call a set \(E \subseteq \Omega\) **Carathéodory measurable** if the reverse inequality (\(\ge\))
also holds for every test set \(T \subseteq \Omega\), so that we have exact equality:
\begin{equation}
\mu^*(T) = \mu^*(T \cap E) + \mu^*(T \setminus E) \quad \text{for every } T \subseteq \Omega. \tag{5.2}
\end{equation}
We denote the collection of all Carathéodory-measurable subsets of \(\Omega\) by \(\mathcal M\).

#+ATTR_LATEX: :width 0.75\textwidth :center t
#+ATTR_HTML: :width 600px :align center
[[file:figures/fig05_caratheodory_split.png]]

*Practical tip for checking \(E \in \mathcal M\):* Because \(\mu^*(T) \le \mu^*(T \cap E) + \mu^*(T \setminus E)\)
is automatic from subadditivity, and because \(\mu^*(T) = \infty\) makes
\(\mu^*(T) \ge \mu^*(T \cap E) + \mu^*(T \setminus E)\) hold trivially, to prove \(E \in \mathcal M\) you
only ever need to check:
\[
\mu^*(T) \ge \mu^*(T \cap E) + \mu^*(T \setminus E) \quad \text{for all } T \subseteq \Omega \text{ with } \mu^*(T) < \infty.
\]

#+begin_quote
*Theorem 5.9 (Carathéodory's measurable-set theorem).* For any outer measure \(\mu^*\) on \(\Omega\), the
collection \(\mathcal M\) defined by (5.2) is a \(\sigma\)-algebra. Furthermore, the restriction of
\(\mu^*\) to \(\mathcal M\) is a *complete measure*: it is a measure on \((\Omega, \mathcal M)\), and
every subset \(Z \subseteq N\) of a set \(N \in \mathcal M\) with \(\mu^*(N) = 0\) also belongs to
\(\mathcal M\) and has measure zero.
#+end_quote

*Proof.* Throughout this proof we use **only** the three properties of an outer measure (vanishing at
\(\emptyset\), monotonicity, countable subadditivity) and the splitting identity (5.2).

*Step 1. Closure under finite set operations (\(\mathcal M\) is an algebra).*
- *\(\emptyset, \Omega \in \mathcal M\):* For \(E = \emptyset\),
  \(\mu^*(T \cap \emptyset) + \mu^*(T \setminus \emptyset) = \mu^*(\emptyset) + \mu^*(T) = \mu^*(T)\).
- *Complements:* Replacing \(E\) by \(E^c\) simply swaps \(T \cap E\) and \(T \setminus E = T \cap E^c\)
  on the right-hand side of (5.2). Thus \(E \in \mathcal M \implies E^c \in \mathcal M\).
- *Finite unions:* Let \(E, F \in \mathcal M\) and let \(T \subseteq \Omega\) be any test set.
  1. First split \(T\) using \(E \in \mathcal M\):
     \[
     \mu^*(T) = \mu^*(T \cap E) + \mu^*(T \setminus E).
     \]
  2. Next split the second piece \(T \setminus E\) using \(F \in \mathcal M\):
     \[
     \mu^*(T \setminus E) = \mu^*\big((T \setminus E) \cap F\big) + \mu^*\big((T \setminus E) \setminus F\big).
     \]
  3. Substitute (2) into (1), noting that \((T \setminus E) \setminus F = T \setminus (E \cup F)\):
     \[
     \mu^*(T) = \mu^*(T \cap E) + \mu^*\big((T \setminus E) \cap F\big) + \mu^*\big(T \setminus (E \cup F)\big).
     \]
  4. Observe that the first two sets on the right have union
     \((T \cap E) \cup ((T \setminus E) \cap F) = T \cap (E \cup F)\) (see diagram below!). By
     subadditivity of \(\mu^*\) on those first two terms,
     \[
     \mu^*(T \cap E) + \mu^*\big((T \setminus E) \cap F\big) \ge \mu^*\big(T \cap (E \cup F)\big).
     \]
     Inserting this into (3) gives
     \[
     \mu^*(T) \ge \mu^*\big(T \cap (E \cup F)\big) + \mu^*\big(T \setminus (E \cup F)\big).
     \]
     Since the opposite inequality (\(\le\)) always holds by subadditivity, equality holds and
     \(E \cup F \in \mathcal M\).

#+caption: Figure 5.3: Splitting Test Set $T$ Successively by Measurable Sets $E$ and $F$
#+ATTR_LATEX: :width 0.75\textwidth :center t
#+ATTR_HTML: :width 600px :align center
[[file:figures/fig05_caratheodory_split_ef.png]]

Since \(\mathcal M\) is closed under complements and finite unions, it is an algebra (hence also closed
under finite intersections and set differences).

*Step 2. Closure under countable unions (\(\mathcal M\) is a \(\sigma\)-algebra).*
First let \(E_1, E_2, \dots \in \mathcal M\) be **pairwise disjoint**, and set
\(E := \bigsqcup_{j=1}^\infty E_j\). Fix any test set \(T \subseteq \Omega\).
- By induction on \(n\), splitting \(T\) successively by \(E_1\), then the remainder by \(E_2\), ..., up
  to \(E_n\) gives, for every finite \(n \ge 1\):
  \[
  \mu^*(T) = \sum_{j=1}^n \mu^*(T \cap E_j) + \mu^*\Big(T \setminus \bigcup_{j=1}^n E_j\Big).
  \]
- Now comes a key monotonicity step: since \(\bigcup_{j=1}^n E_j \subseteq E\), taking complements inside
  \(T\) reverses the inclusion:
  \[
  T \setminus E \subseteq T \setminus \bigcup_{j=1}^n E_j, \quad \text{so} \quad
  \mu^*(T \setminus E) \le \mu^*\Big(T \setminus \bigcup_{j=1}^n E_j\Big).
  \]
  Substituting this lower bound into the previous identity gives an inequality where the second term on
  the right no longer depends on \(n\):
  \[
  \mu^*(T) \ge \sum_{j=1}^n \mu^*(T \cap E_j) + \mu^*(T \setminus E) \quad \text{for every } n \ge 1.
  \]
- Now let \(n \to \infty\) (taking the supremum over \(n\), with no continuity assumption on \(\mu^*\)
  needed!), and then apply countable subadditivity of \(\mu^*\) to
  \(T \cap E = \bigcup_{j=1}^\infty (T \cap E_j)\):
  \begin{equation}
  \mu^*(T) \ge \sum_{j=1}^\infty \mu^*(T \cap E_j) + \mu^*(T \setminus E) \ge \mu^*(T \cap E) + \mu^*(T \setminus E). \tag{$\star$}
  \end{equation}
  Since \(\mu^*(T) \le \mu^*(T \cap E) + \mu^*(T \setminus E)\) always holds, both inequalities in
  \((\star)\) are equalities! In particular, \(E \in \mathcal M\).
- What if \(F_1, F_2, \dots \in \mathcal M\) are *not* disjoint? Disjointify them by setting
  \(E_1 = F_1\) and \(E_j = F_j \setminus \bigcup_{k=1}^{j-1} F_k\) for \(j \ge 2\). By Step 1, each
  \(E_j \in \mathcal M\), they are pairwise disjoint, and \(\bigcup_{j=1}^\infty F_j = \bigsqcup_{j=1}^\infty E_j \in \mathcal M\).
  Thus \(\mathcal M\) is a \(\sigma\)-algebra!

*Step 3. Countable additivity and completeness of \(\mu^*|_{\mathcal M}\).*
- *Countable additivity:* Take \(T = E = \bigsqcup_{j=1}^\infty E_j\) in \((\star)\) for pairwise disjoint
  \(E_j \in \mathcal M\). Since \(E \cap E_j = E_j\) and \(E \setminus E = \emptyset\) (with
  \(\mu^*(\emptyset) = 0\)), \((\star)\) and countable subadditivity give
  \[
  \mu^*(E) \ge \sum_{j=1}^\infty \mu^*(E_j) \ge \mu^*(E),
  \]
  so \(\mu^*(\bigsqcup_{j=1}^\infty E_j) = \sum_{j=1}^\infty \mu^*(E_j)\). Thus \(\mu^*|_{\mathcal M}\)
  is a measure!
- *Completeness:* Suppose \(N \in \mathcal M\) has \(\mu^*(N) = 0\), and let \(Z \subseteq N\) be *any*
  subset. By monotonicity, \(0 \le \mu^*(Z) \le \mu^*(N) = 0\), so \(\mu^*(Z) = 0\). For any test set
  \(T \subseteq \Omega\), monotonicity gives \(\mu^*(T \cap Z) \le \mu^*(Z) = 0\) and
  \(\mu^*(T \setminus Z) \le \mu^*(T)\), so
  \[
  \mu^*(T) \le \mu^*(T \cap Z) + \mu^*(T \setminus Z) = 0 + \mu^*(T \setminus Z) \le \mu^*(T).
  \]
  Thus equality holds, \(Z \in \mathcal M\), and \(\mu^*(Z) = 0\). \(\blacksquare\)

* The extension theorem

** Intuition

Theorem 5.9 works for *any* outer measure \(\mu^*\), but for an arbitrary outer measure \(\mathcal M\)
might be tiny (just \(\{\emptyset, \Omega\}\)). When \(\mu^*\) is built from a premeasure \(\mu_0\) on an
algebra \(\mathcal A\) via covering formula (5.1), we have one last crucial link to check: **every set
\(A \in \mathcal A\) in the original algebra satisfies Carathéodory's splitting test (5.2)**! Once we know
\(\mathcal A \subseteq \mathcal M\), the fact that \(\mathcal M\) is a \(\sigma\)-algebra immediately gives
\(\sigma(\mathcal A) \subseteq \mathcal M\).

** Statement and proof of Carathéodory's extension theorem

#+begin_quote
*Theorem 5.10 (Carathéodory's extension theorem).* Let \(\mu_0\) be a premeasure on an algebra
\(\mathcal A\) of subsets of \(\Omega\).
1. *(Existence)* There exists a measure \(\mu\) on \(\sigma(\mathcal A)\) such that
   \(\mu(A) = \mu_0(A)\) for every \(A \in \mathcal A\).
2. *(Uniqueness)* If there are sets \(E_n \in \mathcal A\) such that \(E_n \uparrow \Omega\) and
   \(\mu_0(E_n) < \infty\) for every \(n\), then this extension \(\mu\) to \(\sigma(\mathcal A)\) is
   unique.
#+end_quote

*Proof.* Let \(\mu^*\) be the outer measure defined by (5.1), and let \(\mathcal M\) be its
\(\sigma\)-algebra of Carathéodory-measurable sets (Theorem 5.9). By Lemma 5.7, \(\mu^*\) agrees with
\(\mu_0\) on \(\mathcal A\).

To prove existence, it suffices to show \(\mathcal A \subseteq \mathcal M\); then
\(\sigma(\mathcal A) \subseteq \mathcal M\) (since \(\mathcal M\) is a \(\sigma\)-algebra), and
restricting the measure \(\mu^*|_{\mathcal M}\) to \(\sigma(\mathcal A)\) gives a measure \(\mu\) on
\(\sigma(\mathcal A)\) with \(\mu(A) = \mu^*(A) = \mu_0(A)\) for all \(A \in \mathcal A\).

Fix \(A \in \mathcal A\) and an arbitrary test set \(T \subseteq \Omega\). Let
\(T \subseteq \bigcup_{n=1}^\infty A_n\) be *any* countable cover of \(T\) by sets \(A_n \in \mathcal A\).
Since \(\mathcal A\) is an algebra, for each \(n\) both \(A_n \cap A \in \mathcal A\) and
\(A_n \setminus A \in \mathcal A\), and finite additivity of \(\mu_0\) on \(\mathcal A\) gives
\[
\mu_0(A_n) = \mu_0(A_n \cap A) + \mu_0(A_n \setminus A).
\]
Furthermore, \(\{A_n \cap A\}_{n \ge 1}\) is an algebra cover of \(T \cap A\), and
\(\{A_n \setminus A\}_{n \ge 1}\) is an algebra cover of \(T \setminus A\)! Summing over \(n \ge 1\) and
using the definition (5.1) of \(\mu^*\) as an infimum over algebra covers gives
\[
\sum_{n=1}^\infty \mu_0(A_n) = \sum_{n=1}^\infty \mu_0(A_n \cap A) + \sum_{n=1}^\infty \mu_0(A_n \setminus A)
\ge \mu^*(T \cap A) + \mu^*(T \setminus A).
\]
Taking the infimum of the left-hand side over all algebra covers \(\{A_n\}\) of \(T\) yields
\[
\mu^*(T) \ge \mu^*(T \cap A) + \mu^*(T \setminus A).
\]
Together with subadditivity, this proves \(A \in \mathcal M\), so \(\mathcal A \subseteq \mathcal M\) and
existence holds!

Finally, under the \(\sigma\)-finiteness assumption \(E_n \in \mathcal A\), \(E_n \uparrow \Omega\),
\(\mu_0(E_n) < \infty\), note that every algebra \(\mathcal A\) is a \(\pi\)-system, so Proposition 4.11
applied with \(\mathcal P = \mathcal A\) immediately guarantees that the extension to
\(\sigma(\mathcal A)\) is unique. \(\blacksquare\)

#+begin_quote
*Example 5.11 (Recognizing the Lebesgue construction).* On \(\Omega = (0, 1]\), let \(\mathcal A\) be the
collection of all finite disjoint unions of half-open intervals \((a, b]\) with \(0 \le a < b \le 1\)
(including the empty union \(\emptyset\)).
- *\(\mathcal A\) is an algebra:* Taking the complement of a finite union of intervals in \((0,1]\), or
  cutting two unions at all of their endpoints, produces another finite disjoint union of half-open
  intervals \((a_j, b_j]\).
- *Premeasure:* Define \(\mu_0\big(\bigsqcup_{j=1}^m (a_j, b_j]\big) := \sum_{j=1}^m (b_j - a_j)\). From
  real analysis (or Chapter 12 using compactness of closed intervals \([a+\delta, b]\)), \(\mu_0\) is a
  premeasure on \(\mathcal A\).
- *Extension:* Theorem 5.10 extends \(\mu_0\) uniquely to a probability measure \(\lambda\) on
  \(\sigma(\mathcal A) = \mathcal B((0, 1])\), namely Lebesgue measure on \((0, 1]\). Doing the same on
  \(\mathbb R\) with the exhausting intervals \(E_n = (-n, n] \in \mathcal A\) gives Lebesgue measure on
  \((\mathbb R, \mathcal B(\mathbb R))\).

Notice that Theorem 5.10 never used distances or Euclidean geometry — only algebra operations, countable
coverings, and additivity. All the geometry of a specific model lives in verifying that \(\mu_0\) is a
premeasure on \(\mathcal A\)!
#+end_quote

*Scope of uniqueness (a subtle point).* Theorem 5.10 guarantees uniqueness on \(\sigma(\mathcal A)\),
*not* on larger \(\sigma\)-algebras! For instance, in Example 5.8 (\(\Omega = \{1,2,3,4\}\) with blocks
\(\{1,2\}\) and \(\{3,4\}\) of mass \(1/2\)), \(\sigma(\mathcal A) = \mathcal A\) has only \(4\) sets,
and on \(\sigma(\mathcal A)\) the measure is unique. If you enlarge the \(\sigma\)-algebra to
\(2^\Omega\), you could assign mass \(1/4\) to each of \(1, 2, 3, 4\), or assign mass \(1/2\) to \(1\)
and \(3\) and \(0\) to \(2\) and \(4\). Both extend \(\mu_0\) to \(2^\Omega\), and this does not
contradict uniqueness on \(\sigma(\mathcal A)\).

* Completing a measure

** Intuition

In Theorem 5.9, the restriction of \(\mu^*\) to the Carathéodory \(\sigma\)-algebra \(\mathcal M\) was
*complete* (every subset of a null set in \(\mathcal M\) was again in \(\mathcal M\)). However, when we
restrict \(\mu^*\) down to the smaller generated \(\sigma\)-algebra \(\mathcal F = \sigma(\mathcal A)\)
(for instance, restricting from Lebesgue-measurable sets \(\mathcal M\) to Borel sets
\(\mathcal B(\mathbb R) = \sigma(\mathcal A)\)), we can lose completeness!

Why do we care about completeness? Suppose two functions \(f\) and \(g\) agree outside a measurable set
\(N \in \mathcal F\) with \(\mu(N) = 0\) (we say \(f = g\) *almost everywhere*). If \(f\) is measurable,
we want \(g\) to be measurable too! On an *incomplete* measure space, \(f\) and \(g\) can differ on a
non-measurable subset \(Z \subseteq N\), making \(g\) non-measurable even though \(\mu\) considers \(N\)
negligible. **Completing** a measure space fixes this once and for all by adjoining all subsets of
\(\mu\)-null sets.

** Definition and construction of the completion

#+begin_quote
*Definition 5.12 (Complete measure space).* A measure space \((\Omega, \mathcal F, \mu)\) is *complete*
if every subset of every measurable set of measure zero belongs to \(\mathcal F\):
\[
N \in \mathcal F,\ \mu(N) = 0,\ Z \subseteq N \implies Z \in \mathcal F.
\]
(By monotonicity, any such \(Z\) then automatically has \(\mu(Z) = 0\).)
#+end_quote

#+begin_quote
*Example 5.13 (A missing event inside a null set).* Let \(\Omega = \{1, 2, 3\}\), let
\(\mathcal F = \{\emptyset, \{1\}, \{2, 3\}, \Omega\}\), and define a probability measure \(\mathbb P\) on
\(\mathcal F\) by \(\mathbb P(\{1\}) = 1\) and \(\mathbb P(\{2, 3\}) = 0\).
Here \(N = \{2, 3\} \in \mathcal F\) has \(\mathbb P(N) = 0\), but its subset \(Z = \{2\} \subseteq N\)
does *not* belong to \(\mathcal F\)! Thus \((\Omega, \mathcal F, \mathbb P)\) is **not complete**.
Adjoining \(\{2\}\) and \(\{3\}\) (and their unions with \(\{1\}\)) with probability \(0\) and \(1\)
respectively enlarges \(\mathcal F\) to \(2^\Omega\) and makes the space complete without changing any
previously defined probability.
#+end_quote

#+begin_quote
*Proposition 5.14 (Completion of a measure space).* For any measure space \((\Omega, \mathcal F, \mu)\),
let \(\overline{\mathcal F}\) consist of all subsets \(E \subseteq \Omega\) that can be squeezed between
two \(\mathcal F\)-measurable sets whose difference has \(\mu\)-measure zero:
\[
\overline{\mathcal F} := \big\{E \subseteq \Omega : \text{there exist } A, B \in \mathcal F \text{ with } A \subseteq E \subseteq B \text{ and } \mu(B \setminus A) = 0\big\}.
\]
Then:
1. \(\overline{\mathcal F}\) is a \(\sigma\)-algebra containing \(\mathcal F\),
2. the formula \(\overline{\mu}(E) := \mu(A) = \mu(B)\) is well-defined and defines a *complete* measure
   \(\overline{\mu}\) on \(\overline{\mathcal F}\) extending \(\mu\), and
3. \(\overline{\mathcal F}\) is the smallest \(\sigma\)-algebra containing \(\mathcal F\) and all subsets
   of \(\mu\)-null sets in \(\mathcal F\), and \(\overline{\mu}\) is the unique measure on
   \(\overline{\mathcal F}\) extending \(\mu\).
#+end_quote

#+caption: Figure 5.5: Squeezing a Completed-Measurable Set $E \in \overline{\mathcal{F}}$ Between $A, B \in \mathcal{F}$
#+ATTR_LATEX: :width 0.75\textwidth :center t
#+ATTR_HTML: :width 600px :align center
[[file:figures/fig05_completion_squeeze.png]]

*Proof.*

*Step 1. \(\overline{\mu}(E)\) is well-defined (independent of the enclosing pair \(A, B\)).*
First, for any pair \(A, B \in \mathcal F\) with \(A \subseteq E \subseteq B\) and \(\mu(B \setminus A) = 0\),
finite additivity gives \(\mu(B) = \mu(A) + \mu(B \setminus A) = \mu(A) + 0 = \mu(A)\) (even if
\(\mu(A) = \infty\)).
Second, suppose \(A', B' \in \mathcal F\) is another pair with \(A' \subseteq E \subseteq B'\) and
\(\mu(B' \setminus A') = 0\). Since \(A \subseteq E \subseteq B'\) and \(A' \subseteq E \subseteq B\),
monotonicity of \(\mu\) on \(\mathcal F\) gives
\[
\mu(A) \le \mu(B') = \mu(A') \qquad \text{and} \qquad \mu(A') \le \mu(B) = \mu(A),
\]
so \(\mu(A) = \mu(A')\). Thus \(\overline{\mu}(E)\) does not depend on the choice of \(A, B\). Moreover,
by monotonicity, *any* measure on \(\overline{\mathcal F}\) extending \(\mu\) must satisfy
\(\mu(A) \le \tilde\mu(E) \le \mu(B) = \mu(A)\), proving uniqueness of \(\overline{\mu}\).

*Step 2. \(\overline{\mathcal F}\) is a \(\sigma\)-algebra and \(\overline{\mu}\) is a measure.*
- *\(\mathcal F \subseteq \overline{\mathcal F}\):* For \(E \in \mathcal F\), take \(A = B = E\); then
  \(B \setminus A = \emptyset\) has measure \(0\) and \(\overline{\mu}(E) = \mu(E)\).
- *Complements:* If \(A \subseteq E \subseteq B\) with \(A, B \in \mathcal F\) and \(\mu(B \setminus A) = 0\),
  then taking complements reverses inclusions:
  \[
  B^c \subseteq E^c \subseteq A^c, \qquad \text{with } A^c \setminus B^c = B \setminus A,
  \]
  so \(\mu(A^c \setminus B^c) = \mu(B \setminus A) = 0\) and \(E^c \in \overline{\mathcal F}\).
- *Countable unions:* Let \(E_n \in \overline{\mathcal F}\) for \(n \ge 1\), and choose enclosing pairs
  \(A_n, B_n \in \mathcal F\) with \(A_n \subseteq E_n \subseteq B_n\) and \(\mu(B_n \setminus A_n) = 0\).
  Set \(A := \bigcup_{n=1}^\infty A_n \in \mathcal F\) and \(B := \bigcup_{n=1}^\infty B_n \in \mathcal F\).
  Then \(A \subseteq \bigcup_{n=1}^\infty E_n \subseteq B\), and any point in \(B \setminus A\) belongs
  to \(B_n \setminus A_n\) for some \(n\):
  \[
  B \setminus A = \Big(\bigcup_{n=1}^\infty B_n\Big) \setminus \Big(\bigcup_{k=1}^\infty A_k\Big)
  \subseteq \bigcup_{n=1}^\infty (B_n \setminus A_n).
  \]
  By monotonicity and countable subadditivity of \(\mu\) on \(\mathcal F\),
  \[
  0 \le \mu(B \setminus A) \le \sum_{n=1}^\infty \mu(B_n \setminus A_n) = \sum_{n=1}^\infty 0 = 0.
  \]
  Thus \(\bigcup_{n=1}^\infty E_n \in \overline{\mathcal F}\), and \(\overline{\mathcal F}\) is a
  \(\sigma\)-algebra.
- *Countable additivity:* If the sets \(E_n \in \overline{\mathcal F}\) above are pairwise disjoint, then
  their inner sets \(A_n \subseteq E_n\) are also pairwise disjoint! Using the enclosing pair
  \(A = \bigsqcup_{n=1}^\infty A_n\) and \(B = \bigcup_{n=1}^\infty B_n\) for \(\bigsqcup_{n=1}^\infty E_n\),
  we get
  \[
  \overline{\mu}\Big(\bigsqcup_{n=1}^\infty E_n\Big) = \mu\Big(\bigsqcup_{n=1}^\infty A_n\Big)
  = \sum_{n=1}^\infty \mu(A_n) = \sum_{n=1}^\infty \overline{\mu}(E_n).
  \]
  Since \(\overline{\mu}(\emptyset) = \mu(\emptyset) = 0\), \(\overline{\mu}\) is a measure on
  \(\overline{\mathcal F}\).

*Step 3. Completeness and minimality of \(\overline{\mathcal F}\).*
- *Completeness:* Suppose \(E \in \overline{\mathcal F}\) has \(\overline{\mu}(E) = 0\), with enclosing
  sets \(A \subseteq E \subseteq B\) in \(\mathcal F\), so \(\mu(B) = \overline{\mu}(E) = 0\). For *any*
  subset \(Z \subseteq E\), we have
  \[
  \emptyset \subseteq Z \subseteq B \qquad \text{with } \mu(B \setminus \emptyset) = \mu(B) = 0,
  \]
  so \(Z \in \overline{\mathcal F}\) and \(\overline{\mu}(Z) = 0\).
- *Minimality:* Every \(E \in \overline{\mathcal F}\) with enclosing pair \(A \subseteq E \subseteq B\)
  can be written as \(E = A \cup (E \setminus A)\), where \(A \in \mathcal F\) and
  \(E \setminus A \subseteq B \setminus A\) is a subset of the \(\mu\)-null set \(B \setminus A \in \mathcal F\).
  Therefore, any \(\sigma\)-algebra containing \(\mathcal F\) and all subsets of \(\mu\)-null sets in
  \(\mathcal F\) must contain both \(A\) and \(E \setminus A\), hence their union \(E\). \(\blacksquare\)

** Examples of completion

#+begin_quote
*Example 5.15 (Completion need not reveal every point).* Partition \(\Omega = \{1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6\}\) into
three blocks:
\[
B_1 = \{1, 2\} \text{ (mass } 1/3\text{)}, \qquad
B_2 = \{3, 4\} \text{ (mass } 2/3\text{)}, \qquad
B_3 = \{5, 6\} \text{ (mass } 0\text{)}.
\]
Let \(\mathcal F\) be the \(\sigma\)-algebra of all unions of these three blocks (\(2^3 = 8\) sets).
- The only nonempty \(\mu\)-null set in \(\mathcal F\) is \(B_3 = \{5, 6\}\), whose subsets are
  \(\emptyset, \{5\}, \{6\}, \{5, 6\}\) (\(4\) choices).
- The positive-mass blocks \(B_1 = \{1, 2\}\) and \(B_2 = \{3, 4\}\) must still be taken whole or not at
  all (\(2 \times 2 = 4\) choices).
Thus the completion \(\overline{\mathcal F}\) has \(2 \times 2 \times 4 = 16\) sets, *not* all
\(2^6 = 64\) subsets of \(\Omega\)! For example, \(E = \{1, 2, 5\} \in \overline{\mathcal F}\) (enclosed
by \(A = \{1, 2\}\) and \(B = \{1, 2, 5, 6\}\) with \(\mu(B \setminus A) = \mu(\{5, 6\}) = 0\), giving
\(\overline{\mu}(\{1, 2, 5\}) = 1/3\)). By contrast, \(\{1\} \notin \overline{\mathcal F}\): any
\(A \subseteq \{1\} \subseteq B\) with \(A, B \in \mathcal F\) forces \(A = \emptyset\) and
\(B \supseteq \{1, 2\}\), so \(\mu(B \setminus A) \ge 1/3 > 0\).
#+end_quote

#+begin_quote
*Example 5.16 (Completion depends on the measure).* Equip \(\mathbb R\) with the Borel \(\sigma\)-algebra
\(\mathcal B(\mathbb R)\) and the Dirac measure \(\delta_0\) at the origin.
- The Borel set \(N = \mathbb R \setminus \{0\} \in \mathcal B(\mathbb R)\) has \(\delta_0(N) = 0\)!
- Every subset \(Z \subseteq \mathbb R\) with \(0 \notin Z\) is a subset of the null set \(N\), hence
  belongs to the completion \(\overline{\mathcal B(\mathbb R)}^{\delta_0}\) with measure \(0\).
- Every subset \(E \subseteq \mathbb R\) with \(0 \in E\) can be written as
  \(E = \{0\} \cup (E \setminus \{0\})\), where \(\{0\} \in \mathcal B(\mathbb R)\) and
  \(E \setminus \{0\} \subseteq N\), so \(E\) also belongs to the completion (with enclosing sets
  \(A = \{0\}\) and \(B = \mathbb R\), since \(\delta_0(\mathbb R \setminus \{0\}) = 0\))!
Consequently, **the completion of \(\mathcal B(\mathbb R)\) with respect to \(\delta_0\) is the entire
power set \(2^{\mathbb R}\)**. By contrast, the completion of \(\mathcal B(\mathbb R)\) with respect to
Lebesgue measure \(\lambda\) (the *Lebesgue \(\sigma\)-algebra*) is strictly smaller than \(2^{\mathbb R}\)
(as we will prove in Chapter 11 by constructing a Vitali non-measurable set).
#+end_quote

** Common confusions

- *"Every finitely additive set function on an algebra extends to a measure":* False! Example 5.4 (the
  finite/cofinite lottery on \(\mathbb N\)) is finitely additive on \(\mathcal A\) with total mass \(1\),
  yet fails to be a premeasure and has no countably additive extension.
- *"Outer measure \(\mu^{\ast}\) is a measure":* False! \(\mu^*\) is defined on *all* of \(2^\Omega\), and on
  sets outside \(\mathcal M\) it is only subadditive, not additive (Example 5.8). It becomes a measure
  only when restricted to \(\mathcal M\) (or \(\sigma(\mathcal A)\)).
- *"To check Carathéodory measurability of \(E\), it suffices to check \(\mu^{\ast}(\Omega) = \mu^{\ast}(E) + \mu^{\ast}(E^c)\)":*
  False in general (especially when \(\mu^*(\Omega) = \infty\))! Criterion (5.2) requires \(E\) to split
  **every** test set \(T \subseteq \Omega\) additively, which is what allows Step 1 and Step 2 of
  Theorem 5.9 to chain splits together (\(T \to T \setminus E \to (T \setminus E) \setminus F\)).
- *"Completing a measure makes every subset measurable":* False! Completion only adds subsets of
  *null* sets (Examples 5.15 and Chapter 11).

** Historical remark and classroom route

*Historical remark (A measurability test motivated by length).* In 1914, Constantin Carathéodory sought a
linear measure of point sets in Euclidean space that would agree with ordinary arc length on rectifiable
curves. He began by developing an abstract axiomatic theory of outer measures \(\mu^*\) before returning
to geometry, emphasizing that his definition also works when \(\mu^*(\Omega) = \infty\). His key insight
was the splitting identity (5.2): instead of comparing inner and outer measures (which fails when
\(\mu^*(E) = \infty\)), test whether \(E\) divides every test set \(T\) additively.

*Classroom route.* Introduce algebras, premeasures, and the failed uniform lottery (Example 5.4) right
after Chapter 4's uniqueness theorem. Use the 4-point block example (Example 5.8) to motivate both why
covering outer measure \(\mu^*\) is not additive on all subsets and how Carathéodory's splitting test
(5.2) filters out subsets that cut across indivisible blocks. Follow the extension proof (Theorem 5.10),
note the decreasing-set continuity test (Proposition 5.5) for upcoming infinite-product constructions,
and record completion (Proposition 5.14).

* Worked Exercises

** Exercise 1
In Example 5.8 (\(\Omega = \{1, 2, 3, 4\}\), \(\mathcal A = \{\emptyset, \{1, 2\}, \{3, 4\}, \Omega\}\),
with \(\mu_0(\{1, 2\}) = \mu_0(\{3, 4\}) = 1/2\)):
1. Compute \(\mu^*(T)\) for every subset \(T \subseteq \Omega\) (all \(16\) subsets).
2. Using Carathéodory's criterion (5.2) directly, prove that \(\{1, 2\} \in \mathcal M\) and that no
   \(1\)-element or \(3\)-element subset belongs to \(\mathcal M\). What is \(\mathcal M\) here?

** Solution
1. From formula (5.1):
   - \(\mu^*(\emptyset) = 0\).
   - If \(T \ne \emptyset\) is contained in \(\{1, 2\}\) (namely \(T = \{1\}, \{2\}, \{1, 2\}\)), the
     minimal algebra cover is \(\{1, 2\}\), so \(\mu^*(T) = 1/2\).
   - If \(T \ne \emptyset\) is contained in \(\{3, 4\}\) (namely \(T = \{3\}, \{4\}, \{3, 4\}\)), the
     minimal algebra cover is \(\{3, 4\}\), so \(\mu^*(T) = 1/2\).
   - If \(T\) contains at least one point from \(\{1, 2\}\) *and* at least one point from \(\{3, 4\}\)
     (the remaining \(9\) subsets, such as \(\{1, 3\}\), \(\{1, 2, 3\}\), \(\Omega\)), any algebra cover
     must cover both blocks, so \(\mu^*(T) = 1/2 + 1/2 = 1\).
   Notice the neat formula: \(\mu^*(T) = \frac12 \mathbf 1_{\{T \cap \{1,2\} \ne \emptyset\}} + \frac12 \mathbf 1_{\{T \cap \{3,4\} \ne \emptyset\}}\).
2. Let \(E = \{1, 2\}\). Then for any \(T \subseteq \Omega\), \(T \cap E = T \cap \{1, 2\}\) lies in
   \(\{1, 2\}\) and \(T \setminus E = T \cap \{3, 4\}\) lies in \(\{3, 4\}\). Using the formula from part 1,
   \[
   \mu^*(T \cap E) = \frac12 \mathbf 1_{\{T \cap \{1,2\} \ne \emptyset\}}, \qquad
   \mu^*(T \setminus E) = \frac12 \mathbf 1_{\{T \cap \{3,4\} \ne \emptyset\}},
   \]
   whose sum is \(\mu^*(T)\) for *every* \(T \subseteq \Omega\)! Thus \(\{1, 2\} \in \mathcal M\).
   On the other hand, if \(E\) is any subset that splits one of the blocks — say \(1 \in E\) and
   \(2 \notin E\) — test with \(T = \{1, 2\}\): then \(T \cap E = \{1\}\) and \(T \setminus E = \{2\}\), so
   \[
   \mu^*(T \cap E) + \mu^*(T \setminus E) = \mu^*(\{1\}) + \mu^*(\{2\}) = \frac12 + \frac12 = 1 \ne \frac12 = \mu^*(T).
   \]
   Similarly, if \(E\) splits \(\{3, 4\}\), testing with \(T = \{3, 4\}\) fails (5.2). Thus a set
   \(E\) belongs to \(\mathcal M\) if and only if it splits neither \(\{1, 2\}\) nor \(\{3, 4\}\), which
   means \(\mathcal M = \mathcal A = \{\emptyset, \{1, 2\}, \{3, 4\}, \Omega\}\).

** Exercise 2
Let \(\Omega\) be a nonempty set and define \(\mu^* : 2^\Omega \to [0, 1]\) by \(\mu^*(\emptyset) = 0\) and
\(\mu^*(E) = 1\) for every nonempty \(E \subseteq \Omega\).
1. Verify that \(\mu^*\) is an outer measure on \(\Omega\).
2. Determine the \(\sigma\)-algebra \(\mathcal M\) of Carathéodory-measurable sets for \(\mu^*\).

** Solution
1. \(\mu^*(\emptyset) = 0\); if \(E \subseteq F\), then either \(E = \emptyset\) (\(\mu^*(E)=0 \le \mu^*(F)\))
   or \(E \ne \emptyset \implies F \ne \emptyset\) (\(\mu^*(E) = 1 = \mu^*(F)\)); and for any sequence
   \(E_n\), if all \(E_n = \emptyset\) then both sides of countable subadditivity are \(0\), while if at
   least one \(E_n \ne \emptyset\) then \(\mu^*(\bigcup_n E_n) = 1 \le \sum_n \mu^*(E_n)\). Thus
   \(\mu^*\) is an outer measure.
2. Always \(\emptyset, \Omega \in \mathcal M\). Now suppose \(E \subseteq \Omega\) is any proper nonempty
   subset (\(\emptyset \subsetneq E \subsetneq \Omega\)), so both \(E \ne \emptyset\) and
   \(E^c \ne \emptyset\). Take the test set \(T = \Omega\):
   \[
   \mu^*(\Omega \cap E) + \mu^*(\Omega \setminus E) = \mu^*(E) + \mu^*(E^c) = 1 + 1 = 2 \ne 1 = \mu^*(\Omega).
   \]
   Thus *no* proper nonempty subset \(E\) satisfies (5.2), and \(\mathcal M = \{\emptyset, \Omega\}\).

** Exercise 3
Let \(\mu^*\) be any outer measure on \(\Omega\), and suppose \(N \subseteq \Omega\) has
\(\mu^*(N) = 0\) (we call \(N\) a *\(\mu^{\ast}\)-null set*).
1. Prove directly from (5.2) that \(N \in \mathcal M\).
2. Conclude that for *any* outer measure \(\mu^*\), the \(\sigma\)-algebra \(\mathcal M\) contains all
   subsets of \(\Omega\) of outer measure zero, and for any \(A \in \mathcal M\) and \(N \subseteq \Omega\)
   with \(\mu^*(N) = 0\), both \(A \cup N\) and \(A \setminus N\) belong to \(\mathcal M\) with
   \(\mu^*(A \cup N) = \mu^*(A \setminus N) = \mu^*(A)\).

** Solution
1. Let \(T \subseteq \Omega\) be any test set. Since \(T \cap N \subseteq N\) and \(T \setminus N \subseteq T\),
   monotonicity of \(\mu^*\) gives
   \[
   0 \le \mu^*(T \cap N) \le \mu^*(N) = 0 \implies \mu^*(T \cap N) = 0,
   \qquad \text{and} \qquad
   \mu^*(T \setminus N) \le \mu^*(T).
   \]
   Combining this with subadditivity \(\mu^*(T) \le \mu^*(T \cap N) + \mu^*(T \setminus N)\) gives
   \[
   \mu^*(T) \le 0 + \mu^*(T \setminus N) \le \mu^*(T),
   \]
   so \(\mu^*(T) = \mu^*(T \cap N) + \mu^*(T \setminus N)\) for every \(T \subseteq \Omega\). Thus
   \(N \in \mathcal M\).
2. By part 1, every set \(N\) with \(\mu^*(N) = 0\) is in \(\mathcal M\). Since \(\mathcal M\) is a
   \(\sigma\)-algebra (Theorem 5.9), if \(A \in \mathcal M\) and \(\mu^*(N) = 0\), then
   \(A \cup N \in \mathcal M\) and \(A \setminus N \in \mathcal M\), and also \(A \cap N \subseteq N\)
   has \(\mu^*(A \cap N) = 0\). By finite additivity of the measure \(\mu^*|_{\mathcal M}\),
   \[
   \mu^*(A \cup N) = \mu^*(A) + \mu^*(N \setminus A) = \mu^*(A) + 0 = \mu^*(A),
   \]
   and \(\mu^*(A) = \mu^*(A \setminus N) + \mu^*(A \cap N) = \mu^*(A \setminus N) + 0 = \mu^*(A \setminus N)\).

** Exercise 4
Let \((\Omega, \mathcal F, \mu)\) be a measure space and let \((\Omega, \overline{\mathcal F}, \overline{\mu})\)
be its completion (Proposition 5.14). Prove that a subset \(E \subseteq \Omega\) belongs to
\(\overline{\mathcal F}\) if and only if \(E\) can be written as
\[
E = A \cup Z \quad \text{where } A \in \mathcal F \text{ and } Z \subseteq N \text{ for some } N \in \mathcal F \text{ with } \mu(N) = 0.
\]

** Solution
- *(\(\implies\))* Suppose \(E \in \overline{\mathcal F}\). By definition of \(\overline{\mathcal F}\),
  there exist \(A, B \in \mathcal F\) such that \(A \subseteq E \subseteq B\) and
  \(\mu(B \setminus A) = 0\). Set \(Z := E \setminus A\) and \(N := B \setminus A \in \mathcal F\). Then
  \(E = A \cup Z\), \(A \in \mathcal F\), \(Z \subseteq N\), and \(\mu(N) = 0\).
- *(\(\Longleftarrow\))* Suppose \(E = A \cup Z\) where \(A \in \mathcal F\) and \(Z \subseteq N\) with
  \(N \in \mathcal F\) and \(\mu(N) = 0\). Set \(B := A \cup N \in \mathcal F\). Then
  \(A \subseteq E \subseteq B\), and \(B \setminus A = N \setminus A \subseteq N\). Since
  \(B \setminus A \in \mathcal F\) and \(\mu\) is monotone, \(0 \le \mu(B \setminus A) \le \mu(N) = 0\),
  so \(\mu(B \setminus A) = 0\) and \(E \in \overline{\mathcal F}\).

** Exercise 5
Suppose \(\mu_0\) is a premeasure on an algebra \(\mathcal A \subseteq 2^\Omega\) with
\(\mu_0(\Omega) < \infty\), and let \(\mu^*\) be the outer measure defined by (5.1). Prove that a subset
\(E \subseteq \Omega\) is Carathéodory measurable (\(E \in \mathcal M\)) if and only if for every
\(\varepsilon > 0\) there exists a set \(B \in \sigma(\mathcal A)\) such that \(E \subseteq B\) and
\(\mu^*(B \setminus E) < \varepsilon\).
*(Hint: First show that for any \(E \subseteq \Omega\) there is always a "measurable hull"
\(B \in \sigma(\mathcal A)\) with \(E \subseteq B\) and \(\mu^*(B) = \mu^*(E)\)!)*

** Solution
1. *Existence of a measurable hull \(B \in \sigma(\mathcal A)\) for any \(E \subseteq \Omega\):*
   For each integer \(k \ge 1\), by definition (5.1) of \(\mu^*(E)\) there exists a countable algebra cover
   \(E \subseteq \bigcup_{n=1}^\infty A_{k,n}\) with \(A_{k,n} \in \mathcal A\) such that
   \(\sum_{n=1}^\infty \mu_0(A_{k,n}) \le \mu^*(E) + 1/k\).
   Let \(B_k := \bigcup_{n=1}^\infty A_{k,n} \in \sigma(\mathcal A)\) and set
   \(B := \bigcap_{k=1}^\infty B_k \in \sigma(\mathcal A)\). Then \(E \subseteq B \subseteq B_k\) for all
   \(k\), so by monotonicity and countable subadditivity of \(\mu^*\),
   \[
   \mu^*(E) \le \mu^*(B) \le \mu^*(B_k) \le \sum_{n=1}^\infty \mu^*(A_{k,n}) = \sum_{n=1}^\infty \mu_0(A_{k,n}) \le \mu^*(E) + \frac1k.
   \]
   Letting \(k \to \infty\) gives \(\mu^*(B) = \mu^*(E)\).
2. *(\(\implies\)) If \(E \in \mathcal M\):* Take the hull \(B \in \sigma(\mathcal A) \subseteq \mathcal M\)
   from Step 1 with \(E \subseteq B\) and \(\mu^*(B) = \mu^*(E) \le \mu_0(\Omega) < \infty\). Since
   \(E \in \mathcal M\), we can split the test set \(T = B\) along \(E\):
   \[
   \mu^*(B) = \mu^*(B \cap E) + \mu^*(B \setminus E) = \mu^*(E) + \mu^*(B \setminus E).
   \]
   Subtracting the finite number \(\mu^*(B) = \mu^*(E)\) gives \(\mu^*(B \setminus E) = 0 < \varepsilon\)!
3. *(\(\Longleftarrow\)) If for every \(\varepsilon > 0\) there is \(B \in \sigma(\mathcal A)\) with
   \(E \subseteq B\) and \(\mu^*(B \setminus E) < \varepsilon\):* Choose \(B_k \in \sigma(\mathcal A) \subseteq \mathcal M\)
   with \(E \subseteq B_k\) and \(\mu^*(B_k \setminus E) < 1/k\), and set
   \(B := \bigcap_{k=1}^\infty B_k \in \sigma(\mathcal A) \subseteq \mathcal M\). Then \(E \subseteq B\)
   and \(\mu^*(B \setminus E) \le \mu^*(B_k \setminus E) < 1/k\) for all \(k\), so
   \(\mu^*(B \setminus E) = 0\). By Exercise 3, \(B \setminus E \in \mathcal M\), so
   \(E = B \setminus (B \setminus E) \in \mathcal M\)!

** Exercise 6
Let \(\Omega = \mathbb N\) and let \(\mathcal A\) be the finite/cofinite algebra of \(\mathbb N\). Let
\(p_1, p_2, \dots \ge 0\) be nonnegative weights with \(\sum_{n=1}^\infty p_n = 1\). For every
\(A \in \mathcal A\) (whether finite or cofinite!), define \(\mu_0(A) := \sum_{n \in A} p_n\).
1. Use Proposition 5.5 to prove that \(\mu_0\) is a premeasure on \(\mathcal A\).
2. What is the Carathéodory extension of \(\mu_0\) to \(\sigma(\mathcal A) = 2^{\mathbb N}\)? Compare
   this with Example 5.4.

** Solution
1. Clearly \(\mu_0(\emptyset) = 0\), \(\mu_0(\mathbb N) = \sum_{n=1}^\infty p_n = 1 < \infty\), and for
   disjoint \(A, B \in \mathcal A\) we have \(\mu_0(A \cup B) = \sum_{n \in A \cup B} p_n = \mu_0(A) + \mu_0(B)\),
   so \(\mu_0\) is finitely additive.
   Now suppose \(A_k \in \mathcal A\) decrease to \(\emptyset\) (\(A_1 \supseteq A_2 \supseteq \cdots\) and
   \(\bigcap_{k=1}^\infty A_k = \emptyset\)). Given \(\varepsilon > 0\), since \(\sum_{n=1}^\infty p_n = 1 < \infty\),
   choose \(N \in \mathbb N\) large enough that the tail satisfies \(\sum_{n = N+1}^\infty p_n < \varepsilon\).
   For each \(m \in \{1, \dots, N\}\), since \(\bigcap_{k=1}^\infty A_k = \emptyset\), there is some index
   \(k_m\) such that \(m \notin A_{k_m}\), hence \(m \notin A_k\) for all \(k \ge k_m\). Set
   \(K := \max(k_1, \dots, k_N)\). Then for all \(k \ge K\), we have
   \(A_k \cap \{1, \dots, N\} = \emptyset\), meaning \(A_k \subseteq \{N+1, N+2, \dots\}\). Therefore,
   \[
   \mu_0(A_k) = \sum_{n \in A_k} p_n \le \sum_{n = N+1}^\infty p_n < \varepsilon \quad \text{for all } k \ge K.
   \]
   Thus \(\mu_0(A_k) \to 0\), and Proposition 5.5 proves that \(\mu_0\) is a premeasure!
2. On \(\sigma(\mathcal A) = 2^{\mathbb N}\), the formula \(\mu(E) = \sum_{n \in E} p_n\) (for *all*
   \(E \subseteq \mathbb N\)) is a probability measure extending \(\mu_0\), and by Theorem 5.10 it is the
   unique extension. Unlike Example 5.4 (where all singletons had mass \(0\) and cofinite sets had mass
   \(1\), putting the mass "at infinity"), here the mass is carried by the points \(n \in \mathbb N\) via
   a convergent series \(\sum_{n=1}^\infty p_n = 1\), so tails \(A_k \downarrow \emptyset\) have
   \(\mu_0(A_k) \to 0\).

*Key takeaways*

- Finite additivity on an algebra \(\mathcal A\) is *not* enough to extend \(\mu_0\) to a measure on
  \(\sigma(\mathcal A)\); \(\mu_0\) must be a **premeasure** (checked via \(A_n \downarrow \emptyset \implies \mu_0(A_n) \to 0\)
  when \(\mu_0(\Omega) < \infty\)).
- Carathéodory's extension works in two steps: first define \(\mu^*(E)\) for *all* subsets
  \(E \subseteq \Omega\) by infimum over countable covers from \(\mathcal A\); second, restrict \(\mu^*\)
  to the \(\sigma\)-algebra \(\mathcal M\) of sets \(E\) that split every test set \(T\) additively.
- Every original algebra set \(A \in \mathcal A\) belongs to \(\mathcal M\), so
  \(\sigma(\mathcal A) \subseteq \mathcal M\).
- Completion \((\Omega, \overline{\mathcal F}, \overline{\mu})\) adds all subsets of \(\mu\)-null sets by
  squeezing \(A \subseteq E \subseteq B\) with \(\mu(B \setminus A) = 0\). It depends on the measure
  \(\mu\) and does not make every subset measurable in general.
