* Overview

** What problem does this chapter solve?

In Chapters 1–3 we defined the core objects of the theory (\(\sigma\)-algebras, measures, measurable
functions), but we have not yet solved the *construction problem*: how do you actually specify a
measure on a huge \(\sigma\)-algebra like \(\mathcal B(\mathbb R)\) or infinite coin-toss space
\(\{0,1\}^{\mathbb N}\)?

On a finite sample space \(\Omega\), we can just list the weights of individual outcomes \(\{\omega\}\).
On an uncountable space, singletons often have measure zero, and we cannot list every measurable set.
Instead, we specify the measure on a small, manageable collection \(\mathcal P\) of "elementary sets" —
for example:
- lengths of intervals \((a,b]\) or half-lines \((-\infty, t]\) on \(\mathbb R\);
- probabilities of finite binary strings (cylinder sets) in \(\{0,1\}^{\mathbb N}\).

Two distinct questions immediately arise:
1. *Uniqueness (this chapter):* If two measures \(\mu\) and \(\nu\) agree on the elementary sets
   \(\mathcal P\), must they agree on the entire generated \(\sigma\)-algebra \(\sigma(\mathcal P)\)?
2. *Existence (Chapter 5):* Does there exist *at least one* measure on \(\sigma(\mathcal P)\) taking
   those prescribed values on \(\mathcal P\)?

Keeping uniqueness and existence separate makes both arguments much easier to follow. In this chapter
we solve the *uniqueness* problem using **Dynkin's \(\pi\)-\(\lambda\) lemma** (Theorem 4.6).

Surprise: unlike checking measurability of a function (Lemma 3.2, where *any* generating collection
works), two probability measures can agree on a generating collection \(\mathcal G\) and still
*disagree* on \(\sigma(\mathcal G)\)! (See Example 4.1 below.) The extra condition needed on the
generating collection is simple and natural: it must be closed under finite intersections (a
**\(\pi\)-system**).

** Probability motivation

How do you know that two random variables \(X\) and \(Y\) have the same distribution (law) on
\((\mathbb R, \mathcal B(\mathbb R))\)? By definition, their laws \(X_\#\mathbb P\) and
\(Y_\#\mathbb P\) agree on *every* Borel set \(B \in \mathcal B(\mathbb R)\) — an impossible thing to
check directly. Theorem 4.7 tells us that because the half-lines \((-\infty, t]\) form a \(\pi\)-system
generating \(\mathcal B(\mathbb R)\), it suffices to check
\[
\mathbb P(X \le t) = \mathbb P(Y \le t) \quad \text{for all } t \in \mathbb R
\]
(or even just for \(t \in \mathbb Q\)!). That is why the cumulative distribution function (CDF)
completely determines the probability law of a real random variable. Similarly, for an infinite
sequence of coin tosses, prescribing the probabilities of all finite initial strings uniquely fixes the
probability of *every* tail or infinite-horizon event (such as "infinitely many ones occur",
Example 4.10).

- *4.2 Dynkin's lemma:* If a \(\lambda\)-system contains a \(\pi\)-system \(\mathcal P\), it contains

  by exhausting \(\Omega\) with finite-measure sets from the \(\pi\)-system (Proposition 4.11).

** Historical remark (Probability, measure, and terminology)

In their 1654 correspondence, Blaise Pascal and Pierre de Fermat discussed how to divide the stake in
an interrupted game of chance. Christiaan Huygens's 1657 treatise studied the value of uncertain
winnings: equal chances of receiving \(3\) or \(7\) have a fair value of \(5\). This is an
*expectation*, not a prediction of the amount the player will actually receive on a single play.

Measure theory developed much later through questions about length and integration. Émile Borel (1898)
extended length using countable set operations, and Henri Lebesgue's thesis (1902) used measure to
integrate functions beyond the reach of Riemann integration. These constructions also supplied
probability models: in 1923, Hugo Steinhaus used Lebesgue measure on \([0,1]\) to model infinite
sequences of fair binary trials. Finally, Andrey Kolmogorov's *Grundbegriffe* (1933) presented the
modern axiomatic framework in which probabilities are measures, random variables are measurable
functions, and expectations are integrals.

Even the vocabulary took time to settle. In a 1997 interview, Joseph Doob recalled disagreeing with
William Feller while they were writing their classic textbooks: Doob preferred "chance variable",
Feller "random variable". Wishing to use the same term, they settled it the probabilistic way —
"That is, we tossed for it and he won."

Throughout these notes we use both the measure-theoretic and probabilistic vocabularies. Calling a
measurable function a *random variable* does not change its mathematical definition; it simply tells us
that the underlying measure space is a probability space \((\Omega, \mathcal F, \mathbb P)\).

* Why an arbitrary generating collection is not enough

To test whether a map \(f : \Omega \to S\) is measurable, Lemma 3.2 showed that it is enough to check
\(f^{-1}(G) \in \mathcal F\) for *any* collection \(\mathcal G\) that generates the target
\(\sigma\)-algebra \(\mathcal S = \sigma(\mathcal G)\). Why did that work? Because preimages commute
with *all* set operations (unions, intersections, complements), so the "good sets" always form a
\(\sigma\)-algebra right away.

Measures do *not* behave this way! Knowing \(\mu(A)\) and \(\mu(B)\) does *not* tell you
\(\mu(A \cup B)\) or \(\mu(A \cap B)\), because in the inclusion-exclusion formula
\[
\mu(A \cup B) = \mu(A) + \mu(B) - \mu(A \cap B)
\]
there are two unknowns: \(\mu(A \cup B)\) and \(\mu(A \cap B)\). A simple 4-point example makes this
crystal clear.

#+begin_quote
*Example 4.1 (Agreement on two generating events is not enough).* Let
\(\Omega = \{00, 01, 10, 11\}\), where the two digits record two binary observations, equipped with the
full \(\sigma\)-algebra \(\mathcal F = 2^\Omega\). Consider two probability measures \(\mu\) and \(\nu\)
given by the following outcome weights:

| Outcome \(\omega\) | Probability under \(\mu\) | Probability under \(\nu\) |
|--------------------+---------------------------+---------------------------|
| \(00\)             | \(1/2\)                   | \(0\)                     |
| \(01\)             | \(0\)                     | \(1/2\)                   |
| \(10\)             | \(0\)                     | \(1/2\)                   |
| \(11\)             | \(1/2\)                   | \(0\)                     |

Let \(A = \{10, 11\}\) ("first digit is \(1\)") and \(B = \{01, 11\}\) ("second digit is \(1\)").
1. *\(\mathcal G = \{A, B\}\) generates \(\mathcal F = 2^\Omega\):* Indeed, every singleton can be
   written as an intersection of sets in \(\mathcal G\) or their complements:
   \[
   \{11\} = A \cap B, \quad \{10\} = A \cap B^c, \quad \{01\} = A^c \cap B, \quad \{00\} = A^c \cap B^c,
   \]
   and any subset of \(\Omega\) is a union of singletons.
2. *\(\mu\) and \(\nu\) agree on \(\mathcal G\):*
   \[
   \mu(A) = \mu(\{10\}) + \mu(\{11\}) = 0 + \tfrac12 = \tfrac12, \qquad
   \nu(A) = \nu(\{10\}) + \nu(\{11\}) = \tfrac12 + 0 = \tfrac12,
   \]
   and similarly \(\mu(B) = \frac12 = \nu(B)\).
3. *Yet \(\mu \ne \nu\) on \(\sigma(\mathcal G)\):* On the intersection \(A \cap B = \{11\}\),
   \[
   \mu(A \cap B) = \frac12 \quad \text{whereas} \quad \nu(A \cap B) = 0.
   \]
   Under \(\mu\), the two digits are always equal (\(00\) or \(11\)); under \(\nu\), they are always
   opposite (\(01\) or \(10\)). Agreement on \(A\) and \(B\) does not imply agreement on \(A \cap B\).
#+end_quote

#+begin_example
   Under mu (digits equal):           Under nu (digits opposite):
          B                                  B
       01   11                            01   11
     +----+----+                        +----+----+
     |  0 | 1/2|  A                     | 1/2|  0 |  A
     +----+----+                        +----+----+
     | 1/2|  0 |  A^c                   |  0 | 1/2|  A^c
     +----+----+                        +----+----+
       00   10                            00   10
   Row & column sums are 1/2 in both tables, but the interiors differ!
#+end_example

*Moral of Example 4.1:* If you know the row sum \(\mu(A)\) and column sum \(\mu(B)\) in a \(2\times 2\)
contingency table, you still have one degree of freedom inside the table. Once you also pin down the
intersection \(\mu(A \cap B)\), all four cells are uniquely determined!

* 4.1 Two closure properties

** Intuition

Why did \(\{A \in \mathcal F : \mu(A) = \nu(A)\}\) fail to be a \(\sigma\)-algebra in Example 4.1?
Because a measure only adds across *disjoint* sets (or subtracts across *nested* sets \(A \subseteq B\)).
So the collection of sets where two finite measures agree is naturally closed under:
- taking the whole space \(\Omega\) (if \(\mu(\Omega) = \nu(\Omega)\));
- taking proper set differences \(B \setminus A\) when \(A \subseteq B\) (since
  \(\mu(B \setminus A) = \mu(B) - \mu(A)\));
- taking increasing countable unions \(A_n \uparrow A\) (since \(\mu(A_n) \uparrow \mu(A)\));
- taking countable *disjoint* unions.

Notice what is missing: **intersections** (and arbitrary unions)! Dynkin's idea is to isolate what the
agreement class has (a *\(\lambda\)-system*) from what we must require of the testing collection (a
*\(\pi\)-system*).

** Definitions and basic properties

#+begin_quote
*Definition 4.2 (\(\pi\)-system).* A nonempty collection \(\mathcal P\) of subsets of \(\Omega\) is a
*\(\pi\)-system* if it is closed under finite intersections:
\[
A, B \in \mathcal P \implies A \cap B \in \mathcal P.
\]
Neither complements nor unions are required to belong to \(\mathcal P\).
#+end_quote

*Quick examples of \(\pi\)-systems:*
- The half-lines \(\{(-\infty, a] : a \in \mathbb R\}\) on \(\mathbb R\), since
  \((-\infty, a] \cap (-\infty, b] = (-\infty, \min(a,b)]\).
- The collection of all intervals \((a,b]\) on \(\mathbb R\) (provided we include \(\emptyset\), since
  two disjoint intervals have intersection \(\emptyset\)).
- On any set \(\Omega\), \(\{\emptyset, \Omega\}\) is a \(\pi\)-system.
- *Non-example:* In Example 4.1, \(\mathcal G = \{A, B\}\) is *not* a \(\pi\)-system because
  \(A \cap B = \{11\} \notin \{A, B\}\). (If we enlarge \(\mathcal G\) to the \(\pi\)-system
  \(\{A, B, A \cap B\}\), then any measure matching on all three must agree on \(2^\Omega\).)

#+begin_quote
*Definition 4.3 (\(\lambda\)-system / Dynkin system).* A collection \(\mathcal D\) of subsets of
\(\Omega\) is a *\(\lambda\)-system* (also called a *Dynkin system*) if:
1. \(\Omega \in \mathcal D\).
2. *(Proper differences)* If \(A, B \in \mathcal D\) and \(A \subseteq B\), then
   \(B \setminus A \in \mathcal D\).
3. *(Increasing limits)* If \(A_n \in \mathcal D\) and \(A_n \uparrow A\) (meaning
   \(A_1 \subseteq A_2 \subseteq \cdots\) and \(A = \bigcup_{n=1}^\infty A_n\)), then
   \(A \in \mathcal D\).
#+end_quote

Let us unpack what a \(\lambda\)-system automatically satisfies:
- *Empty set and complements:* Taking \(B = \Omega\) in (ii) shows that if \(A \in \mathcal D\), then
  \(A^c = \Omega \setminus A \in \mathcal D\). In particular, \(\emptyset = \Omega^c \in \mathcal D\).
- *Finite disjoint unions:* Suppose \(A, B \in \mathcal D\) are disjoint (\(A \cap B = \emptyset\)).
  Then \(B \subseteq A^c\), where \(A^c \in \mathcal D\) by the previous bullet. By (ii),
  \[
  A^c \setminus B \in \mathcal D.
  \]
  Taking the complement of \(A^c \setminus B = A^c \cap B^c = (A \cup B)^c\) gives
  \(A \cup B \in \mathcal D\)! By induction, any finite pairwise disjoint union of sets in
  \(\mathcal D\) belongs to \(\mathcal D\).
- *Countable disjoint unions:* If \(A_1, A_2, \dots \in \mathcal D\) are pairwise disjoint, their
  finite unions \(S_n := \bigcup_{k=1}^n A_k\) belong to \(\mathcal D\) and increase to
  \(S_n \uparrow \bigcup_{k=1}^\infty A_k\), which belongs to \(\mathcal D\) by (iii).

*Equivalent definition of a \(\lambda\)-system (used in many textbooks):* A collection \(\mathcal D\) is
a \(\lambda\)-system if and only if:
- (i') \(\Omega \in \mathcal D\),
- (ii') \(\mathcal D\) is closed under complements (\(A \in \mathcal D \implies A^c \in \mathcal D\)), and
- (iii') \(\mathcal D\) is closed under countable *disjoint* unions.

*Proof of equivalence:* We just showed (i)–(iii) \(\implies\) (i')–(iii'). Conversely, assume (i')–(iii').
- If \(A, B \in \mathcal D\) with \(A \subseteq B\), then \(A\) and \(B^c\) are disjoint sets in
  \(\mathcal D\), so \(A \cup B^c \in \mathcal D\), and taking its complement gives
  \((A \cup B^c)^c = A^c \cap B = B \setminus A \in \mathcal D\), proving (ii).
- If \(A_n \in \mathcal D\) with \(A_n \uparrow A\), rewrite the union as a disjoint union:
  \(A = A_1 \cup \bigcup_{n=1}^\infty (A_{n+1} \setminus A_n)\). Each piece is in \(\mathcal D\) by (ii),
  so \(A \in \mathcal D\) by (iii'). \(\blacksquare\)

** When a \(\pi\)-system and a \(\lambda\)-system meet

#+begin_quote
*Lemma 4.4.* A collection that is both a \(\pi\)-system and a \(\lambda\)-system is a \(\sigma\)-algebra.
#+end_quote

*Proof.* Let \(\mathcal D\) be both a \(\pi\)-system and a \(\lambda\)-system.
1. \(\Omega \in \mathcal D\) and \(\mathcal D\) is closed under complements (from the \(\lambda\)-system
   property).
2. For any \(A, B \in \mathcal D\), we have \(A^c, B^c \in \mathcal D\), hence \(A^c \cap B^c \in \mathcal D\)
   (by the \(\pi\)-system property), hence \(A \cup B = (A^c \cap B^c)^c \in \mathcal D\) (De Morgan's
   law). By induction, \(\mathcal D\) is closed under finite unions.
3. For any sequence \(A_1, A_2, \dots \in \mathcal D\), the finite unions
   \(B_n := A_1 \cup \cdots \cup A_n\) belong to \(\mathcal D\) and satisfy
   \(B_n \uparrow \bigcup_{k=1}^\infty A_k\). By property (iii) of a \(\lambda\)-system,
   \(\bigcup_{k=1}^\infty A_k \in \mathcal D\). Thus \(\mathcal D\) is a \(\sigma\)-algebra. \(\blacksquare\)

#+begin_quote
*Example 4.5 (The sets on which two finite measures agree).* Let \(\mu\) and \(\nu\) be finite measures
on \((\Omega, \mathcal F)\) with equal total mass \(\mu(\Omega) = \nu(\Omega) < \infty\). Define the
*agreement class*
\[
\mathcal D := \{A \in \mathcal F : \mu(A) = \nu(A)\}.
\]
Then \(\mathcal D\) is a \(\lambda\)-system:
- *(i) Total space:* \(\Omega \in \mathcal D\) because \(\mu(\Omega) = \nu(\Omega)\) by assumption.
- *(ii) Proper differences:* If \(A, B \in \mathcal D\) and \(A \subseteq B\), then because \(\mu\) and
  \(\nu\) are finite measures (so we never encounter \(\infty - \infty\)),
  \[
  \mu(B \setminus A) = \mu(B) - \mu(A) = \nu(B) - \nu(A) = \nu(B \setminus A),
  \]
  so \(B \setminus A \in \mathcal D\). (Note: this subtraction step is the *only* place where finiteness
  of the measures is used!)
- *(iii) Increasing unions:* If \(A_n \in \mathcal D\) and \(A_n \uparrow A\), continuity of measures
  from below gives
  \[
  \mu(A) = \lim_{n\to\infty} \mu(A_n) = \lim_{n\to\infty} \nu(A_n) = \nu(A),
  \]
  so \(A \in \mathcal D\).

As Example 4.1 showed, \(\mathcal D\) need *not* be a \(\sigma\)-algebra in general (in Example 4.1,
\(\mathcal D = \{\emptyset, \Omega, A, B, A^c, B^c, (A\cap B)\cup(A^c\cap B^c), (A\cap B^c)\cup(A^c\cap B)\}\),
which has \(8\) sets out of \(16\) and is a \(\lambda\)-system but not closed under intersections).
#+end_quote

* 4.2 Dynkin's lemma

** Intuition and strategy

Suppose a \(\lambda\)-system \(\mathcal L\) contains a \(\pi\)-system \(\mathcal P\). We want to prove
that \(\mathcal L\) contains the entire generated \(\sigma\)-algebra \(\sigma(\mathcal P)\).

How do you bridge the gap between a \(\pi\)-system and a \(\lambda\)-system?
1. First, pass to the *smallest* \(\lambda\)-system containing \(\mathcal P\), call it \(\mathcal D\).
   Since \(\mathcal P \subseteq \mathcal D \subseteq \mathcal L\), if we can show that \(\mathcal D\) is
   a \(\sigma\)-algebra, then \(\sigma(\mathcal P) \subseteq \mathcal D \subseteq \mathcal L\) and we
   are done!
2. By Lemma 4.4, to show the \(\lambda\)-system \(\mathcal D\) is a \(\sigma\)-algebra, we only need to
   show that \(\mathcal D\) is a **\(\pi\)-system** — that is, for every \(A \in \mathcal D\) and
   \(B \in \mathcal D\), we have \(A \cap B \in \mathcal D\).
3. Currently we only know \(A \cap B \in \mathcal P \subseteq \mathcal D\) when *both* \(A \in \mathcal P\)
   and \(B \in \mathcal P\). We upgrade this in two stages using a "test-set" (\(\mathcal D_A\)) trick:
   - *First upgrade:* Fix \(A \in \mathcal P\), let \(B\) vary over \(\mathcal D\).
   - *Second upgrade:* Fix \(B \in \mathcal D\), let \(A\) vary over \(\mathcal D\).

** Statement and proof

#+begin_quote
*Theorem 4.6 (Dynkin's \(\pi\)-\(\lambda\) lemma).* Let \(\mathcal P\) be a \(\pi\)-system on \(\Omega\),
and let \(\mathcal L\) be a \(\lambda\)-system containing \(\mathcal P\). Then
\[
\sigma(\mathcal P) \subseteq \mathcal L.
\]
#+end_quote

*Proof.* Let \(\mathcal D\) be the intersection of all \(\lambda\)-systems on \(\Omega\) that contain
\(\mathcal P\):
\[
\mathcal D := \bigcap \{\mathcal M \subseteq 2^\Omega : \mathcal M \text{ is a }\lambda\text{-system and } \mathcal P \subseteq \mathcal M\}.
\]
At least one such \(\lambda\)-system exists (namely \(2^\Omega\), and also \(\mathcal L\)). Just as for
generated \(\sigma\)-algebras, intersecting any family of \(\lambda\)-systems preserves the three
defining properties (i)–(iii), so \(\mathcal D\) is the *smallest* \(\lambda\)-system containing
\(\mathcal P\), and \(\mathcal P \subseteq \mathcal D \subseteq \mathcal L\).

We will prove that \(\mathcal D\) is closed under intersections (a \(\pi\)-system). Lemma 4.4 will then
imply that \(\mathcal D\) is a \(\sigma\)-algebra containing \(\mathcal P\), whence
\(\sigma(\mathcal P) \subseteq \mathcal D \subseteq \mathcal L\).

*Step 1. Fix one set \(A \in \mathcal D\) and test its intersections.* For any fixed \(A \in \mathcal D\),
define the collection of "good partners for \(A\)" inside \(\mathcal D\):
\[
\mathcal D_A := \{B \in \mathcal D : A \cap B \in \mathcal D\}.
\]
We claim that \(\mathcal D_A\) is itself a \(\lambda\)-system:
- *(i) \(\Omega \in \mathcal D_A\):* Since \(A \in \mathcal D\), we have
  \(A \cap \Omega = A \in \mathcal D\), so \(\Omega \in \mathcal D_A\).
- *(ii) Proper differences:* Let \(B, C \in \mathcal D_A\) with \(B \subseteq C\). First, since
  \(B, C \in \mathcal D\) and \(\mathcal D\) is a \(\lambda\)-system, \(C \setminus B \in \mathcal D\).
  Second, we need \(A \cap (C \setminus B) \in \mathcal D\). Notice the elementary set identity
  \[
  A \cap (C \setminus B) = (A \cap C) \setminus (A \cap B).
  \]
  Why does the right-hand side belong to \(\mathcal D\)? Because \(B, C \in \mathcal D_A\) means both
  \(A \cap C \in \mathcal D\) and \(A \cap B \in \mathcal D\); furthermore, \(B \subseteq C\) implies
  \(A \cap B \subseteq A \cap C\). Since \(\mathcal D\) is closed under proper differences,
  \((A \cap C) \setminus (A \cap B) \in \mathcal D\). Thus \(C \setminus B \in \mathcal D_A\).
- *(iii) Increasing unions:* Let \(B_n \in \mathcal D_A\) with \(B_n \uparrow B\). Since
  \(B_n \in \mathcal D\), we have \(B \in \mathcal D\). Also, \(A \cap B_n \in \mathcal D\) and
  \(A \cap B_n \uparrow A \cap B\). Since \(\mathcal D\) is closed under increasing unions,
  \(A \cap B \in \mathcal D\), so \(B \in \mathcal D_A\).

*Step 2. Start with the fixed set \(A\) in the \(\pi\)-system \(\mathcal P\).* Suppose \(A \in \mathcal P\).
For every \(B \in \mathcal P\), the intersection \(A \cap B\) belongs to \(\mathcal P\) (because
\(\mathcal P\) is a \(\pi\)-system!) and hence to \(\mathcal D\). By definition of \(\mathcal D_A\),
this says
\[
\mathcal P \subseteq \mathcal D_A \quad \text{whenever } A \in \mathcal P.
\]
By Step 1, \(\mathcal D_A\) is a \(\lambda\)-system containing \(\mathcal P\). Since \(\mathcal D\) is
the *smallest* \(\lambda\)-system containing \(\mathcal P\), we must have \(\mathcal D \subseteq \mathcal D_A\).
Unpacking the definition of \(\mathcal D_A\), we have proved:
\[
A \in \mathcal P,\ B \in \mathcal D \implies A \cap B \in \mathcal D.
\]
(One set may now come from the whole \(\lambda\)-system \(\mathcal D\); only the other is restricted to
\(\mathcal P\).)

*Step 3. Remove the remaining restriction (the "double-bootstrapping" flip).* Now fix *any*
\(B \in \mathcal D\). Step 2 tells us that for every \(A \in \mathcal P\), we have
\(B \cap A = A \cap B \in \mathcal D\). In the notation of Step 1 (now with \(B\) as the fixed set!),
this says every \(A \in \mathcal P\) belongs to \(\mathcal D_B\):
\[
\mathcal P \subseteq \mathcal D_B \quad \text{for every } B \in \mathcal D.
\]
Again by Step 1, \(\mathcal D_B\) is a \(\lambda\)-system containing \(\mathcal P\), so minimality of
\(\mathcal D\) gives \(\mathcal D \subseteq \mathcal D_B\). That means for every \(A \in \mathcal D\)
and \(B \in \mathcal D\), we have \(A \cap B \in \mathcal D\).

Thus \(\mathcal D\) is both a \(\pi\)-system and a \(\lambda\)-system. By Lemma 4.4, \(\mathcal D\) is a
\(\sigma\)-algebra containing \(\mathcal P\), so \(\sigma(\mathcal P) \subseteq \mathcal D \subseteq \mathcal L\).
\(\blacksquare\)

                      |
                      v  [Step 2: fix A in P, use minimality of D inside D_A]
   Halfway upgrade:   P  x  D  ---> in D
                      |
                      v  [Step 3: fix B in D, use minimality of D inside D_B]
   Full upgrade:      D  x  D  ---> in D   (so D is a pi-system + lambda-system = sigma-algebra!)
#+end_example

** Uniqueness of finite measures

Combining Example 4.5 with Dynkin's lemma immediately gives our main uniqueness theorem.

#+begin_quote
*Theorem 4.7 (Uniqueness of finite measures).* Suppose that \(\mathcal F = \sigma(\mathcal P)\) for a
\(\pi\)-system \(\mathcal P\). If finite measures \(\mu\) and \(\nu\) on \((\Omega, \mathcal F)\) satisfy
\(\mu(\Omega) = \nu(\Omega) < \infty\) and \(\mu(A) = \nu(A)\) for every \(A \in \mathcal P\), then
\(\mu = \nu\) on \(\mathcal F\).
#+end_quote

*Proof.* By Example 4.5, the agreement class \(\mathcal D = \{A \in \mathcal F : \mu(A) = \nu(A)\}\) is
a \(\lambda\)-system. By hypothesis, \(\mathcal P \subseteq \mathcal D\). Dynkin's lemma (Theorem 4.6)
therefore gives \(\mathcal F = \sigma(\mathcal P) \subseteq \mathcal D\), which means
\(\mu(A) = \nu(A)\) for all \(A \in \mathcal F\). \(\blacksquare\)

*Practical checklist for applying Theorem 4.7:*
1. Identify a collection \(\mathcal P \subseteq \mathcal F\) on which \(\mu\) and \(\nu\) agree.
2. Verify that \(\mathcal P\) is a \(\pi\)-system (closed under intersections) and generates
   \(\mathcal F\).
3. Verify that \(\mu(\Omega) = \nu(\Omega) < \infty\). (For probability measures,
   \(\mu(\Omega) = 1 = \nu(\Omega)\) is automatic! For general finite measures, you must check
   \(\mu(\Omega) = \nu(\Omega)\), which is automatic if \(\Omega \in \mathcal P\).)

* 4.3 Half-lines and finite strings

** Cumulative distribution functions on \(\mathbb R\)

#+begin_quote
*Example 4.8 (A preview of cumulative distribution functions).* Let \(\mu\) and \(\nu\) be probability
measures on \((\mathbb R, \mathcal B(\mathbb R))\) (called *Borel probability measures*). The function
\[
F_\mu(t) := \mu\big((-\infty, t]\big), \qquad t \in \mathbb R,
\]
is the *cumulative distribution function* (CDF) of \(\mu\). Since the half-lines
\(\mathcal P = \{(-\infty, t] : t \in \mathbb R\}\) form a \(\pi\)-system generating
\(\mathcal B(\mathbb R)\) (Chapter 1), Theorem 4.7 gives:
\[
\mu\big((-\infty, t]\big) = \nu\big((-\infty, t]\big) \text{ for every } t \in \mathbb R \implies \mu = \nu \text{ on } \mathcal B(\mathbb R).
\]
Thus a Borel probability measure is completely determined by its values on half-lines.

For two real-valued random variables \(X\) and \(Y\) (possibly defined on *different* probability
spaces!), applying this to their image measures \(\mu = X_\#\mathbb P\) and \(\nu = Y_\#\mathbb P'\)
shows that
\[
\mathbb P(X \le t) = \mathbb P'(Y \le t) \text{ for all } t \in \mathbb R \implies X \text{ and } Y \text{ have the exact same law.}
\]
*(Warning: matching laws means \(\mathbb P(X \in B) = \mathbb P'(Y \in B)\) for all Borel \(B\); it does
NOT mean \(X(\omega) = Y(\omega)\) pointwise! In Example 4.1, the first digit and second digit both have
a fair Bernoulli\((1/2)\) law, even though under \(\nu\) they are never equal.)*

Even better: **rational thresholds suffice!** Suppose \(\mu((-\infty, q]) = \nu((-\infty, q])\) only for
all \(q \in \mathbb Q\). Given any real \(t \in \mathbb R\), choose rationals \(q_n \downarrow t\). Then
\((-\infty, q_n] \downarrow (-\infty, t]\), and continuity of finite measures from above transfers
equality from the rationals \(q_n\) to \(t\). (Alternatively, \(\{(-\infty, q] : q \in \mathbb Q\}\) is
already a \(\pi\)-system generating \(\mathcal B(\mathbb R)\)!)
#+end_quote

** Cylinder sets in infinite coin-toss space

#+begin_quote
*Example 4.9 (Finite strings determine a sequence measure).* Let \(\Omega = \{0,1\}^{\mathbb N}\) be the
set of all infinite binary sequences \(\omega = (\omega_1, \omega_2, \dots)\) with
\(\omega_j \in \{0,1\}\). For any finite binary string \(a_1, \dots, a_n \in \{0,1\}\), define its
*cylinder set*
\[
C(a_1, \dots, a_n) := \{\omega \in \Omega : \omega_1 = a_1, \dots, \omega_n = a_n\},
\]
the event that the first \(n\) tosses come out as \(a_1, \dots, a_n\). Include \(\Omega\) as the
cylinder of length \(0\) (no prescribed digits), and include \(\emptyset\) in the collection
\(\mathcal P\).

*Checking that \(\mathcal P\) is a \(\pi\)-system:* Take two cylinders \(C(a_1, \dots, a_m)\) and
\(C(b_1, \dots, b_n)\) with \(m \le n\).
- *Case 1:* \(a_j \ne b_j\) for some \(j \le m\). No sequence can have its \(j\)-th digit equal to both
  \(a_j\) and \(b_j\), so \(C(a_1, \dots, a_m) \cap C(b_1, \dots, b_n) = \emptyset \in \mathcal P\).
- *Case 2:* \(a_j = b_j\) for all \(1 \le j \le m\). Then the first \(m\) prescriptions of the second
  cylinder match the first cylinder, so the longer string is more restrictive:
  \[
  C(a_1, \dots, a_m) \cap C(b_1, \dots, b_n) = C(b_1, \dots, b_n) \in \mathcal P.
  \]
Intersecting with \(\Omega\) leaves a set unchanged, and intersecting with \(\emptyset\) gives
\(\emptyset\). Thus \(\mathcal P\) is a \(\pi\)-system! Let \(\mathcal F := \sigma(\mathcal P)\) be the
\(\sigma\)-algebra it generates.

By Theorem 4.7, there is **at most one** probability measure \(\mathbb P\) on \((\Omega, \mathcal F)\)
satisfying the fair-coin prescription
\begin{equation}
\mathbb P\big(C(a_1, \dots, a_n)\big) = 2^{-n} \quad \text{for every binary string } (a_1, \dots, a_n). \tag{4.1}
\end{equation}
Note carefully: we have *not* yet proved that such a measure \(\mathbb P\) exists (that requires
Carathéodory's extension theorem in Chapter 5 and Chapter 10). Theorem 4.7 proves *uniqueness* before
*existence*.
#+end_quote

#+begin_quote
*Example 4.10 (Infinitely many ones occur almost surely).* Assuming that the fair-coin measure
\(\mathbb P\) in (4.1) exists, what is the probability that an infinite sequence \(\omega\) contains
*only finitely many ones*?

Let us translate the English statement into countable set operations on cylinders:
1. A sequence \(\omega\) has only finitely many \(1\)s if and only if there is some position
   \(m \ge 0\) after which *every* digit is \(0\). So for each integer \(m \ge 0\), define
   \[
   E_m := \{\omega \in \Omega : \omega_j = 0 \text{ for every integer } j > m\}.
   \]
   Then \(\{\text{finitely many } 1\text{s}\} = \bigcup_{m=0}^\infty E_m\).
2. Fix \(m \ge 0\). To compute \(\mathbb P(E_m)\), first impose only *finitely many* zero requirements
   after position \(m\): for each \(k \ge 1\), define
   \[
   A_k := \{\omega \in \Omega : \omega_{m+1} = \omega_{m+2} = \cdots = \omega_{m+k} = 0\}.
   \]
   In \(A_k\), the first \(m\) digits \((\omega_1, \dots, \omega_m)\) are completely free: there are
   \(2^m\) possible initial strings \((a_1, \dots, a_m) \in \{0,1\}^m\), so \(A_k\) is the disjoint
   union of \(2^m\) cylinders of length \(m+k\):
   \[
   A_k = \bigsqcup_{(a_1,\dots,a_m) \in \{0,1\}^m} C(a_1, \dots, a_m, \underbrace{0, \dots, 0}_{k \text{ zeros}}).
   \]
   By finite additivity and (4.1),
   \[
   \mathbb P(A_k) = 2^m \cdot 2^{-(m+k)} = 2^{-k}.
   \]
3. As \(k\) increases, we add more zero requirements, so \(A_1 \supseteq A_2 \supseteq \cdots\) and a
   sequence satisfies all of them iff it belongs to \(E_m\):
   \[
   A_k \downarrow E_m = \bigcap_{k=1}^\infty A_k.
   \]
   In particular, \(E_m \in \mathcal F\) is measurable, and continuity from above gives
   \[
   \mathbb P(E_m) = \lim_{k\to\infty} \mathbb P(A_k) = \lim_{k\to\infty} 2^{-k} = 0.
   \]
4. Finally, \(\bigcup_{m=0}^\infty E_m\) is a countable union of measurable null sets, so it belongs to
   \(\mathcal F\) and by countable subadditivity (or continuity from below, since
   \(E_0 \subseteq E_1 \subseteq \cdots\)),
   \[
   \mathbb P(\text{finitely many } 1\text{s}) = \mathbb P\Big(\bigcup_{m=0}^\infty E_m\Big) = 0.
   \]
   Taking complements, \(\mathbb P(\text{infinitely many } 1\text{s}) = 1\) ("almost surely"). Notice
   that we used *only* the finite-string probabilities (4.1), countable set operations, and continuity
   of a measure!
#+end_quote

* 4.4 Uniqueness for \(\sigma\)-finite measures

** Intuition

What if \(\mu(\Omega) = \infty\), like Lebesgue measure \(\lambda\) on \(\mathbb R\)? Then the agreement
class \(\mathcal D = \{A : \mu(A) = \nu(A)\}\) is *not* closed under proper differences, because
\(\mu(B) = \nu(B) = \infty\) and \(\mu(A) = \nu(A) = \infty\) does *not* imply
\(\mu(B \setminus A) = \nu(B \setminus A)\) (you cannot subtract \(\infty - \infty\)).

However, if we can exhaust \(\Omega\) by an increasing sequence of sets \(E_n \uparrow \Omega\) *belonging
to the \(\pi\)-system \(\mathcal P\)* on which \(\mu(E_n) = \nu(E_n) < \infty\), we can restrict \(\mu\)
and \(\nu\) to each finite piece \(E_n\), apply the finite uniqueness theorem on \(E_n\), and then let
\(n \to \infty\).

** Statement, proof, and a crucial counterexample

#+begin_quote
*Proposition 4.11 (Uniqueness for \(\sigma\)-finite measures).* Suppose that
\(\mathcal F = \sigma(\mathcal P)\) for a \(\pi\)-system \(\mathcal P\) and that measures \(\mu\) and
\(\nu\) agree on \(\mathcal P\). Assume that there are sets \(E_n \in \mathcal P\) such that
\(E_n \uparrow \Omega\) and \(\mu(E_n) = \nu(E_n) < \infty\) for every \(n\). Then \(\mu = \nu\) on
\(\mathcal F\).
#+end_quote

*Proof.* Fix \(n \ge 1\). Define two restricted measures \(\mu_n, \nu_n\) on \((\Omega, \mathcal F)\) by
\[
\mu_n(A) := \mu(A \cap E_n), \qquad \nu_n(A) := \nu(A \cap E_n) \qquad (A \in \mathcal F).
\]
- Both \(\mu_n\) and \(\nu_n\) are *finite* measures on \((\Omega, \mathcal F)\) with equal total mass:
  \(\mu_n(\Omega) = \mu(E_n) = \nu(E_n) = \nu_n(\Omega) < \infty\).
- For any \(A \in \mathcal P\), since \(E_n \in \mathcal P\) and \(\mathcal P\) is a \(\pi\)-system, we
  have \(A \cap E_n \in \mathcal P\). Since \(\mu\) and \(\nu\) agree on \(\mathcal P\),
  \[
  \mu_n(A) = \mu(A \cap E_n) = \nu(A \cap E_n) = \nu_n(A) \quad \text{for all } A \in \mathcal P.
  \]
- By the finite uniqueness theorem (Theorem 4.7), \(\mu_n = \nu_n\) on all of \(\mathcal F = \sigma(\mathcal P)\);
  that is, \(\mu(A \cap E_n) = \nu(A \cap E_n)\) for every \(A \in \mathcal F\).

Now let \(n \to \infty\): for any \(A \in \mathcal F\), the sets \(A \cap E_n\) increase to
\(A \cap \Omega = A\), so continuity from below gives
\[
\mu(A) = \lim_{n\to\infty} \mu(A \cap E_n) = \lim_{n\to\infty} \nu(A \cap E_n) = \nu(A). \quad \blacksquare
\]

#+begin_quote
*Example 4.12 (Agreement at infinite values is useless).* On \((\mathbb R, \mathcal B(\mathbb R))\),
consider Lebesgue measure \(\lambda\) and twice Lebesgue measure \(\nu = 2\lambda\).
- Both \(\lambda\) and \(2\lambda\) are \(\sigma\)-finite measures on \(\mathbb R\) (since
  \(\lambda([-n,n]) = 2n < \infty\) and \(2\lambda([-n,n]) = 4n < \infty\)).
- Consider the \(\pi\)-system of half-lines \(\mathcal P = \{(-\infty, t] : t \in \mathbb R\}\), which
  generates \(\mathcal B(\mathbb R)\).
- For every half-line \((-\infty, t] \in \mathcal P\), both measures assign the *same* value:
  \[
  \lambda\big((-\infty, t]\big) = \infty = 2\lambda\big((-\infty, t]\big).
  \]
- Yet \(\lambda \ne 2\lambda\) (for instance, \(\lambda((0,1]) = 1 \ne 2 = 2\lambda((0,1])\)).

*Why didn't Proposition 4.11 apply?* Because the exhausting finite-measure sets \(E_n\) must come from
**inside the \(\pi\)-system \(\mathcal P\)**! Every nonempty set in \(\mathcal P = \{(-\infty, t]\}\) has
infinite measure, so no sequence \(E_n \in \mathcal P\) with \(\lambda(E_n) < \infty\) exists. (By
contrast, if we use the \(\pi\)-system \(\mathcal P' = \{(a,b] : a \le b\}\), then \(E_n = (-n, n] \in \mathcal P'\)
has finite measure, and indeed agreement of \(\mu\) and \(\nu\) on all bounded intervals \((a,b]\)
*does* force \(\mu = \nu\).)
#+end_quote

** Additional worked examples

*Example A (Uniqueness of Lebesgue measure on \(\mathcal B(\mathbb R)\)).* Suppose \(\mu\) is any Borel
measure on \(\mathbb R\) such that \(\mu((a,b]) = b - a\) for all \(a \le b\) (with \((a,a] = \emptyset\)).
Let \(\mathcal P = \{(a,b] : -\infty < a \le b < \infty\}\).
- \(\mathcal P\) is a \(\pi\)-system: \((a,b] \cap (c,d] = (\max(a,c), \min(b,d)]\) if
  \(\max(a,c) \le \min(b,d)\), and \(\emptyset = (a,a]\) otherwise.
- \(\sigma(\mathcal P) = \mathcal B(\mathbb R)\).
- The sets \(E_n = (-n, n] \in \mathcal P\) satisfy \(E_n \uparrow \mathbb R\) and
  \(\mu(E_n) = 2n < \infty\).
By Proposition 4.11, there is *at most one* Borel measure on \(\mathbb R\) assigning length \(b-a\) to
every half-open interval \((a,b]\).

*Example B (Why \(\mu(\Omega) = \nu(\Omega)\) cannot be dropped in Theorem 4.7).* Let
\(\Omega = \{1, 2\}\), \(\mathcal F = 2^\Omega\), and \(\mathcal P = \{\{1\}\}\). Clearly \(\mathcal P\)
is a \(\pi\)-system (since \(\{1\} \cap \{1\} = \{1\}\)) and \(\sigma(\mathcal P) = 2^\Omega\) (since
\(\{2\} = \{1\}^c\)). Define two finite measures:
- \(\mu(\{1\}) = 1,\ \mu(\{2\}) = 1\) (total mass \(\mu(\Omega) = 2\)),
- \(\nu(\{1\}) = 1,\ \nu(\{2\}) = 5\) (total mass \(\nu(\Omega) = 6\)).
They agree on \(\mathcal P\), yet \(\mu \ne \nu\) because \(\mu(\Omega) \ne \nu(\Omega)\).

** Common confusions

- *"Any generating family determines a measure":* False! Example 4.1 is the standard counterexample. You
  need the generating family \(\mathcal P\) to be closed under intersections (a \(\pi\)-system).
- *"Every \(\lambda\)-system is a \(\sigma\)-algebra":* False! In Example 4.1, the agreement class of
  \(\mu\) and \(\nu\) is a \(\lambda\)-system of \(8\) sets inside \(2^\Omega\) (which has \(16\) sets),
  because \(A, B \in \mathcal D\) while \(A \cap B \notin \mathcal D\).
- *"\(\mu\) and \(\nu\) are \(\sigma\)-finite and agree on a generating \(\pi\)-system \(\implies \mu=\nu\)":*
  False! As Example 4.12 (\(\lambda\) vs. \(2\lambda\) on half-lines) shows, the \(\sigma\)-finiteness
  exhaustion \(E_n \uparrow \Omega\) must consist of sets **in \(\mathcal P\)** on which \(\mu(E_n) = \nu(E_n) < \infty\).

** Historical remark and classroom route

*Historical remark (A proof about families of sets).* In 1928, Wacław Sierpiński proved a result about
families of sets closely related to Dynkin's lemma (Theorem 4.6). Starting from a family closed under
intersections, he formed the smallest class closed under countable disjoint unions and differences of
nested sets, and proved that it was also closed under intersections using two successive classes of test
sets, just as in Steps 1–3 above. Eugene Dynkin's book *Theory of Markov Processes* (1960) introduced
the names *\(\pi\)-system* and *\(\lambda\)-system* and used the lemma to prove uniqueness of Markov
process probability measures before constructing them.

*Classroom route.* The core thread is Dynkin's lemma followed by finite-measure uniqueness. The two-bit
counterexample (Example 4.1) explains why the intersection hypothesis (\(\pi\)-system) is mandatory, and
half-lines (Example 4.8) and cylinders (Example 4.9) illustrate the payoff. The \(\sigma\)-finite result
(Proposition 4.11) is simply a reduction to the finite case by restricting to \(E_n \in \mathcal P\),
not a second abstract theorem to memorize.

* Worked Exercises

** Exercise 1
Let \(\Omega = \{1, 2, 3, 4\}\). Let \(A = \{1, 2\}\) and \(B = \{2, 3\}\).
1. Is \(\mathcal G = \{A, B\}\) a \(\pi\)-system? What is \(\sigma(\mathcal G)\)?
2. Find all subsets belonging to \(\lambda(\mathcal G)\), the smallest \(\lambda\)-system containing
   \(\mathcal G\). Is \(\lambda(\mathcal G) = \sigma(\mathcal G)\)?

** Solution
1. \(A \cap B = \{2\} \notin \mathcal G\), so \(\mathcal G\) is *not* a \(\pi\)-system. However,
   \(\sigma(\mathcal G)\) must contain \(\{2\} = A \cap B\), hence \(\{1\} = A \setminus \{2\}\),
   \(\{3\} = B \setminus \{2\}\), and \(\{4\} = (A \cup B)^c\). Since \(\sigma(\mathcal G)\) contains
   all four singletons, \(\sigma(\mathcal G) = 2^\Omega\) (all \(16\) subsets of \(\Omega\)).
2. Let \(\mathcal D\) be the collection of subsets \(E \subseteq \Omega\) containing an even number of
   elements (\(0, 2,\) or \(4\) elements), EXCEPT that we be careful: notice that in Example 4.1 (with
   \(1\leftrightarrow 10, 2\leftrightarrow 11, 3\leftrightarrow 01, 4\leftrightarrow 00\)), the two
   measures \(\mu = \frac12(\delta_2 + \delta_4)\) and \(\nu = \frac12(\delta_1 + \delta_3)\) satisfy
   \(\mu(A) = \frac12 = \nu(A)\) and \(\mu(B) = \frac12 = \nu(B)\).
   A subset \(E \subseteq \Omega\) has \(\mu(E) = \nu(E)\) iff
   \[
   |E \cap \{2, 4\}| = |E \cap \{1, 3\}|.
   \]
   There are \(1 + 4 + 1 = 6\) such sets:
   - \(0\) from each: \(\emptyset\) (1 set).
   - \(1\) from \(\{2,4\}\) and \(1\) from \(\{1,3\}\): \(2 \times 2 = 4\) sets, namely
     \(\{1,2\}, \{2,3\}, \{3,4\}, \{1,4\}\).
   - \(2\) from each: \(\Omega\) (1 set).
   Let \(\mathcal L = \{\emptyset, \{1,2\}, \{2,3\}, \{3,4\}, \{1,4\}, \Omega\}\) (6 sets). Let us check
   that \(\mathcal L\) is a \(\lambda\)-system!
   - \(\Omega \in \mathcal L\).
   - Complements of the four \(2\)-element sets in \(\mathcal L\) are again in \(\mathcal L\)
     (\(\{1,2\}^c = \{3,4\}\), \(\{2,3\}^c = \{1,4\}\)).
   - Proper differences \(F \setminus E\) for \(E \subseteq F\) in \(\mathcal L\): if \(E = \emptyset\)
     or \(F = \Omega\) this is just complements; and no two distinct \(2\)-element sets are nested!
   - Increasing unions on a finite space are eventually constant.
   Thus \(\mathcal L\) is a \(\lambda\)-system of \(6\) sets containing \(\mathcal G = \{A, B\}\).
   Conversely, any \(\lambda\)-system containing \(A=\{1,2\}\) and \(B=\{2,3\}\) must contain
   \(\Omega\), \(\emptyset\), \(A^c = \{3,4\}\), and \(B^c = \{1,4\}\), so \(\lambda(\mathcal G) = \mathcal L\).
   In particular, \(\lambda(\mathcal G)\) has \(6\) sets while \(\sigma(\mathcal G) = 2^\Omega\) has
   \(16\) sets, so \(\lambda(\mathcal G) \ne \sigma(\mathcal G)\).

** Exercise 2
Let \((\Omega, \mathcal F, \mathbb P)\) be a probability space, and let \(\mathcal P_1, \mathcal P_2 \subseteq \mathcal F\)
be two \(\pi\)-systems that are *independent*, meaning
\[
\mathbb P(A \cap B) = \mathbb P(A)\,\mathbb P(B) \quad \text{for all } A \in \mathcal P_1,\ B \in \mathcal P_2.
\]
Use Dynkin's \(\pi\)-\(\lambda\) lemma twice to prove that the generated \(\sigma\)-algebras
\(\sigma(\mathcal P_1)\) and \(\sigma(\mathcal P_2)\) are independent (i.e., the above equality holds
for all \(A \in \sigma(\mathcal P_1)\) and \(B \in \sigma(\mathcal P_2)\)).

** Solution
*(This is a preview of Chapter 13 and one of the most important applications of Theorem 4.7!)*
1. *Step 1 (Fix \(A \in \mathcal P_1\), extend \(B\) to \(\sigma(\mathcal P_2)\)):* Fix \(A \in \mathcal P_1\).
   Consider the collection
   \[
   \mathcal L_A := \{B \in \mathcal F : \mathbb P(A \cap B) = \mathbb P(A)\,\mathbb P(B)\}.
   \]
   We check that \(\mathcal L_A\) is a \(\lambda\)-system:
   - \(\Omega \in \mathcal L_A\) since \(\mathbb P(A \cap \Omega) = \mathbb P(A) = \mathbb P(A)\,\mathbb P(\Omega)\).
   - If \(B_1, B_2 \in \mathcal L_A\) with \(B_1 \subseteq B_2\), then \(A \cap B_1 \subseteq A \cap B_2\)
     and \(A \cap (B_2 \setminus B_1) = (A \cap B_2) \setminus (A \cap B_1)\), so
     \[
     \mathbb P\big(A \cap (B_2 \setminus B_1)\big) = \mathbb P(A \cap B_2) - \mathbb P(A \cap B_1)
     = \mathbb P(A)\big(\mathbb P(B_2) - \mathbb P(B_1)\big) = \mathbb P(A)\,\mathbb P(B_2 \setminus B_1).
     \]
   - If \(B_n \in \mathcal L_A\) with \(B_n \uparrow B\), then \(A \cap B_n \uparrow A \cap B\), so
     continuity from below gives
     \(\mathbb P(A \cap B) = \lim_n \mathbb P(A \cap B_n) = \lim_n \mathbb P(A)\mathbb P(B_n) = \mathbb P(A)\mathbb P(B)\).
   By hypothesis, \(\mathcal P_2 \subseteq \mathcal L_A\). Since \(\mathcal P_2\) is a \(\pi\)-system,
   Dynkin's lemma gives \(\sigma(\mathcal P_2) \subseteq \mathcal L_A\).
2. *Step 2 (Fix \(B \in \sigma(\mathcal P_2)\), extend \(A\) to \(\sigma(\mathcal P_1)\)):* Fix any
   \(B \in \sigma(\mathcal P_2)\) and define
   \[
   \mathcal L'_B := \{A \in \mathcal F : \mathbb P(A \cap B) = \mathbb P(A)\,\mathbb P(B)\}.
   \]
   By the exact same verification as in Step 1, \(\mathcal L'_B\) is a \(\lambda\)-system. By Step 1,
   \(\mathcal P_1 \subseteq \mathcal L'_B\). Since \(\mathcal P_1\) is a \(\pi\)-system, Dynkin's lemma
   gives \(\sigma(\mathcal P_1) \subseteq \mathcal L'_B\). Thus
   \(\mathbb P(A \cap B) = \mathbb P(A)\,\mathbb P(B)\) for all \(A \in \sigma(\mathcal P_1)\) and
   \(B \in \sigma(\mathcal P_2)\).

*(Alternative proof via Theorem 4.7:* If \(\mathbb P(A) = 0\) the identity is \(0 = 0\); if
\(\mathbb P(A) > 0\), compare the finite measures \(B \mapsto \mathbb P(A \cap B)\) and
\(B \mapsto \mathbb P(A)\mathbb P(B)\) on \(\sigma(\mathcal P_2)\)!)*

** Exercise 3
Prove that Lebesgue measure \(\lambda\) on \((\mathbb R, \mathcal B(\mathbb R))\) is *translation-invariant*
and *reflection-invariant*: for every Borel set \(B \in \mathcal B(\mathbb R)\) and every \(x \in \mathbb R\),
\[
\lambda(B + x) = \lambda(B) \quad \text{and} \quad \lambda(-B) = \lambda(B),
\]
where \(B + x = \{y + x : y \in B\}\) and \(-B = \{-y : y \in B\}\), assuming only that
\(\lambda((a,b]) = b - a\) for all \(a \le b\).

** Solution
Fix \(x \in \mathbb R\) and define \(\mu(B) := \lambda(B + x)\) for \(B \in \mathcal B(\mathbb R)\).
(Note that \(B + x\) is the preimage of \(B\) under the continuous map \(y \mapsto y - x\), so
\(B + x \in \mathcal B(\mathbb R)\) and \(\mu\) is a Borel measure.)
Let \(\mathcal P = \{(a,b] : -\infty < a \le b < \infty\}\), which is a \(\pi\)-system generating
\(\mathcal B(\mathbb R)\). For any \((a,b] \in \mathcal P\),
\[
\mu((a,b]) = \lambda((a+x, b+x]) = (b+x) - (a+x) = b - a = \lambda((a,b]).
\]
Moreover, \(E_n = (-n, n] \in \mathcal P\) satisfies \(E_n \uparrow \mathbb R\) and
\(\mu(E_n) = \lambda(E_n) = 2n < \infty\). By Proposition 4.11, \(\mu = \lambda\) on
\(\mathcal B(\mathbb R)\), i.e. \(\lambda(B + x) = \lambda(B)\).

Similarly, for reflection, define \(\nu(B) := \lambda(-B)\). Since \(-(a,b] = [-b, -a)\), let us check
\(\nu((a,b])\): we know singletons have Lebesgue measure zero (\(\lambda(\{c\}) = \lim_{n\to\infty}\lambda((c-1/n, c]) = 0\)),
so
\[
\nu((a,b]) = \lambda([-b, -a)) = \lambda((-b, -a]) + \lambda(\{-b\}) - \lambda(\{-a\}) = (-a) - (-b) = b - a = \lambda((a,b]).
\]
Again \(E_n = (-n, n] \in \mathcal P\) has \(\nu(E_n) = \lambda(E_n) = 2n < \infty\), so Proposition 4.11
gives \(\nu = \lambda\) on \(\mathcal B(\mathbb R)\).

** Exercise 4
In the infinite fair-coin space \(\Omega = \{0,1\}^{\mathbb N}\) of Example 4.9, let \(B\) be the event
that the sequence \(\omega = (\omega_1, \omega_2, \dots)\) is *eventually constant* (meaning either all
digits after some index \(m\) are \(0\), or all digits after some index \(m\) are \(1\)). Show that
\(B \in \mathcal F\) and find \(\mathbb P(B)\).

** Solution
Let \(E_m^{(0)} = \{\omega : \omega_j = 0 \text{ for all } j > m\}\) and
\(E_m^{(1)} = \{\omega : \omega_j = 1 \text{ for all } j > m\}\). Then
\[
B = \Big(\bigcup_{m=0}^\infty E_m^{(0)}\Big) \cup \Big(\bigcup_{m=0}^\infty E_m^{(1)}\Big).
\]
In Example 4.10 we showed that each \(E_m^{(0)} \in \mathcal F\) with \(\mathbb P(E_m^{(0)}) = 0\). By the
exact same argument (replacing \(0\) with \(1\) in the cylinders \(A_k\)), each
\(E_m^{(1)} \in \mathcal F\) with \(\mathbb P(E_m^{(1)}) = \lim_{k\to\infty} 2^m 2^{-(m+k)} = 0\).
As a countable union of measurable null sets, \(B \in \mathcal F\) and \(\mathbb P(B) = 0\).

** Exercise 5
Let \(\mu\) and \(\nu\) be two probability measures on \((\mathbb R^2, \mathcal B(\mathbb R^2))\).
Prove that if \(\mu\big((-\infty, s] \times (-\infty, t]\big) = \nu\big((-\infty, s] \times (-\infty, t]\big)\)
for all \(s, t \in \mathbb R\), then \(\mu = \nu\) on \(\mathcal B(\mathbb R^2)\).

** Solution
Let \(\mathcal P = \{(-\infty, s] \times (-\infty, t] : s, t \in \mathbb R\}\).
1. *\(\mathcal P\) is a \(\pi\)-system:* The intersection of two south-west quadrants is another
   south-west quadrant:
   \[
   \big((-\infty, s_1] \times (-\infty, t_1]\big) \cap \big((-\infty, s_2] \times (-\infty, t_2]\big)
   = (-\infty, \min(s_1, s_2)] \times (-\infty, \min(t_1, t_2)] \in \mathcal P.
   \]
2. *\(\mathcal P\) generates \(\mathcal B(\mathbb R^2)\):* From \(\mathcal P\), set differences inside
   \(\sigma(\mathcal P)\) produce strips \((a, b] \times (-\infty, t]\) and rectangles
   \((a, b] \times (c, d]\), and every open set in \(\mathbb R^2\) is a countable union of open
   rectangles \((a,b) \times (c,d) = \bigcup_{n=1}^\infty (a, b-1/n] \times (c, d-1/n]\). Thus
   \(\sigma(\mathcal P) = \mathcal B(\mathbb R^2)\).
3. Both \(\mu\) and \(\nu\) are probability measures, so \(\mu(\mathbb R^2) = 1 = \nu(\mathbb R^2)\).
By Theorem 4.7, \(\mu = \nu\) on \(\mathcal B(\mathbb R^2)\). (This is why the joint CDF
\(F(s,t) = \mathbb P(X \le s, Y \le t)\) uniquely determines the joint distribution of \((X,Y)\).)

** Exercise 6
Suppose \(\mathcal C\) is a collection of subsets of \(\Omega\). Explain why the intersection of all
\(\pi\)-systems containing \(\mathcal C\) is a \(\pi\)-system, and describe explicitly the smallest
\(\pi\)-system containing \(\mathcal C\).

** Solution
If \(\{\mathcal P_\alpha\}_{\alpha \in I}\) is the family of all \(\pi\)-systems containing \(\mathcal C\)
(nonempty since \(2^\Omega\) is one), and \(A, B \in \bigcap_\alpha \mathcal P_\alpha\), then for every
\(\alpha\) we have \(A, B \in \mathcal P_\alpha\), hence \(A \cap B \in \mathcal P_\alpha\), so
\(A \cap B \in \bigcap_\alpha \mathcal P_\alpha\).
Unlike \(\sigma(\mathcal C)\) or \(\lambda(\mathcal C)\) (which cannot be written down by finite formulas),
the smallest \(\pi\)-system containing a nonempty collection \(\mathcal C\) has a very simple explicit
description: it is simply the set of all *finite intersections* of sets from \(\mathcal C\):
\[
\pi(\mathcal C) = \{A_1 \cap A_2 \cap \cdots \cap A_k : k \ge 1,\ A_1, \dots, A_k \in \mathcal C\},
\]
because the intersection of two finite intersections of sets in \(\mathcal C\) is again a finite
intersection of sets in \(\mathcal C\).

*Key takeaways*

- Keep *uniqueness* (this chapter) and *existence* (Chapter 5) separate.
- To prove two finite measures \(\mu\) and \(\nu\) are equal on \(\mathcal F = \sigma(\mathcal P)\), check
  three things: (1) \(\mathcal P\) is a \(\pi\)-system, (2) \(\mu = \nu\) on \(\mathcal P\), and
  (3) \(\mu(\Omega) = \nu(\Omega) < \infty\).
- The agreement class \(\{A \in \mathcal F : \mu(A) = \nu(A)\}\) is a \(\lambda\)-system (when
  \(\mu(\Omega) = \nu(\Omega) < \infty\)), not necessarily a \(\sigma\)-algebra until Dynkin's lemma is
  applied.
- For infinite measures, you must find an increasing exhaustion \(E_n \uparrow \Omega\) of finite-measure
  sets **belonging to the \(\pi\)-system \(\mathcal P\)**.
