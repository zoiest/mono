* Overview

** What problem does this chapter solve?

In calculus, the Riemann integral \(\int_a^b f(x)\,dx\) chops the *domain* \([a, b]\) into thin vertical
intervals and approximates \(f\) by step functions on those intervals. That requires the domain to be an
interval in \(\mathbb R^d\) and requires \(f\) to oscillate very little on small intervals (so badly
discontinuous functions like \(\mathbf 1_{\mathbb Q}\) are not Riemann integrable, and pointwise limits
often destroy Riemann integrability).

Lebesgue's revolutionary idea (1902) is to chop the **range** (vertical axis) instead of the domain!
To integrate a function \(f : \Omega \to [0, \infty]\) on *any* measure space \((\Omega, \mathcal F, \mu)\):
1. Ask where \(f(\omega)\) falls into horizontal slices \([k 2^{-n}, (k+1) 2^{-n})\); because \(f\) is
   measurable, the preimage \(A_{n,k}\) of each slice is a measurable set in \(\mathcal F\)!
2. Multiply the height of each slice by the measure \(\mu(A_{n,k})\) and add them up (a **simple
   function** integral).
3. Pass to the limit as the slices get finer and higher.

Because nothing in the definition of a simple function requires intervals, distances, or coordinates,
this single construction unifies:
- **Discrete weighted sums and series** (when \(\mu\) is counting measure on \(\mathbb N\) or a discrete
  probability law);
- **Lebesgue integrals on \(\mathbb R^d\)** (when \(\mu = \lambda\));
- **Expectations \(\mathbb E[X] = \int_\Omega X\,d\mathbb P\)** on an abstract probability space!

Even better, Lebesgue integration comes equipped with three powerhouse limit theorems — **Monotone
Convergence (MCT)**, **Fatou's Lemma**, and **Dominated Convergence (DCT)** — that tell you *exact,
easily checkable conditions* under which you can swap a limit and an integral:
\(\lim_{n\to\infty} \int f_n\,d\mu = \int (\lim_{n\to\infty} f_n)\,d\mu\).

** Probability motivation

In probability theory, the integral of a random variable \(X\) with respect to \(\mathbb P\) is its
**expectation** (or *mean*) \(\mathbb E[X] := \int_\Omega X\,d\mathbb P\).
- For a nonnegative random variable \(X \ge 0\), \(\mathbb E[X]\) is *always* well-defined in
  \([0, \infty]\) (Section 6.2), and for integer-valued \(X \ge 0\) we immediately get the famous tail-sum
  formula \(\mathbb E[X] = \sum_{k=1}^\infty \mathbb P(X \ge k)\) (Example 6.7).
- For a signed random variable \(X = X^+ - X^-\), \(\mathbb E[X]\) is defined and finite iff
  \(\mathbb E[|X|] < \infty\) (Section 6.3).
- How do you actually *compute* \(\mathbb E[h(X)]\) without knowing the abstract sample space \(\Omega\)?
  Theorem 6.12 (**image-measure integration**, often called the *Law of the Unconscious Statistician*)
  proves that \(\mathbb E[h(X)] = \int_{\mathbb R} h(x)\,d(X_\#\mathbb P)(x)\): the expectation depends
  *only* on the distribution (law) \(X_\#\mathbb P\) of \(X\)!

** Roadmap

This chapter follows the **Standard Machine** (also called the *4-step bootstrap*), which is the master
proof template of measure and integration theory:
- *6.1 Step 1 (Simple functions):* Extended measurable functions \(f : \Omega \to [0, \infty]\) and
  dyadic staircase approximation \(s_n \uparrow f\) by simple functions (Lemma 6.2).
- *6.2 Step 2 (Nonnegative functions):* Defining \(\int_\Omega f\,d\mu\) for \(f \ge 0\) and proving the
  **Monotone Convergence Theorem** (Theorem 6.5) and term-by-term integration of nonnegative series
  (Proposition 6.6).
- *6.3 Step 3 (Signed integrable functions):* Decomposition \(f = f^+ - f^-\), integrability
  \(\int |f|\,d\mu < \infty\), linearity, and why conditional convergence is not Lebesgue integration
  (Example 6.11).
- *6.4 Putting the Standard Machine to work:* Image-measure integration \(\int h\,d(X_\#\mu) = \int (h\circ X)\,d\mu\)
  (Theorem 6.12).
- *6.5 Passage to the limit:* **Fatou's lemma** (Theorem 6.13), shrinking spikes (Example 6.14), and the
  **Dominated Convergence Theorem** (Theorem 6.15).

* Approximation by simple functions

** Measurability of extended-real functions and limit operations

Because sums of nonnegative functions and suprema of sequences can equal \(+\infty\), we allow functions
\(f : \Omega \to [0, \infty]\) taking values in the extended nonnegative reals.

#+begin_quote
*Definition (Measurability for \(f : \Omega \to [0, \infty]\)).* A function \(f : \Omega \to [0, \infty]\)
is *measurable* if the upper level set
\[
\{f > t\} := \{\omega \in \Omega : f(\omega) > t\} \in \mathcal F \quad \text{for every real } t \in \mathbb R.
\]
#+end_quote

Let us check that all familiar operations preserve measurability:
1. *The infinite-value set \(\{f = \infty\}\) is measurable:* A number in \([0, \infty]\) equals
   \(\infty\) iff it exceeds every positive integer \(n\), so
   \[
   \{f = \infty\} = \bigcap_{n=1}^\infty \{f > n\} \in \mathcal F.
   \]
2. *Strict lower level sets \(\{f < t\}\) give an equivalent test:* Just as for real-valued functions
   (Chapter 3),
   \[
   \{f < t\} = \bigcup_{m=1}^\infty \{f \le t - 1/m\} = \bigcup_{m=1}^\infty \{f > t - 1/m\}^c,
   \qquad
   \{f > t\} = \bigcup_{m=1}^\infty \{f \ge t + 1/m\} = \bigcup_{m=1}^\infty \{f < t + 1/m\}^c.
   \]
3. *Pointwise suprema, infima, \(\liminf\), \(\limsup\), and pointwise limits:* Given any sequence of
   measurable functions \(f_n : \Omega \to [0, \infty]\), define \(\sup_n f_n\) and \(\inf_n f_n\)
   pointwise at each \(\omega \in \Omega\). Then:
   - \(\sup_n f_n(\omega) > t \iff \text{there exists } n \text{ with } f_n(\omega) > t\), so
     \[
     \Big\{\sup_n f_n > t\Big\} = \bigcup_{n=1}^\infty \{f_n > t\} \in \mathcal F.
     \]
   - \(\inf_n f_n(\omega) < t \iff \text{there exists } n \text{ with } f_n(\omega) < t\), so
     \[
     \Big\{\inf_n f_n < t\Big\} = \bigcup_{n=1}^\infty \{f_n < t\} \in \mathcal F.
     \]
   - Consequently, the *lower limit* and *upper limit*
     \[
     \liminf_{n\to\infty} f_n := \sup_{N \in \mathbb N} \Big(\inf_{n \ge N} f_n\Big)
     \qquad \text{and} \qquad
     \limsup_{n\to\infty} f_n := \inf_{N \in \mathbb N} \Big(\sup_{n \ge N} f_n\Big)
     \]
     are always measurable! In particular, if \(f_n(\omega) \to f(\omega)\) pointwise for every
     \(\omega \in \Omega\), then \(f = \liminf_n f_n = \limsup_n f_n\) is automatically measurable. We
     write \(f_n \uparrow f\) when \(f_n(\omega) \le f_{n+1}(\omega)\) for all \(n, \omega\) and
     \(f_n(\omega) \to f(\omega)\) pointwise.
4. *Sums \(f + g\) and compositions:* If \(f, g : \Omega \to \mathbb R\) (or \(f, g : \Omega \to [0, \infty]\),
   where no undefined \(\infty - \infty\) can occur), then \(f + g\) is measurable because
   \[
   \{f + g > t\} = \bigcup_{q \in \mathbb Q} \big(\{f > q\} \cap \{g > t - q\}\big) \in \mathcal F
   \]
   (indeed, \(f(\omega) + g(\omega) > t \iff f(\omega) > t - g(\omega) \iff\) there exists a rational
   \(q \in \mathbb Q\) with \(f(\omega) > q > t - g(\omega)\)). Finally, if \(\phi : \mathbb R \to \mathbb R\)
   is continuous ( hence Borel measurable, such as \(\phi(x) = \max(x, 0)\) or \(\phi(x) = |x|^p\)) and
   \(f\) is measurable, then \(\phi \circ f\) is measurable since
   \((\phi \circ f)^{-1}(B) = f^{-1}(\phi^{-1}(B)) \in \mathcal F\).

** Simple functions and the dyadic staircase approximation

#+begin_quote
*Definition 6.1 (Nonnegative simple function).* A *nonnegative simple function* on \((\Omega, \mathcal F)\)
is a measurable function \(s : \Omega \to [0, \infty)\) taking only *finitely many finite* nonnegative
values. It can be written in partition form as
\[
s = \sum_{j=1}^m a_j \mathbf 1_{A_j},
\]
where the sets \(A_1, \dots, A_m \in \mathcal F\) form a finite measurable partition of \(\Omega\)
(\(\Omega = \bigsqcup_{j=1}^m A_j\)) and \(a_1, \dots, a_m \in [0, \infty)\) are finite nonnegative real
numbers (including a block with \(a_j = 0\) where \(s = 0\), if needed).
#+end_quote

*(Note: There is NO requirement that the sets \(A_j\) have finite measure! Thus the constant function
\(s = \mathbf 1_\Omega = 1\) is a simple function on every measure space, even when \(\mu(\Omega) = \infty\).)*

Now comes the foundational approximation lemma of Lebesgue integration: *every* nonnegative measurable
function \(f : \Omega \to [0, \infty]\) can be approximated from below by an increasing sequence of
nonnegative simple functions \(s_n \uparrow f\).

#+begin_quote
*Lemma 6.2 (Increasing simple approximation / the dyadic staircase).* Every nonnegative measurable
function \(f : \Omega \to [0, \infty]\) is the pointwise limit of an increasing sequence of nonnegative
simple functions \(0 \le s_1 \le s_2 \le \cdots \le f\) with \(s_n \uparrow f\) pointwise on \(\Omega\).
#+end_quote

*Proof.* For any finite real number \(r \ge 0\), let \(\lfloor r \rfloor\) denote the floor of \(r\)
(the largest integer \(\le r\)). At stage \(n \ge 1\), we do two things to \(f(\omega)\):
1. **Truncate at height \(n\)** (replace \(f(\omega)\) by \(\min\{f(\omega), n\}\), so heights \(\ge n\)
   including \(\infty\) are capped at \(n\));
2. **Round down to the nearest multiple of \(2^{-n}\)** by defining
\begin{equation}
s_n(\omega) := 2^{-n} \big\lfloor 2^n \min\{f(\omega), n\} \big\rfloor. \tag{6.1}
\end{equation}
Equivalently, in terms of horizontal slices of thickness \(2^{-n}\):
\[
s_n(\omega) = \begin{cases}
k 2^{-n}, & \text{if } k 2^{-n} \le f(\omega) < (k+1) 2^{-n} \text{ for } k \in \{0, 1, \dots, n 2^n - 1\},\\
n, & \text{if } f(\omega) \ge n.
\end{cases}
\]
Let us check all required properties of \(s_n\):
- *\(s_n\) is a nonnegative simple function:* It takes at most \(n 2^n + 1\) distinct finite values in
  \(\{0, 2^{-n}, 2 \cdot 2^{-n}, \dots, n\}\), and each level set
  \(\{s_n = k 2^{-n}\} = \{k 2^{-n} \le f < (k+1) 2^{-n}\} \in \mathcal F\) (or \(\{f \ge n\} \in \mathcal F\))
  is measurable because \(f\) is measurable.
- *Monotonicity (\(s_n \le s_{n+1} \le f\)):* When we pass from \(n\) to \(n+1\), each slice
  \([k 2^{-n}, (k+1) 2^{-n})\) is bisected into two half-slices
  \([2k 2^{-(n+1)}, (2k+1) 2^{-(n+1)})\) (where \(s_{n+1} = s_n\)) and
  \([(2k+1) 2^{-(n+1)}, (2k+2) 2^{-(n+1)})\) (where \(s_{n+1} = s_n + 2^{-(n+1)} > s_n\)), and the
  ceiling cap increases from \(n\) to \(n+1\). In neither case can the rounded-down value decrease! Thus
  \(s_n(\omega) \le s_{n+1}(\omega) \le f(\omega)\) for all \(\omega\).
- *Pointwise convergence (\(s_n(\omega) \uparrow f(\omega)\)):*
  - *Case 1:* \(f(\omega) < \infty\). Then for all \(n > f(\omega)\), the truncation cap \(n\) is inactive
    (\(\min\{f(\omega), n\} = f(\omega)\)), and rounding down to the grid \(2^{-n}\mathbb Z\) makes an
    error of at most one grid step:
    \[
    0 \le f(\omega) - s_n(\omega) < 2^{-n} \xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{} 0.
    \]
  - *Case 2:* \(f(\omega) = \infty\). Then \(\min\{f(\omega), n\} = n\) for every \(n\), so
    \(s_n(\omega) = n \uparrow \infty = f(\omega)\). \(\blacksquare\)

* Nonnegative integrals and expectations

** Step 1 of the Standard Machine: Integrating simple functions

For a nonnegative simple function \(s = \sum_{j=1}^m a_j \mathbf 1_{A_j}\) represented by a measurable
partition \(\Omega = \bigsqcup_{j=1}^m A_j\) with \(a_j \in [0, \infty)\), we define its integral by
\[
\int_\Omega s\,d\mu := \sum_{j=1}^m a_j \mu(A_j) \in [0, \infty],
\]
using the measure-theory convention **\(0 \cdot \infty = 0\)** (so a block where \(s = 0\) contributes
\(0\) to the integral even if \(\mu(A_j) = \infty\)).

*Well-definedness (independence of the partition):* What if we write the same simple function \(s\) using
a *different* measurable partition, say \(s = \sum_{k=1}^\ell b_k \mathbf 1_{B_k}\) where
\(\Omega = \bigsqcup_{k=1}^\ell B_k\)?
Take the common refinement \(\{A_j \cap B_k\}_{j,k}\):
- By finite additivity of \(\mu\), \(\mu(A_j) = \sum_{k=1}^\ell \mu(A_j \cap B_k)\) and
  \(\mu(B_k) = \sum_{j=1}^m \mu(A_j \cap B_k)\).
- Whenever \(A_j \cap B_k \ne \emptyset\), picking any point \(\omega \in A_j \cap B_k\) gives
  \(a_j = s(\omega) = b_k\)! And whenever \(A_j \cap B_k = \emptyset\), \(\mu(A_j \cap B_k) = 0\) so both
  \(a_j \mu(A_j \cap B_k) = 0\) and \(b_k \mu(A_j \cap B_k) = 0\).
Therefore:
\[
\sum_{j=1}^m a_j \mu(A_j) = \sum_{j=1}^m \sum_{k=1}^\ell a_j \mu(A_j \cap B_k)
= \sum_{j=1}^m \sum_{k=1}^\ell b_k \mu(A_j \cap B_k) = \sum_{k=1}^\ell b_k \mu(B_k).
\]
Thus \(\int_\Omega s\,d\mu\) depends only on the function \(s\), not on how we partition \(\Omega\)!

*Elementary rules for simple functions \(s, t \ge 0\) and \(c \in [0, \infty)\):* Using the common
refinement partition \(\{A_j \cap B_k\}\) (on which \(s + t\) takes the constant value \(a_j + b_k\)),
we immediately get:
1. *Additivity:* \(\int_\Omega (s + t)\,d\mu = \int_\Omega s\,d\mu + \int_\Omega t\,d\mu\).
2. *Nonnegative homogeneity:* \(\int_\Omega (c s)\,d\mu = c \int_\Omega s\,d\mu\) (using \(0 \cdot \infty = 0\)
   if \(c = 0\)).
3. *Monotonicity:* If \(s \le t\) everywhere on \(\Omega\), then \(a_j \le b_k\) on every nonempty
   \(A_j \cap B_k\), so \(\int_\Omega s\,d\mu \le \int_\Omega t\,d\mu\).

** Step 2 of the Standard Machine: Integrating general nonnegative functions

#+begin_quote
*Definition 6.3 (Integral of a nonnegative measurable function).* For any nonnegative measurable function
\(f : \Omega \to [0, \infty]\), its *integral* with respect to \(\mu\) is defined by
\begin{equation}
\int_\Omega f\,d\mu := \sup\left\{\int_\Omega s\,d\mu : s \text{ is nonnegative and simple, } 0 \le s \le f\right\} \in [0, \infty]. \tag{6.2}
\end{equation}
On a probability space \((\Omega, \mathcal F, \mathbb P)\), this integral is called the *expectation* of
\(f\), written \(\mathbb E[f] := \int_\Omega f\,d\mathbb P\).
#+end_quote

*Sanity checks:*
- *Consistency for simple functions:* If \(f\) is already a simple function, then every simple
  \(s \le f\) has \(\int_\Omega s\,d\mu \le \int_\Omega f\,d\mu\) by simple-function monotonicity, and
  \(s = f\) is an admissible choice in (6.2), so the supremum equals \(\int_\Omega f\,d\mu\).
- *Monotonicity (\(f \le g \implies \int_\Omega f\,d\mu \le \int_\Omega g\,d\mu\)):* If \(0 \le f \le g\),
  then every simple function \(s\) satisfying \(0 \le s \le f\) also satisfies \(0 \le s \le g\), so the
  supremum for \(f\) is taken over a subset of the collection for \(g\).

#+begin_quote
*Example 6.4 (A bounded function can have an infinite integral).* Boundedness is a property of a function
alone; whether its integral is finite also depends on the measure \(\mu\)!
- On \((\mathbb N, 2^{\mathbb N}, \#)\) with counting measure \(\#\), the constant function
  \(f = \mathbf 1_{\mathbb N} \equiv 1\) is bounded (\(0 \le f \le 1\)), yet
  \[
  \int_{\mathbb N} 1\,d\# = 1 \cdot \#(\mathbb N) = \infty.
  \]
- On any probability space \((\Omega, \mathcal F, \mathbb P)\), the same constant function \(f \equiv 1\)
  has \(\mathbb E[1] = 1 \cdot \mathbb P(\Omega) = 1\).
#+end_quote

** The Monotone Convergence Theorem (MCT)

Definition 6.3 defines \(\int_\Omega f\,d\mu\) as a supremum over *all* simple functions \(s \le f\). To
compute or manipulate integrals in practice, we want to know that for *any* increasing sequence of
nonnegative measurable functions \(f_n \uparrow f\) (and in particular for the dyadic simple staircase
\(s_n \uparrow f\) of Lemma 6.2!), the integrals \(\int_\Omega f_n\,d\mu\) converge to
\(\int_\Omega f\,d\mu\).

#+begin_quote
*Theorem 6.5 (Monotone Convergence Theorem — MCT).* Let \(f_n, f : \Omega \to [0, \infty]\) be
measurable functions. If \(f_n \uparrow f\) *almost everywhere* with respect to \(\mu\) (meaning there is
a measurable set \(N \in \mathcal F\) with \(\mu(N) = 0\) such that
\(0 \le f_1(\omega) \le f_2(\omega) \le \cdots\) and \(f_n(\omega) \uparrow f(\omega)\) for all
\(\omega \notin N\)), then
\[
\int_\Omega f_n\,d\mu \uparrow \int_\Omega f\,d\mu \quad \text{as } n \to \infty.
\]
(No finiteness or \(\sigma\)-finiteness assumption on \(\mu\) is required!)
#+end_quote

*Proof.* We unpack the proof into two clean steps: first reduce "almost everywhere" to "everywhere", then
use the famous \(0 < \theta < 1\) trick.

*Step 1. Reduce to convergence everywhere on \(\Omega\).*
Let \(N \in \mathcal F\) with \(\mu(N) = 0\) be the exceptional null set outside of which
\(f_n(\omega) \uparrow f(\omega)\). Observe that for any nonnegative simple function
\(s = \sum_{j=1}^m a_j \mathbf 1_{A_j}\), setting \(s\) to zero on \(N\) produces the simple function
\(s \mathbf 1_{N^c} = \sum_{j=1}^m a_j \mathbf 1_{A_j \setminus N}\), and since
\(\mu(A_j) = \mu(A_j \setminus N) + \mu(A_j \cap N) = \mu(A_j \setminus N) + 0\),
\[
\int_\Omega s \mathbf 1_{N^c}\,d\mu = \int_\Omega s\,d\mu.
\]
Consequently, for *any* nonnegative measurable function \(g\), multiplying by \(\mathbf 1_{N^c}\) leaves
its integral unchanged: if \(0 \le s \le g\) is simple, then \(0 \le s \mathbf 1_{N^c} \le g \mathbf 1_{N^c}\)
has the same integral \(\int s\,d\mu\), so taking suprema over \(s\) gives
\(\int_\Omega g\,d\mu \le \int_\Omega g \mathbf 1_{N^c}\,d\mu\), while \(g \mathbf 1_{N^c} \le g\) gives
the reverse inequality!
Therefore, we may replace each \(f_n\) by \(\tilde f_n := f_n \mathbf 1_{N^c}\) and \(f\) by
\(\tilde f := f \mathbf 1_{N^c}\): they remain measurable, their integrals are unchanged, and now
\(\tilde f_n(\omega) \uparrow \tilde f(\omega)\) holds **at every point \(\omega \in \Omega\)** (since on
\(N\) we have \(0 \uparrow 0\)). From now on we assume \(f_n(\omega) \uparrow f(\omega)\) for all
\(\omega \in \Omega\).

*Step 2. Prove the everywhere-convergent case.*
- *Easy inequality (\(\le\)):* Since \(f_n \le f_{n+1} \le f\) everywhere, monotonicity of the integral
  gives \(\int_\Omega f_n\,d\mu \le \int_\Omega f_{n+1}\,d\mu \le \int_\Omega f\,d\mu\) for all \(n\), so
  the limit \(\lim_{n\to\infty} \int_\Omega f_n\,d\mu\) exists in \([0, \infty]\) and satisfies
  \[
  \lim_{n\to\infty} \int_\Omega f_n\,d\mu \le \int_\Omega f\,d\mu.
  \]
- *Reverse inequality (\(\ge\), the \(0 < \theta < 1\) trick):* To show that
  \(\lim_{n\to\infty} \int_\Omega f_n\,d\mu \ge \int_\Omega f\,d\mu\), by Definition 6.3 it suffices to
  take an arbitrary nonnegative simple function \(0 \le s \le f\) and show that
  \(\lim_{n\to\infty} \int_\Omega f_n\,d\mu \ge \int_\Omega s\,d\mu\).
  Why can't we just look at \(\{f_n \ge s\}\)? Because \(f_n(\omega)\) might approach \(s(\omega)\)
  strictly from below (e.g. \(1 - 1/n \uparrow 1\)), so \(\{f_n \ge s\}\) could be **empty for every
  finite \(n\)**!
  To fix this, fix a slightly smaller fraction \(0 < \theta < 1\) (think of \(\theta = 0.99\)), and
  define the measurable sets
  \[
  E_n := \{\omega \in \Omega : f_n(\omega) \ge \theta s(\omega)\} \in \mathcal F.
  \]
  Because \(f_n(\omega) \uparrow f(\omega) \ge s(\omega)\):
  - First, \(E_1 \subseteq E_2 \subseteq \cdots\) (since \(f_n\) increases with \(n\)).
  - Second, **\(E_n \uparrow \Omega\)**: at any point \(\omega\) where \(s(\omega) = 0\), we have
    \(f_n(\omega) \ge 0 = \theta s(\omega)\) for all \(n \ge 1\) (so \(\omega \in E_1\)); and at any
    point \(\omega\) where \(s(\omega) > 0\), since \(s(\omega) < \infty\) (simple functions are finite!)
    and \(\theta < 1\), we have \(\lim_{n\to\infty} f_n(\omega) = f(\omega) \ge s(\omega) > \theta s(\omega)\),
    so \(f_n(\omega) \ge \theta s(\omega)\) for all sufficiently large \(n\)!
  Now compare functions on \(\Omega\): on \(E_n\) we have \(f_n \ge \theta s\), and outside \(E_n\) we
  have \(f_n \ge 0\), so pointwise on all of \(\Omega\),
  \[
  f_n \ge \theta s \mathbf 1_{E_n}.
  \]
  Write \(s = \sum_{j=1}^m a_j \mathbf 1_{A_j}\). Then
  \(\theta s \mathbf 1_{E_n} = \sum_{j=1}^m \theta a_j \mathbf 1_{A_j \cap E_n}\) is a simple function,
  so monotonicity of the integral gives
  \[
  \int_\Omega f_n\,d\mu \ge \int_\Omega \theta s \mathbf 1_{E_n}\,d\mu = \theta \sum_{j=1}^m a_j \mu(A_j \cap E_n).
  \]
  Now let \(n \to \infty\): since \(A_j \cap E_n \uparrow A_j\), **continuity of the measure \(\mu\) from
  below** gives \(\mu(A_j \cap E_n) \uparrow \mu(A_j)\) for each \(j = 1, \dots, m\)! Since this is a
  finite sum of \(m\) terms,
  \[
  \lim_{n\to\infty} \int_\Omega f_n\,d\mu \ge \theta \sum_{j=1}^m a_j \mu(A_j) = \theta \int_\Omega s\,d\mu.
  \]
  This holds for every \(0 < \theta < 1\); letting \(\theta \uparrow 1\) gives
  \(\lim_{n\to\infty} \int_\Omega f_n\,d\mu \ge \int_\Omega s\,d\mu\). Finally, taking the supremum over
  all simple \(0 \le s \le f\) yields \(\lim_{n\to\infty} \int_\Omega f_n\,d\mu \ge \int_\Omega f\,d\mu\).
  \(\blacksquare\)

** Linearity and nonnegative series (Tonelli for series)

With MCT in hand, we can immediately upgrade additivity from simple functions to *all* nonnegative
measurable functions, and then to infinite series of nonnegative functions!

#+begin_quote
*Proposition 6.6 (Addition and nonnegative series).* For nonnegative measurable functions
\(f, g, f_n : \Omega \to [0, \infty]\) and \(c \in [0, \infty)\), integration is additive and
homogeneous (with \(0 \cdot \infty = 0\)):
\[
\int_\Omega (f + g)\,d\mu = \int_\Omega f\,d\mu + \int_\Omega g\,d\mu, \qquad
\int_\Omega (c f)\,d\mu = c \int_\Omega f\,d\mu.
\]
Moreover, for any sequence of nonnegative measurable functions \(f_n \ge 0\), we can always interchange
an infinite sum and an integral:
\begin{equation}
\int_\Omega \Big(\sum_{n=1}^\infty f_n\Big)\,d\mu = \sum_{n=1}^\infty \int_\Omega f_n\,d\mu. \tag{6.3}
\end{equation}
#+end_quote

*Proof.*
1. *Finite additivity:* By Lemma 6.2, choose simple functions \(s_n \uparrow f\) and \(t_n \uparrow g\).
   Then \(s_n + t_n\) are simple functions with \(s_n + t_n \uparrow f + g\). By additivity for simple
   functions and the Monotone Convergence Theorem (applied three times),
   \[
   \int_\Omega (f + g)\,d\mu = \lim_{n\to\infty} \int_\Omega (s_n + t_n)\,d\mu
   = \lim_{n\to\infty} \left(\int_\Omega s_n\,d\mu + \int_\Omega t_n\,d\mu\right)
   = \int_\Omega f\,d\mu + \int_\Omega g\,d\mu.
   \]
   Homogeneity \(\int (cf)\,d\mu = c \int f\,d\mu\) follows similarly from \(c s_n \uparrow c f\) (for
   \(c > 0\); \(c = 0\) is \(0 = 0\)).
2. *Infinite series (6.3):* Let \(S_N := \sum_{n=1}^N f_n\) be the partial sums. Since \(f_n \ge 0\),
   we have \(S_N \uparrow \sum_{n=1}^\infty f_n\). Applying finite additivity to \(S_N\) and then MCT as
   \(N \to \infty\) gives
   \[
   \int_\Omega \Big(\sum_{n=1}^\infty f_n\Big)\,d\mu
   = \lim_{N\to\infty} \int_\Omega S_N\,d\mu
   = \lim_{N\to\infty} \sum_{n=1}^N \int_\Omega f_n\,d\mu
   = \sum_{n=1}^\infty \int_\Omega f_n\,d\mu. \quad \blacksquare
   \]

#+begin_quote
*Example 6.7 (Counting the layers of an integer-valued random variable).* Let \(X\) be a nonnegative
random variable on \((\Omega, \mathcal F, \mathbb P)\) taking values in \(\{0, 1, 2, \dots\} \cup \{\infty\}\).
Pointwise at every \(\omega \in \Omega\), any nonnegative integer (or \(\infty\)) equals the number of
positive integers \(k \ge 1\) that it reaches or exceeds (the "layer-cake representation"):
\[
X(\omega) = \sum_{k=1}^\infty \mathbf 1_{\{X \ge k\}}(\omega).
\]
Applying Proposition 6.6 (equation (6.3)) to the nonnegative functions \(f_k = \mathbf 1_{\{X \ge k\}}\)
immediately yields the **tail-sum formula for expectation**:
\[
\mathbb E[X] = \sum_{k=1}^\infty \mathbb E\big[\mathbf 1_{\{X \ge k\}}\big] = \sum_{k=1}^\infty \mathbb P(X \ge k).
\]
Notice that because all terms are nonnegative, this identity holds in \([0, \infty]\) *without* assuming
in advance that \(\mathbb E[X] < \infty\)!

Let us test this on two concrete laws on \(\Omega = \mathbb N\) with \(X(n) = n\):
1. *Geometric\((1/2)\) law:* Assign outcome \(n \in \mathbb N\) probability \(\mathbb P(\{n\}) = 2^{-n}\).
   Then \(\mathbb P(X \ge k) = \sum_{n=k}^\infty 2^{-n} = 2^{1-k}\), so
   \[
   \mathbb E[X] = \sum_{n=1}^\infty n 2^{-n} = \sum_{k=1}^\infty \mathbb P(X \ge k) = \sum_{k=1}^\infty 2^{1-k} = 1 + \frac12 + \frac14 + \cdots = 2.
   \]
2. *A heavy-tailed law with \(X < \infty\) everywhere but \(\mathbb E[X] = \infty\):* Assign outcome
   \(n \in \mathbb N\) probability \(\mathbb P(\{n\}) = \frac{1}{n(n+1)}\). Since
   \(\frac{1}{n(n+1)} = \frac{1}{n} - \frac{1}{n+1}\), the telescoping sum gives
   \[
   \mathbb P(X \ge k) = \sum_{n=k}^\infty \left(\frac{1}{n} - \frac{1}{n+1}\right) = \frac{1}{k}.
   \]
   In particular, \(\mathbb P(\mathbb N) = \mathbb P(X \ge 1) = 1\), so \(\mathbb P\) is a valid
   probability measure and \(X(n) = n < \infty\) is finite at **every** outcome! Yet its expectation is
   the harmonic series:
   \[
   \mathbb E[X] = \sum_{k=1}^\infty \mathbb P(X \ge k) = \sum_{k=1}^\infty \frac{1}{k} = \infty.
   \]
   (If we truncate \(X\) at height \(m\), \(\mathbb E[\min\{X, m\}] = \sum_{k=1}^m \frac1k < \infty\), and
   MCT sends \(\sum_{k=1}^m \frac1k \uparrow \infty = \mathbb E[X]\).) **Moral:** *A random variable can
   be finite everywhere without having a finite expectation!*
#+end_quote

* Signed functions and integrability

** Null sets and almost-everywhere agreement

#+begin_quote
*Lemma 6.8.*
1. A nonnegative measurable function \(f : \Omega \to [0, \infty]\) has \(\int_\Omega f\,d\mu = 0\) if and
   only if \(f = 0\) almost everywhere (that is, \(\mu(\{f > 0\}) = 0\)).
2. If \(f, g : \Omega \to [0, \infty]\) are measurable and \(f = g\) almost everywhere, then
   \(\int_\Omega f\,d\mu = \int_\Omega g\,d\mu\).
#+end_quote

*Proof.*
1. *(\(\implies\), Chebyshev/Markov trick):* Suppose \(\int_\Omega f\,d\mu = 0\). Notice that the set where
   \(f > 0\) can be exhausted by sets where \(f\) is bounded below by \(1/m\):
   \[
   \{f > 0\} = \bigcup_{m=1}^\infty \{f > 1/m\}.
   \]
   For each \(m \ge 1\), the simple function \(\frac{1}{m} \mathbf 1_{\{f > 1/m\}}\) satisfies
   \(0 \le \frac{1}{m} \mathbf 1_{\{f > 1/m\}} \le f\) everywhere on \(\Omega\)! By monotonicity of the
   integral,
   \[
   \frac{1}{m} \mu\big(\{f > 1/m\}\big) = \int_\Omega \frac{1}{m}\mathbf 1_{\{f > 1/m\}}\,d\mu \le \int_\Omega f\,d\mu = 0,
   \]
   which forces \(\mu(\{f > 1/m\}) = 0\) for every \(m \ge 1\). By countable subadditivity (or continuity
   from below), \(\mu(\{f > 0\}) \le \sum_{m=1}^\infty \mu(\{f > 1/m\}) = 0\).
   *(\(\Longleftarrow\)):* Conversely, if \(N = \{f > 0\}\) has \(\mu(N) = 0\), then any nonnegative simple
   function \(0 \le s \le f\) vanishes outside \(N\), so each positive-coefficient block \(A_j \subseteq N\)
   has \(\mu(A_j) = 0\), giving \(\int_\Omega s\,d\mu = 0\) and hence \(\int_\Omega f\,d\mu = 0\).
2. If \(f = g\) outside a measurable null set \(N\) (\(\mu(N) = 0\)), split \(f = f\mathbf 1_N + f\mathbf 1_{N^c}\)
   and \(g = g\mathbf 1_N + g\mathbf 1_{N^c}\). By part 1, \(\int f\mathbf 1_N\,d\mu = 0 = \int g\mathbf 1_N\,d\mu\),
   and on \(N^c\) we have \(f\mathbf 1_{N^c} = g\mathbf 1_{N^c}\), so Proposition 6.6 gives
   \(\int_\Omega f\,d\mu = \int_\Omega f\mathbf 1_{N^c}\,d\mu = \int_\Omega g\mathbf 1_{N^c}\,d\mu = \int_\Omega g\,d\mu\).
   \(\blacksquare\)

** Step 3 of the Standard Machine: Positive/negative parts and integrability

For a real-valued measurable function \(f : \Omega \to \mathbb R\), we decompose \(f\) into its
**positive part** \(f^+\) and **negative part** \(f^-\):
\[
f^+(\omega) := \max\{f(\omega), 0\} \ge 0, \qquad f^-(\omega) := \max\{-f(\omega), 0\} \ge 0.
\]
*(Watch out for the sign! Both \(f^+\) and \(f^-\) are **nonnegative** functions: \(f^-\) records the
magnitude of the negative values of \(f\).)* At every point \(\omega \in \Omega\), we have the two key
identities:
\[
f = f^+ - f^- \qquad \text{and} \qquad |f| = f^+ + f^-.
\]
In particular, \(0 \le f^+ \le |f|\) and \(0 \le f^- \le |f|\), so \(\int_\Omega |f|\,d\mu < \infty\) if
and only if *both* \(\int_\Omega f^+\,d\mu < \infty\) and \(\int_\Omega f^-\,d\mu < \infty\).

#+begin_quote
*Definition 6.9 (Integrable function and signed integral).* A real-valued measurable function
\(f : \Omega \to \mathbb R\) is *integrable* (with respect to \(\mu\)) if
\[
\int_\Omega |f|\,d\mu < \infty.
\]
Its *integral* is then the finite real number
\[
\int_\Omega f\,d\mu := \int_\Omega f^+\,d\mu - \int_\Omega f^-\,d\mu \in \mathbb R.
\]
For an integrable random variable \(X\) on a probability space, this is its *expectation*
\(\mathbb E[X] = \mathbb E[X^+] - \mathbb E[X^-]\).
#+end_quote

*(Remark on extended integrals:* If one of \(\int f^+\,d\mu, \int f^-\,d\mu\) is finite and the other is
\(\infty\), one can still assign \(\int f\,d\mu\) the extended value \(+\infty\) or \(-\infty\). However,
if **both** \(\int f^+\,d\mu = \infty\) and \(\int f^-\,d\mu = \infty\), the integral \(\int_\Omega f\,d\mu\)
is **completely undefined** because \(\infty - \infty\) is meaningless! In this course, the word
**integrable** *always* means \(\int_\Omega |f|\,d\mu < \infty\).)*

** Linearity, monotonicity, and the triangle inequality for integrable functions

Let us check that integrable functions form a vector space and that \(f \mapsto \int_\Omega f\,d\mu\) is
linear, monotone, and satisfies the triangle inequality:
1. *Vector space:* If \(f, g\) are integrable and \(c \in \mathbb R\), then \(|cf| = |c|\,|f|\) and
   \(|f + g| \le |f| + |g|\) (pointwise triangle inequality), so monotonicity and Proposition 6.6 give
   \[
   \int_\Omega |f + g|\,d\mu \le \int_\Omega |f|\,d\mu + \int_\Omega |g|\,d\mu < \infty.
   \]
2. *Additivity \(\int (f+g)\,d\mu = \int f\,d\mu + \int g\,d\mu\):* How do we prove this when
   \((f+g)^+ \ne f^+ + g^+\) (because positive and negative parts of \(f\) and \(g\) can cancel)?
   Write \(f + g\) in two ways:
   \[
   (f + g)^+ - (f + g)^- = f + g = (f^+ - f^-) + (g^+ - g^-).
   \]
   Move all minus signs to the opposite sides so that **every term is nonnegative**:
   \[
   (f + g)^+ + f^- + g^- = (f + g)^- + f^+ + g^+.
   \]
   Now apply Proposition 6.6 (additivity for nonnegative functions!) to both sides:
   \[
   \int_\Omega (f + g)^+\,d\mu + \int_\Omega f^-\,d\mu + \int_\Omega g^-\,d\mu
   = \int_\Omega (f + g)^-\,d\mu + \int_\Omega f^+\,d\mu + \int_\Omega g^+\,d\mu.
   \]
   Since \(f, g, f+g\) are integrable, all six integrals are **finite real numbers**, so we can rearrange
   them by ordinary real subtraction:
   \[
   \int_\Omega (f + g)^+\,d\mu - \int_\Omega (f + g)^-\,d\mu
   = \left(\int_\Omega f^+\,d\mu - \int_\Omega f^-\,d\mu\right)
   + \left(\int_\Omega g^+\,d\mu - \int_\Omega g^-\,d\mu\right).
   \]
3. *Scalar multiplication \(\int (cf)\,d\mu = c \int f\,d\mu\):* For \(c \ge 0\), \((cf)^+ = c f^+\) and
   \((cf)^- = c f^-\); for \(c < 0\), \((cf)^+ = |c| f^-\) and \((cf)^- = |c| f^+\), which swaps the two
   parts and pulls out \(c = -|c|\).
4. *Monotonicity and the integral triangle inequality:* If \(f \le g\) are integrable, then \(g - f \ge 0\),
   so \(\int g\,d\mu - \int f\,d\mu = \int (g - f)\,d\mu \ge 0\). Applying monotonicity to
   \(-|f| \le f \le |f|\) gives \(-\int |f|\,d\mu \le \int f\,d\mu \le \int |f|\,d\mu\), i.e.
   \[
   \left|\int_\Omega f\,d\mu\right| \le \int_\Omega |f|\,d\mu.
   \]
5. *Almost-everywhere invariance:* If \(f, g\) are measurable and \(f = g\) almost everywhere, then
   \(f^+ = g^+\), \(f^- = g^-\), and \(|f| = |g|\) almost everywhere, so by Lemma 6.8 \(f\) is integrable
   iff \(g\) is integrable, and their integrals are equal. *(Caution: both \(f\) and \(g\) must be
   measurable! On an incomplete measure space, changing \(f\) on a non-measurable subset of a null set can
   destroy measurability.)*

#+begin_quote
*Example 6.10 (One payoff function, two probability measures).* On a 6-sided die
\(\Omega = \{1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6\}\) with \(\mathcal F = 2^\Omega\), consider the payoff random variable
\[
X(\omega) = \begin{cases} +3, & \text{if } \omega \in \{2, 4, 6\} \text{ (even)},\\ -1, & \text{if } \omega \in \{1, 3, 5\} \text{ (odd)}.\end{cases}
\]
Here \(X^+ = 3\,\mathbf 1_{\{2,4,6\}}\) and \(X^- = 1\,\mathbf 1_{\{1,3,5\}}\).
- *Under a fair die \(\mathbb P_{\text{fair}}(\{k\}) = 1/6\):* Both \(\{2,4,6\}\) and \(\{1,3,5\}\) have
  probability \(3/6 = 1/2\), so
  \[
  \mathbb E_{\text{fair}}[X] = 3 \cdot \frac12 - 1 \cdot \frac12 = 1.
  \]
- *Under a loaded die \(\mathbb P(\{k\}) = k/21\) (for \(k = 1, \dots, 6\)):* Now
  \(\mathbb P(\{2,4,6\}) = \frac{2+4+6}{21} = \frac{12}{21} = \frac47\) and
  \(\mathbb P(\{1,3,5\}) = \frac{1+3+5}{21} = \frac{9}{21} = \frac37\). For the *exact same function* \(X\),
  the expectation is now
  \[
  \mathbb E[X] = 3 \cdot \frac47 - 1 \cdot \frac37 = \frac{9}{7}.
  \]
A random variable is a measurable function on \((\Omega, \mathcal F)\), but its expectation is a joint
property of the function *and* the probability measure.
#+end_quote

#+begin_quote
*Example 6.11 (Why conditional summation is NOT a Lebesgue integral).* On \((\mathbb N, 2^{\mathbb N}, \#)\)
with counting measure \(\#\), consider the alternating harmonic sequence
\[
f(n) = \frac{(-1)^{n+1}}{n} \qquad (n = 1, 2, 3, \dots).
\]
In first-year calculus, the *ordered* alternating series \(\sum_{n=1}^\infty \frac{(-1)^{n+1}}{n} = 1 - \frac12 + \frac13 - \frac14 + \cdots = \ln 2\)
converges conditionally because we sum in the specific order \(1, 2, 3, \dots\) where positive and
negative terms cancel.
However, as a Lebesgue integral with respect to counting measure \(\#\):
\[
\int_{\mathbb N} f^+\,d\# = 1 + \frac13 + \frac15 + \cdots = \infty
\qquad \text{and} \qquad
\int_{\mathbb N} f^-\,d\# = \frac12 + \frac14 + \frac16 + \cdots = \infty,
\]
so \(\int_{\mathbb N} |f|\,d\# = \sum_{n=1}^\infty \frac1n = \infty\). Thus **\(f\) is NOT integrable**
with respect to \(\#\), and \(\int_{\mathbb N} f\,d\#\) is undefined! Why? Because a measure space
\((\Omega, \mathcal F, \mu)\) has no built-in linear ordering of its points \(\omega \in \Omega\) (and by
Riemann's rearrangement theorem, rearranging a conditionally convergent series can make it converge to
*any* real number or diverge). Lebesgue integration requires **absolute integrability**
\(\int_\Omega |f|\,d\mu < \infty\) so that the integral is completely independent of how you chop up or
order the space.
#+end_quote

* Integrating with respect to an image measure

** Intuition and the "Standard Machine" in action

In Chapter 3, we saw that a measurable map \(X : (\Omega, \mathcal F) \to (S, \mathcal S)\) pushes a
measure \(\mu\) on \(\Omega\) forward to the **image measure** \(X_\#\mu\) on \(S\), defined by
\[
(X_\#\mu)(B) := \mu\big(X^{-1}(B)\big) = \mu(\{X \in B\}) \qquad (B \in \mathcal S).
\]
When \(\mu = \mathbb P\) is a probability measure, \(X_\#\mathbb P\) is the **distribution (or law)** of
the random variable \(X\).

Suppose we want to compute the integral (or expectation) of a function \(h(X(\omega))\) of \(X\). Must we
integrate over the big, abstract sample space \(\Omega\), or can we integrate \(h(x)\) directly on the
target space \(S\) against the law \(X_\#\mu\)? Theorem 6.12 says both give the **exact same answer**, and
its proof is the textbook showcase of the 4-step **Standard Machine**:
1. *Step 1 (Indicators \(h = \mathbf 1_B\)):* True by the definition of \(X_\#\mu\)!
2. *Step 2 (Nonnegative simple functions \(h = \sum a_j \mathbf 1_{B_j}\)):* True by linearity!
3. *Step 3 (Nonnegative measurable \(h \ge 0\)):* Approximate \(h_n \uparrow h\) by simple functions and
   apply MCT!
4. *Step 4 (Signed integrable \(h = h^+ - h^-\)):* Apply Step 3 to \(|h|, h^+, h^-\) and subtract!

#+begin_quote
*Theorem 6.12 (Image-measure integration / Change of variables).* Let \(X : (\Omega, \mathcal F) \to (S, \mathcal S)\)
be a measurable map, and let \(X_\#\mu\) be the image measure of \(\mu\) under \(X\).
1. For every nonnegative measurable function \(h : (S, \mathcal S) \to [0, \infty]\),
\begin{equation}
\int_S h\,d(X_\#\mu) = \int_\Omega h\big(X(\omega)\big)\,d\mu(\omega). \tag{6.4}
\end{equation}
2. For a real-valued measurable function \(h : (S, \mathcal S) \to \mathbb R\), \(h\) is integrable with
   respect to \(X_\#\mu\) if and only if \(h \circ X\) is integrable with respect to \(\mu\), and when
   they are integrable, (6.4) holds.
#+end_quote

*Proof (via the Standard Machine).*
- *Step 1 (Indicators):* Let \(h = \mathbf 1_B\) for some \(B \in \mathcal S\). Then
  \((h \circ X)(\omega) = \mathbf 1_B(X(\omega)) = \mathbf 1_{X^{-1}(B)}(\omega)\), so
  \[
  \int_S \mathbf 1_B\,d(X_\#\mu) = (X_\#\mu)(B) = \mu\big(X^{-1}(B)\big) = \int_\Omega \mathbf 1_{X^{-1}(B)}\,d\mu = \int_\Omega (\mathbf 1_B \circ X)\,d\mu.
  \]
- *Step 2 (Nonnegative simple functions):* If \(h = \sum_{j=1}^m a_j \mathbf 1_{B_j}\) is a nonnegative
  simple function on \(S\), then \(h \circ X = \sum_{j=1}^m a_j \mathbf 1_{X^{-1}(B_j)}\) is a
  nonnegative simple function on \(\Omega\). By linearity of the integral and Step 1, (6.4) holds for
  \(h\).
- *Step 3 (General nonnegative measurable functions):* Let \(h : S \to [0, \infty]\) be measurable. By
  Lemma 6.2, choose nonnegative simple functions \(h_n\) on \(S\) with \(h_n \uparrow h\) pointwise on
  \(S\). Then at every \(\omega \in \Omega\), \(h_n(X(\omega)) \uparrow h(X(\omega))\), so
  \(h_n \circ X \uparrow h \circ X\) pointwise on \(\Omega\). Applying the Monotone Convergence Theorem
  (Theorem 6.5) on \((S, \mathcal S, X_\#\mu)\) and on \((\Omega, \mathcal F, \mu)\), together with Step 2
  for each \(h_n\), gives
  \[
  \int_S h\,d(X_\#\mu) = \lim_{n\to\infty} \int_S h_n\,d(X_\#\mu)
  = \lim_{n\to\infty} \int_\Omega (h_n \circ X)\,d\mu
  = \int_\Omega (h \circ X)\,d\mu.
  \]
- *Step 4 (Signed measurable functions):* Let \(h : S \to \mathbb R\) be measurable. Since
  \(|h \circ X| = |h| \circ X \ge 0\), applying Step 3 to the nonnegative function \(|h|\) gives
  \[
  \int_S |h|\,d(X_\#\mu) = \int_\Omega |h \circ X|\,d\mu,
  \]
  so the left-hand side is finite iff the right-hand side is finite. When they are finite, applying
  Step 3 to \(h^+\) and \(h^-\) (noting \((h \circ X)^+ = h^+ \circ X\) and
  \((h \circ X)^- = h^- \circ X\)) and subtracting yields (6.4). \(\blacksquare\)

*Probabilistic payoff (Example 6.10 revisited).* In probability language, Theorem 6.12 says that for any
random variable \(X\) with law \(\mu_X := X_\#\mathbb P\),
\[
\mathbb E\big[h(X)\big] = \int_\Omega h(X(\omega))\,d\mathbb P(\omega) = \int_S h(x)\,d\mu_X(x).
\]
In the loaded-die payoff of Example 6.10, \(X\) only takes two values: \(+3\) (with probability \(4/7\))
and \(-1\) (with probability \(3/7\)). Thus the image measure (law) of \(X\) on \(\mathbb R\) is simply
\(\mu_X = \frac47 \delta_3 + \frac37 \delta_{-1}\). Integrating \(h(x) = x\) against \(\mu_X\) on
\(\mathbb R\) immediately gives \(3 \cdot \frac47 + (-1) \cdot \frac37 = \frac97\), without ever needing
the 6-point die space \(\Omega\) again! (Notice also that \(\mu_X\) is a discrete measure here, *not*
Lebesgue measure.)

* Passage to the limit

** Fatou's lemma: what survives without monotonicity?

The Monotone Convergence Theorem (Theorem 6.5) requires \(0 \le f_1 \le f_2 \le \cdots\) to be
*increasing*. What if \(f_n \ge 0\) is a sequence of nonnegative measurable functions that is *not*
increasing? As Example 6.14 below shows, mass can escape into narrower and narrower spikes (or escape to
infinity), causing the integral of the pointwise limit to be **strictly smaller** than the limit of the
integrals. However, mass can only be *lost* in the limit, never created out of nowhere! **Fatou's lemma**
makes this precise.

#+begin_quote
*Theorem 6.13 (Fatou's lemma).* For any sequence of nonnegative measurable functions
\(f_n : \Omega \to [0, \infty]\),
\[
\int_\Omega \Big(\liminf_{n\to\infty} f_n\Big)\,d\mu \le \liminf_{n\to\infty} \int_\Omega f_n\,d\mu.
\]
#+end_quote

*(Memory aid for the direction of the inequality: "Integral of \(\liminf\) \(\le\) \(\liminf\) of
integrals" — in the pointwise limit on the left, spikes/mass can disappear before you integrate, making
the left-hand side smaller!)*

*Proof.* Recall the definition of the pointwise lower limit:
\[
\liminf_{n\to\infty} f_n = \sup_{n \ge 1} \Big(\inf_{k \ge n} f_k\Big).
\]
Define the auxiliary sequence \(g_n := \inf_{k \ge n} f_k\) for \(n \ge 1\).
1. Each \(g_n : \Omega \to [0, \infty]\) is a nonnegative measurable function, and as \(n\) increases we
   take the infimum over a *smaller* set of indices \(\{k : k \ge n\}\), so \(g_n\) **increases** with
   \(n\):
   \[
   0 \le g_1 \le g_2 \le \cdots \quad \text{and} \quad g_n \uparrow \liminf_{n\to\infty} f_n \text{ pointwise on }\Omega.
   \]
2. By the Monotone Convergence Theorem (Theorem 6.5) applied to \(g_n \uparrow \liminf_n f_n\),
   \[
   \int_\Omega \Big(\liminf_{n\to\infty} f_n\Big)\,d\mu = \lim_{n\to\infty} \int_\Omega g_n\,d\mu.
   \]
3. On the other hand, for every fixed \(n\) and every \(k \ge n\), we have \(g_n = \inf_{j \ge n} f_j \le f_k\)
   pointwise on \(\Omega\). By monotonicity of the integral,
   \[
   \int_\Omega g_n\,d\mu \le \int_\Omega f_k\,d\mu \quad \text{for all } k \ge n.
   \]
   Taking the infimum over all \(k \ge n\) on the right-hand side gives
   \[
   \int_\Omega g_n\,d\mu \le \inf_{k \ge n} \int_\Omega f_k\,d\mu.
   \]
4. Now take \(\lim_{n\to\infty}\) on both sides (using step 2 on the left):
   \[
   \int_\Omega \Big(\liminf_{n\to\infty} f_n\Big)\,d\mu = \lim_{n\to\infty} \int_\Omega g_n\,d\mu
   \le \lim_{n\to\infty} \left(\inf_{k \ge n} \int_\Omega f_k\,d\mu\right)
   = \liminf_{n\to\infty} \int_\Omega f_n\,d\mu. \quad \blacksquare
   \]

#+begin_quote
*Example 6.14 (Shrinking spikes: strict inequality in Fatou's lemma).* On \(\Omega = (0, 1)\) with
Lebesgue measure \(\lambda\), define
\[
f_n(x) := n\,\mathbf 1_{(0, 1/n)}(x) \qquad (n \ge 1,\ x \in (0, 1)).
\]
- *Pointwise limit:* Fix *any* point \(x \in (0, 1)\). Since \(x > 0\), for all \(n > 1/x\) we have
  \(1/n < x\), so \(x \notin (0, 1/n)\) and therefore \(f_n(x) = 0\)! Thus \(f_n(x) \to 0\) at **every**
  point \(x \in (0, 1)\), so \(\liminf_{n\to\infty} f_n = 0\) and
  \[
  \int_{(0,1)} \Big(\liminf_{n\to\infty} f_n\Big)\,d\lambda = \int_{(0,1)} 0\,d\lambda = 0.
  \]
- *Limit of integrals:* For each \(n \ge 1\), the spike has height \(n\) and base length \(1/n\), so
  \[
  \int_{(0,1)} f_n\,d\lambda = n \cdot \lambda\big((0, 1/n)\big) = n \cdot \frac{1}{n} = 1 \quad \text{for every } n \ge 1!
  \]
Thus Fatou's inequality reads **\(0 < 1\)** (strict inequality!). Even though \(\int f_n\,d\lambda = 1\) is
uniformly bounded and \(f_n \to 0\) pointwise everywhere on \((0, 1)\), we cannot swap \(\lim\) and
\(\int\).
#+end_quote

** The Dominated Convergence Theorem (DCT)

How do we prevent the mass of \(f_n\) from escaping into a tall thin spike (like Example 6.14) or escaping
to \(\infty\) (like \(\mathbf 1_{(n, n+1)}\) on \(\mathbb R\))? By trapping all the functions \(|f_n|\)
under a *single fixed integrable roof* \(g \in L^1(\mu)\): \(|f_n| \le g\) for all \(n\), with
\(\int_\Omega g\,d\mu < \infty\)!

#+begin_quote
*Theorem 6.15 (Dominated Convergence Theorem — DCT).* Suppose that \(f_n : \Omega \to \mathbb R\) are
real-valued measurable functions such that:
1. *(Pointwise a.e. convergence)* \(f_n \to f\) almost everywhere for some measurable function
   \(f : \Omega \to \mathbb R\), and
2. *(Domination)* There exists a nonnegative **integrable** function \(g : \Omega \to [0, \infty]\)
   (meaning \(\int_\Omega g\,d\mu < \infty\)) such that
   \[
   |f_n| \le g \quad \text{almost everywhere, for every } n \ge 1.
   \]
Then \(f_n\) and \(f\) are integrable, and as \(n \to \infty\),
\[
\int_\Omega |f_n - f|\,d\mu \longrightarrow 0 \qquad \text{and} \qquad
\int_\Omega f_n\,d\mu \longrightarrow \int_\Omega f\,d\mu.
\]
#+end_quote

*Proof.*
- *Step 1 (Clean up null sets):* For each \(n \ge 1\), the inequality \(|f_n| \le g\) can fail on a
  measurable null set \(N_n\), and \(f_n(\omega) \to f(\omega)\) can fail on a measurable null set
  \(N_0\). Also, since \(\int_\Omega g\,d\mu < \infty\), the set \(N_\infty := \{g = \infty\}\) has
  measure zero (by Exercise 1 below, or because \(m \mathbf 1_{\{g = \infty\}} \le g \implies \mu(\{g=\infty\}) \le \frac1m \int g\,d\mu \to 0\)).
  The countable union \(N := N_0 \cup N_\infty \cup \bigcup_{n=1}^\infty N_n \in \mathcal F\) still has
  \(\mu(N) = 0\). By Lemma 6.8, setting \(f\), every \(f_n\), and \(g\) to \(0\) on \(N\) preserves
  measurability and leaves all their integrals unchanged! After this modification,
  \(f_n(\omega) \to f(\omega)\) and \(|f_n(\omega)| \le g(\omega) < \infty\) hold **at every point
  \(\omega \in \Omega\)**.
  Taking the limit as \(n \to \infty\) gives \(|f(\omega)| \le g(\omega)\) for all \(\omega \in \Omega\).
  Since \(\int_\Omega g\,d\mu < \infty\), both \(f\) and every \(f_n\) are integrable.
- *Step 2 (Apply Fatou's lemma to \(2g - |f_n - f| \ge 0\)):* Define the error functions
  \[
  h_n := |f_n - f|.
  \]
  By the triangle inequality, \(0 \le h_n \le |f_n| + |f| \le g + g = 2g\) everywhere on \(\Omega\), and
  \(h_n \to 0\) pointwise everywhere on \(\Omega\).
  We want to show \(\int_\Omega h_n\,d\mu \to 0\). Since \(h_n\) is not monotone, we cannot apply MCT,
  and applying Fatou directly to \(h_n\) only gives the useless \(0 \le \liminf_n \int h_n\,d\mu\). Instead,
  flip \(h_n\) upside down under the roof \(2g\): the functions
  \[
  2g - h_n \ge 0
  \]
  are **nonnegative** measurable functions, and \(2g - h_n \to 2g\) pointwise on \(\Omega\)! Applying
  Fatou's lemma (Theorem 6.13) to \(2g - h_n \ge 0\) gives
  \[
  \int_\Omega 2g\,d\mu = \int_\Omega \liminf_{n\to\infty} (2g - h_n)\,d\mu
  \le \liminf_{n\to\infty} \int_\Omega (2g - h_n)\,d\mu
  = \int_\Omega 2g\,d\mu - \limsup_{n\to\infty} \int_\Omega h_n\,d\mu.
  \]
  *(Note: In the last step we used linearity \(\int (2g - h_n)\,d\mu = \int 2g\,d\mu - \int h_n\,d\mu\)
  and the real-sequence identity \(\liminf_{n\to\infty} (c - a_n) = c - \limsup_{n\to\infty} a_n\) for the
  **finite** constant \(c = 2\int_\Omega g\,d\mu < \infty\) and bounded sequence
  \(0 \le a_n = \int_\Omega h_n\,d\mu \le c\).)*
- *Step 3 (Cancel the finite constant \(2\int_\Omega g\,d\mu\)):* Subtracting the finite real number
  \(2\int_\Omega g\,d\mu < \infty\) from both sides yields
  \[
  \limsup_{n\to\infty} \int_\Omega h_n\,d\mu \le 0.
  \]
  Since \(\int_\Omega h_n\,d\mu \ge 0\) for every \(n\), we have
  \[
  0 \le \liminf_{n\to\infty} \int_\Omega h_n\,d\mu \le \limsup_{n\to\infty} \int_\Omega h_n\,d\mu \le 0,
  \]
  which proves \(\lim_{n\to\infty} \int_\Omega |f_n - f|\,d\mu = 0\)!
- *Step 4 (Conclude \(\int f_n\,d\mu \to \int f\,d\mu\)):* By linearity and the integral triangle
  inequality,
  \[
  \left|\int_\Omega f_n\,d\mu - \int_\Omega f\,d\mu\right|
  = \left|\int_\Omega (f_n - f)\,d\mu\right|
  \le \int_\Omega |f_n - f|\,d\mu \xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{} 0. \quad \blacksquare
  \]

** Examples: checking the dominating function

#+begin_quote
*Example 6.16 (Checking when a valid dominating function exists).*
1. *Success on \((0, 1)\):* Let \(f_n = \mathbf 1_{(0, 1/n)}\) on \((0, 1)\) with Lebesgue measure
   \(\lambda\). Here \(f_n \to 0\) pointwise and \(|f_n| \le 1\). Because \(\lambda((0, 1)) = 1 < \infty\),
   the constant function \(g \equiv 1\) is **integrable** on \((0, 1)\)! Thus DCT applies and gives
   \(\int_{(0,1)} f_n\,d\lambda \to 0\), matching the direct calculation \(\lambda((0, 1/n)) = 1/n \to 0\).
2. *Why the shrinking spikes of Example 6.14 have NO integrable majorant:* For
   \(f_n = n\,\mathbf 1_{(0, 1/n)}\) on \((0, 1)\), could there be an integrable \(g\) with
   \(f_n \le g\) for all \(n\)? No! Any \(g \ge \sup_n f_n\) must satisfy \(g(x) \ge n\) on
   \((1/(n+1), 1/n)\), essentially behaving like \(1/x\), whose integral \(\int_0^1 \frac{dx}{x} = \infty\)
   diverges. (Even without computing \(\sup_n f_n\), DCT itself proves no integrable majorant can exist,
   since \(\int f_n\,d\lambda = 1 \not\to 0\)!)
3. *Why a constant bound \(|f_n| \le 1\) is NOT enough on an infinite measure space ("mass escaping to
   infinity"):* On \((\mathbb N, 2^{\mathbb N}, \#)\) with counting measure \(\#\), let
   \(f_n = \mathbf 1_{\{n\}}\). For each fixed \(k \in \mathbb N\), \(f_n(k) = 0\) for all \(n > k\), so
   \(f_n \to 0\) pointwise everywhere on \(\mathbb N\), and \(|f_n| \le 1\). Yet
   \[
   \int_{\mathbb N} f_n\,d\# = \#(\{n\}) = 1 \not\to 0!
   \]
   Why didn't DCT apply? Because on \((\mathbb N, \#)\), the constant function \(g \equiv 1\) has
   \(\int_{\mathbb N} 1\,d\# = \infty\) (Example 6.4), so \(g \equiv 1\) is **not integrable**! And any
   function dominating every \(f_n = \mathbf 1_{\{n\}}\) must satisfy \(g(n) \ge 1\) for all \(n\), so no
   integrable majorant exists.
   *Rule of thumb:* **A constant bound \(|f_n| \le M\) suffices for DCT on a probability space (or any
   finite measure space, since \(\int M\,d\mu = M\mu(\Omega) < \infty\)), where this special case is often
   called the Bounded Convergence Theorem, but it does NOT suffice when \(\mu(\Omega) = \infty\)!**
#+end_quote

#+begin_quote
*Example 6.17 (Truncating an integrable function).* Let \(f : \Omega \to \mathbb R\) be an integrable
function (\(\int_\Omega |f|\,d\mu < \infty\)). For each \(n \ge 1\), define its bounded truncation at
level \(\pm n\):
\[
f_n(\omega) := \max\big\{-n, \min\{f(\omega), n\}\big\} = \begin{cases}
n, & \text{if } f(\omega) > n,\\
f(\omega), & \text{if } -n \le f(\omega) \le n,\\
-n, & \text{if } f(\omega) < -n.
\end{cases}
\]
Then each \(f_n\) is bounded (\(|f_n| \le n\)), \(f_n(\omega) \to f(\omega)\) pointwise everywhere on
\(\Omega\) (in fact \(f_n(\omega) = f(\omega)\) as soon as \(n \ge |f(\omega)|\)), and
\[
|f_n(\omega)| \le |f(\omega)| \quad \text{for every } n \ge 1 \text{ and } \omega \in \Omega.
\]
Since \(g := |f|\) is integrable by hypothesis, the Dominated Convergence Theorem immediately gives
\[
\int_\Omega |f_n - f|\,d\mu \longrightarrow 0 \quad \text{as } n \to \infty!
\]
Thus every integrable function can be approximated in \(L^1\) by bounded functions. (By contrast, if
\(f \ge 0\) is a nonnegative measurable function with \(\int_\Omega f\,d\mu = \infty\), the truncations
\(\min\{f, n\} \uparrow f\) instead fall under the Monotone Convergence Theorem, giving
\(\int_\Omega \min\{f, n\}\,d\mu \uparrow \infty\).)
#+end_quote

** Common confusions

- *"Simple function means step function on intervals":* No! On \(\mathbb R\), \(\mathbf 1_{\mathbb Q}\) is
  a perfectly valid simple function (\(1 \cdot \mathbf 1_{\mathbb Q} + 0 \cdot \mathbf 1_{\mathbb R \setminus \mathbb Q}\)),
  even though it is discontinuous everywhere and not a step function on intervals.
- *"For MCT, \(f_n \uparrow f\) needs \(\int f\,d\mu < \infty\)":* No! MCT works even when
  \(\int_\Omega f\,d\mu = \infty\) (in which case \(\int_\Omega f_n\,d\mu \uparrow \infty\)) and even when
  \(\mu(\Omega) = \infty\). What MCT *does* require is \(f_n \ge 0\) (nonnegativity!). If
  \(f_n = -\mathbf 1_{(n, \infty)}\) on \((\mathbb R, \lambda)\), then \(f_n \uparrow 0\) pointwise, yet
  \(\int_{\mathbb R} f_n\,d\lambda = -\infty \not\to 0\) because \(f_1\) is not bounded below by an
  integrable function.
- *"For DCT, it is enough that \(|f_n| \le g_n\) with \(\int g_n\,d\mu < \infty\)":* No! In the shrinking
  spikes \(f_n = n \mathbf 1_{(0, 1/n)}\) (Example 6.14), each individual \(f_n\) has
  \(\int f_n\,d\lambda = 1 < \infty\), yet \(\int f_n\,d\lambda \not\to 0\). The dominating function
  \(g\) in DCT must be a **single fixed** integrable function independent of \(n\)!

** Historical remark and classroom route

*Historical remark (When Fatou's inequality is strict).* In his 1906 paper on trigonometric and Taylor
series, Pierre Fatou proved a version of the inequality now known as Fatou's lemma. Immediately
afterwards, he noted that the inequality can be strict and credited this observation to Henri Lebesgue.
The shrinking spikes of Example 6.14 provide the cleanest modern illustration: the supports \((0, 1/n)\)
shrink so that the pointwise limit vanishes everywhere on \((0, 1)\), while the increasing heights \(n\)
keep the area equal to \(1\) at every stage.

*Classroom route.* Recall simple approximation (Lemma 6.2) and the definitions of nonnegative and signed
integrals, state the three convergence theorems (MCT, Fatou, DCT), and prove the image-measure formula
(Theorem 6.12) via the 4-step Standard Machine. Work through the tail-sum expectation (Example 6.7), the
weighted-die payoff (Example 6.10), and the shrinking-spike and escaping-mass counterexamples
(Examples 6.14 and 6.16). Note how \(\int_\Omega |f_n - f|\,d\mu \to 0\) in DCT previews \(L^1\)
convergence in Chapter 7.

** Exercise 1
Let \((\Omega, \mathcal F, \mu)\) be a measure space and let \(f : \Omega \to [0, \infty]\) be measurable.
1. *(Markov's inequality)* Prove that for every \(t > 0\),
   \[
   \mu\big(\{f \ge t\}\big) \le \frac{1}{t} \int_\Omega f\,d\mu.
   \]
2. Deduce that if \(\int_\Omega f\,d\mu < \infty\), then \(f < \infty\) almost everywhere (that is,
   \(\mu(\{f = \infty\}) = 0\)), and the set \(\{f > 0\}\) is \(\sigma\)-finite (meaning it is a
   countable union of measurable sets of finite \(\mu\)-measure).

** Solution
1. Fix \(t > 0\). Consider the nonnegative simple function \(s := t\,\mathbf 1_{\{f \ge t\}}\). At every
   \(\omega \in \{f \ge t\}\) we have \(s(\omega) = t \le f(\omega)\), and at every
   \(\omega \notin \{f \ge t\}\) we have \(s(\omega) = 0 \le f(\omega)\). Thus \(0 \le s \le f\) pointwise
   on \(\Omega\). By monotonicity of the integral,
   \[
   t\,\mu\big(\{f \ge t\}\big) = \int_\Omega t\,\mathbf 1_{\{f \ge t\}}\,d\mu \le \int_\Omega f\,d\mu.
   \]
   Dividing by \(t > 0\) gives Markov's inequality.
2. Suppose \(M := \int_\Omega f\,d\mu < \infty\).
   - Since \(\{f = \infty\} \subseteq \{f \ge n\}\) for every integer \(n \ge 1\), monotonicity of \(\mu\)
     and Markov's inequality give
     \[
     0 \le \mu\big(\{f = \infty\}\big) \le \mu\big(\{f \ge n\}\big) \le \frac{M}{n} \xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{} 0,
     \]
     so \(\mu(\{f = \infty\}) = 0\) (i.e. \(f < \infty\) almost everywhere).
   - Similarly, write \(\{f > 0\} = \bigcup_{m=1}^\infty E_m\) where \(E_m := \{f \ge 1/m\} \in \mathcal F\).
     By Markov's inequality with \(t = 1/m\), each \(E_m\) has
     \[
     \mu(E_m) \le m \int_\Omega f\,d\mu = m M < \infty.
     \]
     Thus \(\{f > 0\}\) is a countable union of finite-measure sets!

** Exercise 2
Let \(f : \Omega \to [0, \infty]\) be a nonnegative measurable function on \((\Omega, \mathcal F, \mu)\).
For every \(A \in \mathcal F\), define
\[
\nu(A) := \int_\Omega f\,\mathbf 1_A\,d\mu.
\]
Prove that \(\nu\) is a **measure** on \((\Omega, \mathcal F)\), and that \(\nu\) is *absolutely
continuous* with respect to \(\mu\) in the sense that \(\mu(A) = 0 \implies \nu(A) = 0\).

** Solution
1. *\(\nu(\emptyset) = 0\):* Since \(f\,\mathbf 1_\emptyset = 0\) everywhere on \(\Omega\),
   \(\nu(\emptyset) = \int_\Omega 0\,d\mu = 0\).
2. *Countable additivity:* Let \(A_1, A_2, \dots \in \mathcal F\) be pairwise disjoint, and let
   \(A := \bigsqcup_{n=1}^\infty A_n\). Then at every \(\omega \in \Omega\),
   \(\mathbf 1_A(\omega) = \sum_{n=1}^\infty \mathbf 1_{A_n}(\omega)\), and multiplying by
   \(f(\omega) \ge 0\) gives
   \[
   f(\omega)\,\mathbf 1_A(\omega) = \sum_{n=1}^\infty f(\omega)\,\mathbf 1_{A_n}(\omega).
   \]
   Since each \(f_n := f\,\mathbf 1_{A_n} \ge 0\) is a nonnegative measurable function, Proposition 6.6
   (equation (6.3), term-by-term integration of nonnegative series) immediately gives
   \[
   \nu\Big(\bigsqcup_{n=1}^\infty A_n\Big) = \int_\Omega \Big(\sum_{n=1}^\infty f\,\mathbf 1_{A_n}\Big)\,d\mu
   = \sum_{n=1}^\infty \int_\Omega f\,\mathbf 1_{A_n}\,d\mu = \sum_{n=1}^\infty \nu(A_n).
   \]
   Thus \(\nu\) is a measure on \((\Omega, \mathcal F)\).
3. *Absolute continuity:* If \(\mu(A) = 0\), then \(f\,\mathbf 1_A = 0\) outside the null set \(A\) (i.e.
   \(f\,\mathbf 1_A = 0\) \(\mu\)-almost everywhere), so Lemma 6.8 gives \(\nu(A) = \int_\Omega f\,\mathbf 1_A\,d\mu = 0\).

** Exercise 3
Compute the limit
\[
\lim_{n\to\infty} \int_0^\infty \frac{n \sin(x/n)}{x (1 + x^2)}\,dx
\]
(where the integral is with respect to Lebesgue measure \(\lambda\) on \((0, \infty)\)), carefully
justifying every step using a convergence theorem from this chapter.

** Solution
For \(x \in (0, \infty)\) and \(n \ge 1\), define
\[
f_n(x) := \frac{n \sin(x/n)}{x (1 + x^2)} = \frac{\sin(x/n)}{x/n} \cdot \frac{1}{1 + x^2}.
\]
1. *Pointwise limit:* For each fixed \(x \in (0, \infty)\), as \(n \to \infty\) we have \(u = x/n \to 0\),
   and \(\lim_{u\to 0} \frac{\sin u}{u} = 1\). Therefore,
   \[
   \lim_{n\to\infty} f_n(x) = 1 \cdot \frac{1}{1 + x^2} = \frac{1}{1 + x^2} =: f(x) \quad \text{for all } x \in (0, \infty).
   \]
2. *Domination:* Recall the elementary inequality \(|\sin u| \le |u|\) for all \(u \in \mathbb R\), so
   \(\left|\frac{\sin(x/n)}{x/n}\right| \le 1\) for all \(x > 0\) and all \(n \ge 1\). Consequently,
   \[
   |f_n(x)| \le \frac{1}{1 + x^2} =: g(x) \quad \text{for all } x \in (0, \infty) \text{ and all } n \ge 1.
   \]
3. *Integrability of the majorant \(g\):* On \((0, \infty)\), \(g(x) = \frac{1}{1+x^2} \ge 0\) is
   integrable with respect to Lebesgue measure because
   \[
   \int_0^\infty \frac{1}{1 + x^2}\,dx = \lim_{R\to\infty} [\arctan x]_0^R = \frac{\pi}{2} < \infty.
   \]
4. *Conclusion via DCT:* By the Dominated Convergence Theorem (Theorem 6.15),
   \[
   \lim_{n\to\infty} \int_0^\infty \frac{n \sin(x/n)}{x (1 + x^2)}\,dx
   = \int_0^\infty \frac{1}{1 + x^2}\,dx = \frac{\pi}{2}.
   \]

** Exercise 4
Evaluate the limit
\[
\lim_{n\to\infty} \int_0^n \left(1 - \frac{x}{n}\right)^n e^{x/2}\,dx
\]
(with respect to Lebesgue measure on \((0, \infty)\)) and justify your steps.

** Solution
Write the integral over \((0, n)\) as an integral over the fixed domain \((0, \infty)\) by multiplying by
the indicator \(\mathbf 1_{(0, n)}(x)\):
\[
f_n(x) := \left(1 - \frac{x}{n}\right)^n e^{x/2} \mathbf 1_{(0, n)}(x) \qquad (x \in (0, \infty),\ n \ge 1).
\]
1. *Pointwise limit:* For each fixed \(x > 0\), once \(n > x\) we have \(\mathbf 1_{(0,n)}(x) = 1\), and
   from calculus \(\lim_{n\to\infty} (1 - x/n)^n = e^{-x}\). Thus
   \[
   \lim_{n\to\infty} f_n(x) = e^{-x} e^{x/2} = e^{-x/2} =: f(x) \quad \text{for all } x \in (0, \infty).
   \]
2. *Domination (or Monotone Convergence!):*
   - *Via DCT:* Using the standard inequality \(1 - u \le e^{-u}\) for all \(u \in \mathbb R\), on
     \(x \in (0, n)\) we have \(0 \le 1 - x/n \le e^{-x/n}\), so taking the \(n\)-th power gives
     \(0 \le (1 - x/n)^n \le e^{-x}\). Therefore, for all \(x > 0\) and all \(n \ge 1\),
     \[
     0 \le f_n(x) \le e^{-x} e^{x/2} = e^{-x/2} =: g(x).
     \]
     The dominating function \(g(x) = e^{-x/2}\) is integrable on \((0, \infty)\) since
     \(\int_0^\infty e^{-x/2}\,dx = [-2 e^{-x/2}]_0^\infty = 2 < \infty\).
   - *(Note: In fact \((1 - x/n)^n \mathbf 1_{(0,n)}(x)\) is also strictly increasing in \(n\), so MCT
     applies as well!)*
3. *Conclusion:* By DCT (Theorem 6.15),
   \[
   \lim_{n\to\infty} \int_0^n \left(1 - \frac{x}{n}\right)^n e^{x/2}\,dx
   = \int_0^\infty e^{-x/2}\,dx = 2.
   \]

** Exercise 5
Let \((\Omega, \mathcal F, \mu)\) be a measure space and suppose \(f : \Omega \to \mathbb R\) is
integrable (\(\int_\Omega |f|\,d\mu < \infty\)). Prove the **"absolute continuity of the Lebesgue
integral"**: for every \(\varepsilon > 0\), there exists \(\delta > 0\) such that
\[
A \in \mathcal F \text{ and } \mu(A) < \delta \implies \int_A |f|\,d\mu := \int_\Omega |f|\,\mathbf 1_A\,d\mu < \varepsilon.
\]
*(Hint: Use the bounded truncations \(f_n = \min\{|f|, n\}\) from Example 6.17!)*

** Solution
Fix \(\varepsilon > 0\). For each \(n \ge 1\), define the bounded truncation \(g_n := \min\{|f|, n\}\).
Then \(0 \le g_n \uparrow |f|\) pointwise (and \(|f| - g_n \to 0\) with \(0 \le |f| - g_n \le |f|\)). By
MCT or DCT (Example 6.17),
\[
\lim_{n\to\infty} \int_\Omega (|f| - g_n)\,d\mu = 0.
\]
Choose and fix a single integer \(N \ge 1\) large enough that
\[
\int_\Omega (|f| - g_N)\,d\mu < \frac{\varepsilon}{2}.
\]
Now notice that \(g_N \le N\) is bounded! Set \(\delta := \frac{\varepsilon}{2N} > 0\). Then for *any*
measurable set \(A \in \mathcal F\) with \(\mu(A) < \delta\), writing \(|f| = (|f| - g_N) + g_N\) on \(A\)
gives
\[
\int_\Omega |f|\,\mathbf 1_A\,d\mu
= \int_\Omega (|f| - g_N)\,\mathbf 1_A\,d\mu + \int_\Omega g_N\,\mathbf 1_A\,d\mu
\le \int_\Omega (|f| - g_N)\,d\mu + N\,\mu(A)
< \frac{\varepsilon}{2} + N \cdot \frac{\varepsilon}{2N} = \varepsilon.
\]

** Exercise 6
Let \(X\) be a random variable on \((\Omega, \mathcal F, \mathbb P)\) taking values in \(\mathbb R\), and
suppose its distribution \(X_\#\mathbb P\) is the discrete probability measure
\[
X_\#\mathbb P = \sum_{k=1}^\infty p_k \delta_{x_k},
\]
where \(x_1, x_2, \dots \in \mathbb R\) are distinct real numbers and \(p_k \ge 0\) satisfy
\(\sum_{k=1}^\infty p_k = 1\). Using Theorem 6.12 (image-measure integration), prove that for any Borel
function \(h : \mathbb R \to \mathbb R\), \(h(X)\) is integrable iff
\(\sum_{k=1}^\infty |h(x_k)|\,p_k < \infty\), and in that case
\[
\mathbb E\big[h(X)\big] = \sum_{k=1}^\infty h(x_k)\,p_k.
\]

** Solution
First consider any nonnegative Borel function \(g : \mathbb R \to [0, \infty]\). Notice that with respect
to \(\mu_X := X_\#\mathbb P = \sum_{k=1}^\infty p_k \delta_{x_k}\), the complement of
\(D := \{x_1, x_2, \dots\}\) has \(\mu_X(\mathbb R \setminus D) = 0\). On \(D\), we can write \(g\) as a
countable series of point indicators:
\[
g(x)\,\mathbf 1_D(x) = \sum_{k=1}^\infty g(x_k)\,\mathbf 1_{\{x_k\}}(x).
\]
Since \(g = g\,\mathbf 1_D\) \(\mu_X\)-almost everywhere, Lemma 6.8 and Proposition 6.6 (equation (6.3))
give
\[
\int_{\mathbb R} g\,d(X_\#\mathbb P)
= \int_{\mathbb R} \Big(\sum_{k=1}^\infty g(x_k)\,\mathbf 1_{\{x_k\}}\Big)\,d\mu_X
= \sum_{k=1}^\infty g(x_k)\,\mu_X(\{x_k\})
= \sum_{k=1}^\infty g(x_k)\,p_k.
\]
Now let \(h : \mathbb R \to \mathbb R\) be any real-valued Borel function. By Theorem 6.12, \(h(X)\) is
integrable with respect to \(\mathbb P\) iff \(h\) is integrable with respect to \(X_\#\mathbb P\), which
by applying the nonnegative formula to \(g = |h|\) means
\[
\mathbb E\big[|h(X)|\big] = \int_{\mathbb R} |h|\,d(X_\#\mathbb P) = \sum_{k=1}^\infty |h(x_k)|\,p_k < \infty.
\]
When this holds, applying the nonnegative formula to \(h^+\) and \(h^-\) and subtracting via Theorem 6.12
gives
\[
\mathbb E\big[h(X)\big] = \int_{\mathbb R} h\,d(X_\#\mathbb P)
= \sum_{k=1}^\infty h^+(x_k)\,p_k - \sum_{k=1}^\infty h^-(x_k)\,p_k
= \sum_{k=1}^\infty h(x_k)\,p_k.
\]

*Key takeaways*

- **The Standard Machine** proves identities for integrals in 4 steps: (1) indicators \(\mathbf 1_B\),
  (2) nonnegative simple functions \(s = \sum a_j \mathbf 1_{B_j}\) by linearity, (3) nonnegative
  measurable \(f \ge 0\) via \(s_n \uparrow f\) and MCT, and (4) signed integrable \(f = f^+ - f^-\) by
  subtracting.
- **Which limit theorem do I use?**
  - Nonnegative and increasing (\(0 \le f_n \uparrow f\)) or series of nonnegative terms (\(\sum f_n\) with
    \(f_n \ge 0\)) \(\implies\) **MCT (Theorem 6.5) / Proposition 6.6**.
  - Signed or oscillating \(f_n \to f\), trapped under a *single* integrable roof \(|f_n| \le g\) with
    \(\int g\,d\mu < \infty\) \(\implies\) **DCT (Theorem 6.15)**.
  - Nonnegative \(f_n \ge 0\) with neither monotonicity nor an integrable majorant, and you only need an
    inequality \(\int (\liminf f_n)\,d\mu \le \liminf \int f_n\,d\mu\) \(\implies\) **Fatou's lemma
    (Theorem 6.13)**.
- "Integrable" in measure theory *always* means **absolutely integrable** (\(\int_\Omega |f|\,d\mu < \infty\)).
  Changing a measurable function on a measurable set of measure zero changes neither its integrability nor
  its integral.
