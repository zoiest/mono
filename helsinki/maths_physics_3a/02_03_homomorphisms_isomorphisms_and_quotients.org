* Group Homomorphisms and Isomorphisms

** Group Homomorphisms
Let $(G, \cdot)$ and $(H, \bullet)$ be two groups. A mapping $\phi : G \to H$ is called a *group homomorphism* if it preserves the group operation:
$$ \forall g_1, g_2 \in G, \quad \phi(g_1 \cdot g_2) = \phi(g_1) \bullet \phi(g_2) $$

*** Elementary Properties
1. *Preservation of Identity:*
   $$ \phi(e_G) = e_H $$
   *Proof:* $\phi(e_G) = \phi(e_G \cdot e_G) = \phi(e_G) \bullet \phi(e_G)$. Multiplying on the right by $\phi(e_G)^{-1}$ in $H$ gives $e_H = \phi(e_G)$. $\blacksquare$
2. *Preservation of Inverses:*
   $$ \phi(g^{-1}) = (\phi(g))^{-1} $$
   *Proof:* $\phi(g) \bullet \phi(g^{-1}) = \phi(g \cdot g^{-1}) = \phi(e_G) = e_H$. Since inverses in $H$ are unique, $\phi(g^{-1}) = (\phi(g))^{-1}$. $\blacksquare$
3. *Preservation of Powers:*
   $$ \phi(g^n) = (\phi(g))^n, \quad \forall n \in \mathbb{Z} $$

** Group Isomorphisms
A group homomorphism $\phi : G \to H$ is a *group isomorphism* if it is a bijection (both injective and surjective).
If an isomorphism exists between $G$ and $H$, the groups are called *isomorphic*, denoted:
$$ G \cong H $$

*** Isomorphism as an Equivalence Relation
The relation of isomorphism is an equivalence relation on any family of groups:
- *Reflexive:* $G \cong G$ via the identity automorphism $\mathrm{id}_G(g) = g$.
- *Symmetric:* If $\phi : G \to H$ is an isomorphism, the set-theoretic inverse $\phi^{-1} : H \to G$ is also a group isomorphism:
  $$ \phi^{-1}(h_1 \bullet h_2) = \phi^{-1}(\phi(g_1) \bullet \phi(g_2)) = \phi^{-1}(\phi(g_1 \cdot g_2)) = g_1 \cdot g_2 = \phi^{-1}(h_1) \cdot \phi^{-1}(h_2) $$
- *Transitive:* If $\phi : G \to H$ and $\psi : H \to K$ are isomorphisms, then the composition $\psi \circ \phi : G \to K$ is an isomorphism.

*Consequence:* An abstract group is an equivalence class of groups under the isomorphism relation.

*** Example: Logarithm Map
Let $G = (\mathbb{R}^+, \cdot)$ (positive real numbers under multiplication) and $H = (\mathbb{R}, +)$ (real numbers under addition).
Define $f : \mathbb{R}^+ \to \mathbb{R}$ by $f(x) = \ln x$:
$$ f(xy) = \ln(xy) = \ln x + \ln y = f(x) + f(y) $$
$f$ is bijective, and its inverse is $f^{-1}(y) = e^y$. Thus $(\mathbb{R}^+, \cdot) \cong (\mathbb{R}, +)$.

** Automorphisms
An isomorphism from a group $G$ to itself is called an *automorphism*.
The set of all automorphisms of $G$ forms a group under function composition, denoted $\mathrm{Aut}(G)$.

* Kernel and Image

Let $\phi : G \to H$ be a group homomorphism.

** The Kernel
The *kernel* of $\phi$ is the preimage of the identity in $H$:
$$ \mathrm{Ker}(\phi) = \{ g \in G \mid \phi(g) = e_H \} $$
- $\mathrm{Ker}(\phi)$ is a subgroup of $G$:
  - $e_G \in \mathrm{Ker}(\phi)$ since $\phi(e_G) = e_H$.
  - If $k_1, k_2 \in \mathrm{Ker}(\phi)$, $\phi(k_1 k_2^{-1}) = \phi(k_1) \phi(k_2)^{-1} = e_H e_H^{-1} = e_H \implies k_1 k_2^{-1} \in \mathrm{Ker}(\phi)$.

** The Image
The *image* of $\phi$ is the range of $\phi$ in $H$:
$$ \mathrm{Im}(\phi) = \phi(G) = \{ h \in H \mid \exists g \in G, \, h = \phi(g) \} $$
- $\mathrm{Im}(\phi)$ is a subgroup of $H$:
  - $e_H = \phi(e_G) \in \mathrm{Im}(\phi)$.
  - If $h_1 = \phi(g_1)$ and $h_2 = \phi(g_2)$, then $h_1 h_2^{-1} = \phi(g_1) \phi(g_2^{-1}) = \phi(g_1 g_2^{-1}) \in \mathrm{Im}(\phi)$.

** Injectivity Criterion
#+begin_quote
*Proposition:* A group homomorphism $\phi : G \to H$ is injective if and only if $\mathrm{Ker}(\phi) = \{e_G\}$.
#+end_quote
*Proof:*
- $(\implies)$ If $\phi$ is injective, since $\phi(e_G) = e_H$, no other element can map to $e_H$. Thus $\mathrm{Ker}(\phi) = \{e_G\}$.
- $(\impliedby)$ Suppose $\mathrm{Ker}(\phi) = \{e_G\}$. If $\phi(g_1) = \phi(g_2)$, then:
  $$ \phi(g_1)\phi(g_2)^{-1} = e_H \implies \phi(g_1 g_2^{-1}) = e_H \implies g_1 g_2^{-1} \in \mathrm{Ker}(\phi) = \{e_G\} $$
  Hence $g_1 g_2^{-1} = e_G \implies g_1 = g_2$. Thus $\phi$ is injective. $\blacksquare$

* Normal Subgroups and Conjugacy

** Normal (Invariant) Subgroups
A subgroup $N \le G$ is called a *normal subgroup* (or *invariant subgroup*), denoted $N \triangleleft G$, if it is invariant under conjugation by all elements of $G$:
$$ \forall g \in G, \forall n \in N, \quad g n g^{-1} \in N $$
Equivalently:
$$ g N g^{-1} = N \quad \iff \quad gN = Ng, \quad \forall g \in G $$
(Every left coset of $N$ equals its right coset.)

** The Fundamental Link: Kernels are Normal Subgroups
#+begin_quote
*Theorem:* The kernel of every group homomorphism $\phi : G \to H$ is a normal subgroup of $G$.
#+end_quote
*Proof:*
Let $k \in \mathrm{Ker}(\phi)$ and $g \in G$. Consider the conjugate element $g k g^{-1}$:
$$ \phi(g k g^{-1}) = \phi(g) \phi(k) \phi(g^{-1}) = \phi(g) e_H \phi(g)^{-1} = \phi(g) \phi(g)^{-1} = e_H $$
Therefore, $g k g^{-1} \in \mathrm{Ker}(\phi)$. Thus $\mathrm{Ker}(\phi) \triangleleft G$. $\blacksquare$

** Simple and Semisimple Groups
- *Simple Group:* A group $G$ is *simple* if it has no proper normal subgroups (its only normal subgroups are $\{e\}$ and $G$ itself).
- *Semisimple Group:* A group $G$ is *semisimple* if it contains no non-trivial, proper normal *Abelian* subgroups.
- Every simple group is semisimple, but not vice versa (e.g., $\mathrm{SO}(4)$ is semisimple but not simple).

* Quotient Groups (Factor Groups)

Let $N \triangleleft G$ be a normal subgroup.
The coset space $G/N = \{ gN \mid g \in G \}$ can be endowed with a natural binary operation:
$$ (g_1 N) \cdot (g_2 N) \equiv (g_1 g_2) N $$

** Well-Definedness of Coset Multiplication
#+begin_quote
*Theorem:* The coset multiplication $(g_1 N)(g_2 N) = (g_1 g_2)N$ is well-defined if and only if $N \triangleleft G$.
#+end_quote
*Proof:*
Let $g_1' \in g_1 N$ and $g_2' \in g_2 N$, so $g_1' = g_1 n_1$ and $g_2' = g_2 n_2$ for some $n_1, n_2 \in N$.
We must verify that $(g_1' g_2')N = (g_1 g_2)N$:
$$ g_1' g_2' = g_1 n_1 g_2 n_2 = g_1 g_2 (g_2^{-1} n_1 g_2) n_2 $$
Since $N \triangleleft G$, the conjugate $n_1' = g_2^{-1} n_1 g_2 \in N$.
Hence $g_1' g_2' = (g_1 g_2) (n_1' n_2)$. Since $N$ is a subgroup, $n_3 = n_1' n_2 \in N$.
Thus $g_1' g_2' \in (g_1 g_2)N$, which proves $(g_1' g_2')N = (g_1 g_2)N$. $\blacksquare$

** Group Properties of $G/N$
Under this multiplication, $G/N$ forms a group, called the *quotient group* (or *factor group*):
1. *Associativity:* Inherited from $G$: $((g_1 N)(g_2 N))(g_3 N) = (g_1 g_2 g_3)N = (g_1 N)((g_2 N)(g_3 N))$.
2. *Identity:* The identity element is the coset $eN = N$.
3. *Inverses:* $(gN)^{-1} = g^{-1}N$.
4. *Order:* If $G$ is finite, $|G/N| = [G:N] = |G|/|N|$.

* The First Isomorphism Theorem

#+begin_quote
*Theorem 2.6 (First Isomorphism Theorem / Fundamental Homomorphism Theorem):*
Let $\phi : G \to H$ be a group homomorphism. Then the quotient group $G / \mathrm{Ker}(\phi)$ is isomorphic to the image $\mathrm{Im}(\phi)$:
$$ G / \mathrm{Ker}(\phi) \cong \mathrm{Im}(\phi) $$
#+end_quote
*Proof:*
Let $K = \mathrm{Ker}(\phi)$. Define the map $\Phi : G/K \to \mathrm{Im}(\phi)$ by:
$$ \Phi(gK) = \phi(g) $$
1. *Well-defined:* If $g_1 K = g_2 K$, then $g_1^{-1} g_2 \in K \implies \phi(g_1^{-1} g_2) = e_H \implies \phi(g_1)^{-1}\phi(g_2) = e_H \implies \phi(g_1) = \phi(g_2)$. Thus $\Phi(g_1 K) = \Phi(g_2 K)$.
2. *Homomorphism:*
   $$ \Phi((g_1 K)(g_2 K)) = \Phi((g_1 g_2)K) = \phi(g_1 g_2) = \phi(g_1) \phi(g_2) = \Phi(g_1 K) \Phi(g_2 K) $$
3. *Injective:*
   $$ \Phi(gK) = e_H \implies \phi(g) = e_H \implies g \in K \implies gK = K = e_{G/K} $$
   By the injectivity criterion, $\mathrm{Ker}(\Phi) = \{K\}$, so $\Phi$ is injective.
4. *Surjective:* For any $h \in \mathrm{Im}(\phi)$, there exists $g \in G$ such that $h = \phi(g)$. Then $\Phi(gK) = \phi(g) = h$.
Being a bijective homomorphism, $\Phi$ is an isomorphism: $G / K \cong \mathrm{Im}(\phi)$. $\blacksquare$

*Physical Application:*
This theorem is central to physics:
- $\mathrm{SU}(2) / \mathbb{Z}_2 \cong \mathrm{SO}(3)$ (Spin-1/2 double cover of spatial rotations, see [[id:0c71e7cb-1a90-4ce5-bc72-38c37afda295][Groups Acting on a Set]]).
- $\mathrm{SL}(2, \mathbb{C}) / \mathbb{Z}_2 \cong \mathrm{SO}^+(1, 3)$ (Universal cover of the restricted Lorentz group).
