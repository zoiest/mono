Week 03 introduces several interlocking concepts that often cause confusion. Keep this master conceptual map in mind:

#+caption: The Master Conceptual Map
| Concept | Mathematical Symbol | Where it Lives | Physical / Geometric Meaning |
|---|---|---|---|
| *The Symmetry Group* | $G$ | Group of operators | All transformations leaving physics invariant |
| *The State / Config Space* | $X$ | Set or manifold | The geometric space of points, fields, or states |
| *Group Action* | $g \cdot x$ | Map $G \times X \to X$ | Applying symmetry operation $g$ to state $x$ |
| *Orbit* | $\mathcal{O}_x = G \cdot x$ | *Subset of $X$* | The trajectory of state $x$ under all symmetries |
| *Stabilizer (Isotropy Group)* | $G_x = \mathrm{Stab}_G(x)$ | *Subgroup of $G$* | The symmetries that leave state $x$ completely unchanged |
| *Coset* | $g H$ | *Subset of $G$* | A shifted copy of subgroup $H$ inside $G$ |
| *Coset Space (Homogeneous Space)* | $G/H$ | Set or manifold | Space of orbits $\mathcal{O}_x \cong G/G_x$ (points on a sphere, spacetime) |
| *Quotient Group* | $G/N$ ($N \triangleleft G$) | *A brand new group* | Symmetries of the coarse-grained system |

#+begin_quote
*The Golden Rule of Week 03:*
- An *orbit* $\mathcal{O}_x$ is a subset of the *space $X$* (where a point can travel).
- A *stabilizer* $G_x$ is a subgroup of the *group $G$* (symmetries that don't move the point).
- The *Orbit-Stabilizer Theorem* bridges them:
  $$ (\text{Size of Orbit in } X) \times (\text{Size of Stabilizer in } G) = (\text{Total Symmetries in } G) $$
  $$ |\mathcal{O}_x| \cdot |G_x| = |G| $$
#+end_quote

* Equivalence Relations, Partitions, and Quotient Spaces (§2.9)

** Equivalence Relations
A binary relation $\sim$ on a set $X$ is an *equivalence relation* if it satisfies three axioms for all $x, y, z \in X$:
1. *Reflexivity:* $x \sim x$ (every state is equivalent to itself).
2. *Symmetry:* $x \sim y \implies y \sim x$ (if $x$ is equivalent to $y$, then $y$ is equivalent to $x$).
3. *Transitivity:* $x \sim y \text{ and } y \sim z \implies x \sim z$ (equivalence chains together).

** Partitions and Quotient Spaces
- *Equivalence Class:* The collection of all elements equivalent to $x$:
  $$ [x] = \{y \in X \mid y \sim x\} $$
- *Fundamental Partition Theorem:*
  Every equivalence relation partitions $X$ into disjoint, non-empty equivalence classes that cover $X$:
  $$ X = \bigsqcup_{i} [x_i] $$
- *Quotient Space:* The set of equivalence classes:
  $$ X / \sim \; = \{[x] \mid x \in X\} $$

*** Worked Example: Clocks and Modular Arithmetic
Let $X = \mathbb{Z}$ (the integers). Define $a \sim b \iff a - b$ is a multiple of $12$ ($a \equiv b \pmod{12}$).
- This is an equivalence relation:
  - $a - a = 0 = 0 \times 12$ (reflexive).
  - $a - b = 12k \implies b - a = 12(-k)$ (symmetric).
  - $a - b = 12k$ and $b - c = 12m \implies a - c = 12(k+m)$ (transitive).
- Equivalence classes: $[0] = \{\dots, -12, 0, 12, 24, \dots\}$, $[1] = \{\dots, -11, 1, 13, 25, \dots\}$, up to $[11]$.
- Quotient space: $\mathbb{Z} / 12\mathbb{Z} = \{[0], [1], \dots, [11]\}$ (the 12 hours on a clock face).

* Group Actions: Translations, Conjugation, and Orbits (§2.9)

Let $G$ be a group acting on a set $X$ via a left action $\cdot : G \times X \to X$ satisfying:
1. $e \cdot x = x$ (identity does nothing).
2. $g_1 \cdot (g_2 \cdot x) = (g_1 g_2) \cdot x$ (successive operations combine according to group multiplication).

** Canonical Actions of $G$ on Itself ($X = G$)
1. *Left Translation:* $L_g(x) = g x$. Shifts elements from the left.
2. *Right Translation:* $R_g(x) = x g^{-1}$. Shifts elements from the right.
3. *Conjugation (Inner Automorphism):*
   $$ C_g(x) = g x g^{-1} $$
   *Physical Meaning:* Conjugation represents a *change of coordinate frame* or basis. If $x$ is a physical operation in frame 1, and $g$ translates from frame 1 to frame 2, then $g x g^{-1}$ is the exact same operation viewed from frame 2.

** Orbits and the Orbit Space $X/G$
- *Orbit of a point $x \in X$:* The set of all destinations reachable from $x$ by applying group elements:
  $$ \mathcal{O}_x = G \cdot x = \{g \cdot x \mid g \in G\} \subseteq X $$
- *Orbit Partition:* Two points are in the same orbit if and only if a group element connects them ($x \sim y \iff \exists g, \, y = g \cdot x$).
  Because this is an equivalence relation, the orbits partition the state space $X$:
  $$ X = \bigsqcup \mathcal{O}_x $$
- *Orbit Space:* $X/G = \{\mathcal{O}_x \mid x \in X\}$.

** Transitive Actions and Homogeneous Spaces
- An action is *transitive* if there is only a single orbit: any point can be mapped to any other point.
- *Homogeneous Space:* A space where a group acts transitively. Every point looks identical.

*** Worked Example: The Rotation Group $\mathrm{SO}(3)$ Acting on $\mathbb{R}^3$
Let $G = \mathrm{SO}(3)$ (3D spatial rotations) act on $X = \mathbb{R}^3$:
1. *Orbit of the Origin $\vec{0} = (0, 0, 0)$:*
   Every rotation leaves $\vec{0}$ fixed: $R \vec{0} = \vec{0}$.
   $$ \mathcal{O}_{\vec{0}} = \{\vec{0}\} \quad (\text{a single point, 0-dimensional}) $$
2. *Orbit of a Point at Radius $R > 0$ (e.g. $\vec{x}_0 = (0, 0, R)$):*
   Any vector at distance $R$ from the origin can be reached by rotating $\vec{x}_0$.
   $$ \mathcal{O}_{\vec{x}_0} = \{\vec{x} \in \mathbb{R}^3 \mid \|\vec{x}\| = R\} = S^2(R) \quad (\text{a 2D sphere!}) $$
3. *Orbit Space $\mathbb{R}^3 / \mathrm{SO}(3)$:*
   Each orbit is a concentric sphere of radius $r \ge 0$. The orbit space is parameterized by the radius:
   $$ \mathbb{R}^3 / \mathrm{SO}(3) \cong [0, \infty) \quad (\text{a 1D half-line}) $$
4. *Homogeneous Space:*
   Restricted to the sphere $S^2$, $\mathrm{SO}(3)$ acts *transitively*: any point on the sphere can be rotated to any other point on the sphere. Thus $S^2$ is a homogeneous space!

* Conjugacy Classes and the Class Equation (§2.9)

** Conjugacy Classes
When $G$ acts on itself by conjugation $C_g(x) = g x g^{-1}$, the orbits in $G$ are called *conjugacy classes*:
$$ \mathrm{Cl}(x) = \{g x g^{-1} \mid g \in G\} $$
- In matrix groups: Conjugate matrices $B = P A P^{-1}$ represent the *same linear operator in different bases*. They share the same eigenvalues, determinant, and trace!
- In symmetric groups $S_n$: Conjugate permutations have the *same cycle structure*.

** The Class Equation
Since conjugacy classes partition $G$:
$$ |G| = |Z(G)| + \sum_{i} |\mathrm{Cl}(x_i)| $$
where $Z(G)$ is the center (elements that commute with everything, so $\mathrm{Cl}(z) = \{z\}$).

*** Worked Example: Conjugacy Classes of $S_3 \cong D_3$
The symmetric group $S_3$ has $|S_3| = 6$ elements. Let us partition them into conjugacy classes by cycle structure:
1. *Identity (1-cycles, partition $1+1+1$):*
   $\mathrm{Cl}(e) = \{e\}$ (size 1). Identity is always in the center $Z(S_3) = \{e\}$.
2. *Transpositions / Reflections (2-cycles, partition $2+1$):*
   $\mathrm{Cl}((12)) = \{(12), (23), (13)\}$ (size 3).
   Verify by conjugation: $(123)(12)(123)^{-1} = (123)(12)(132) = (23)$.
3. *3-Cycles / Rotations (3-cycles, partition $3$):*
   $\mathrm{Cl}((123)) = \{(123), (132)\}$ (size 2).
   Verify: $(12)(123)(12)^{-1} = (12)(123)(12) = (132)$.
- *Class Equation Check:*
  $$ |S_3| = 6 = 1 + 3 + 2 \quad \checkmark $$
  Notice that the size of every conjugacy class ($1, 3, 2$) divides the group order $6$ (a consequence of the Orbit-Stabilizer Theorem!).

* Cosets, Quotient Spaces $G/H$, and Lagrange's Theorem (§2.2)

** Left and Right Cosets
Let $H \le G$ be a subgroup.
- *Left Coset containing $g$:* $g H = \{g h \mid h \in H\}$.
- *Right Coset containing $g$:* $H g = \{h g \mid h \in H\}$.
- *Crucial Properties:*
  1. $e \in H \implies g \in gH$.
  2. Every coset has the exact same number of elements as $H$: $|gH| = |H|$.
  3. Two left cosets are either *completely identical* or *completely disjoint*:
     $$ g_1 H = g_2 H \iff g_1^{-1} g_2 \in H $$
  4. The left cosets partition the group $G$:
     $$ G = \bigsqcup_{i=1}^k g_i H $$

** Lagrange's Theorem
#+begin_quote
*Theorem (Lagrange):*
If $G$ is a finite group and $H$ is a subgroup of $G$, the order of $H$ divides the order of $G$:
$$ |G| = [G : H] \cdot |H| $$
where $[G : H] = |G/H|$ is the *index* (number of distinct cosets).
#+end_quote

*** Immediate Physical and Algebraic Consequences:
1. *Element Order Divisibility:* The order of any element $\mathrm{ord}(g)$ divides $|G|$ (since $\mathrm{ord}(g) = |\langle g \rangle|$).
2. *Groups of Prime Order are Cyclic:* If $|G| = p$ (e.g. 2, 3, 5, 7), $G$ has *no* non-trivial subgroups at all, and must be cyclic: $G \cong \mathbb{Z}_p$.

*** Worked Example: Cosets of a Subgroup in $S_3$
Let $G = S_3 = \{e, (12), (13), (23), (123), (132)\}$ and choose the subgroup $H = \{e, (12)\}$ (order 2).
Since $|G| = 6$ and $|H| = 2$, by Lagrange's Theorem there are $[G:H] = 6/2 = 3$ cosets:
1. *Coset 1 (Identity coset):*
   $$ e H = \{e, (12)\} = H $$
2. *Coset 2 (Pick an element not in $H$, e.g. $(123)$):*
   $$ (123) H = \{(123)e, \, (123)(12)\} = \{(123), \, (13)\} $$
3. *Coset 3 (Pick an element not in Cosets 1 or 2, e.g. $(132)$):*
   $$ (132) H = \{(132)e, \, (132)(12)\} = \{(132), \, (23)\} $$
- *Partition Check:*
  $$ G = \{e, (12)\} \cup \{(123), (13)\} \cup \{(132), (23)\} $$
  All 6 elements appear exactly once across the 3 cosets of size 2.

* Isotropy Groups and the Orbit-Stabilizer Theorem (§2.9)

** The Isotropy Group (Stabilizer / Little Group)
For any point $x \in X$, the *stabilizer* $G_x$ is the collection of all symmetry operations that leave $x$ where it is:
$$ G_x \equiv \mathrm{Stab}_G(x) = \{g \in G \mid g \cdot x = x\} $$
- *Theorem:* $G_x$ is always a subgroup of $G$ ($G_x \le G$).
  - Identity: $e \cdot x = x \implies e \in G_x$.
  - Closure: $g, h \in G_x \implies (gh) \cdot x = g \cdot (h \cdot x) = g \cdot x = x \implies gh \in G_x$.
  - Inverses: $g \in G_x \implies g \cdot x = x \implies g^{-1} \cdot x = x \implies g^{-1} \in G_x$.

** The Orbit-Stabilizer Theorem
#+begin_quote
*Theorem (Orbit-Stabilizer Theorem):*
Let $G$ act on $X$. For any point $x \in X$, there is a natural bijection between the orbit $\mathcal{O}_x$ and the set of left cosets $G / G_x$:
$$ \mathcal{O}_x \cong G / G_x $$
For a finite group $G$:
$$ |\mathcal{O}_x| = [G : G_x] = \frac{|G|}{|G_x|} \iff |\mathcal{O}_x| \cdot |G_x| = |G| $$
#+end_quote

*** Why is this true intuitively?
Suppose $g_1$ and $g_2$ map $x$ to the exact same destination: $g_1 \cdot x = g_2 \cdot x$.
Then $g_2^{-1} g_1 \cdot x = x$, meaning $g_2^{-1} g_1 \in G_x \implies g_1 G_x = g_2 G_x$.
*All group elements that send $x$ to the same place belong to the exact same coset of $G_x$!*
Therefore, destinations (points in the orbit) correspond one-to-one with cosets!

*** Worked Example: Discrete Orbit-Stabilizer on a Square ($D_4$)
Let $G = D_4$ (the symmetries of a square, $|D_4| = 8$: 4 rotations, 4 reflections).
Let $X = \{1, 2, 3, 4\}$ be the set of the 4 vertices of the square.
Pick vertex $1$:
1. *Where can vertex 1 go? (Orbit $\mathcal{O}_1$):*
   By rotations and reflections, vertex 1 can be moved to vertex 1, 2, 3, or 4.
   $$ \mathcal{O}_1 = \{1, 2, 3, 4\} \implies |\mathcal{O}_1| = 4 $$
2. *Which symmetries leave vertex 1 fixed? (Stabilizer $G_1$):*
   - Identity $e$: fixes 1.
   - Rotations by $90^\circ, 180^\circ, 270^\circ$: move 1 to 2, 3, 4 (none fix 1).
   - Horizontal and vertical edge reflections: move 1 to 4 and 2.
   - Diagonal reflection passing through vertex 1 and 3 ($\sigma_{\mathrm{diag}}$): fixes 1 (and 3), swapping 2 and 4.
   - Other diagonal reflection: moves 1 to 3.
   Thus:
   $$ G_1 = \{e, \sigma_{\mathrm{diag}}\} \implies |G_1| = 2 $$
3. *Orbit-Stabilizer Verification:*
   $$ |\mathcal{O}_1| \times |G_1| = 4 \times 2 = 8 = |D_4| \quad \checkmark $$

*** Worked Example: Continuous Orbit-Stabilizer on the 2-Sphere ($S^2$)
Let $G = \mathrm{SO}(3)$ (continuous rotations in 3D, dimension 3) act on $X = S^2$ (the 2-sphere).
Pick the North Pole $\vec{n} = (0, 0, 1)$:
1. *Orbit:* Rotations can move the North Pole to any point on the sphere:
   $$ \mathcal{O}_{\vec{n}} = S^2 \quad (\text{dimension } 2) $$
2. *Stabilizer:* What rotations fix the North Pole?
   Any rotation about the $z$-axis by angle $\theta \in [0, 2\pi)$ fixes $(0, 0, 1)$:
   $$ G_{\vec{n}} = \left\{ \begin{pmatrix} \cos\theta & -\sin\theta & 0 \\ \sin\theta & \cos\theta & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix} \right\} \cong \mathrm{SO}(2) \quad (\text{dimension } 1) $$
3. *Coset Manifold Identification:*
   $$ S^2 \cong \mathrm{SO}(3) / \mathrm{SO}(2) $$
   Dimension check: $\dim S^2 = \dim \mathrm{SO}(3) - \dim \mathrm{SO}(2) = 3 - 1 = 2$.
   *The 2-sphere is literally the quotient space of 3D rotations by 2D axial rotations!*

* Fixed Points and Burnside's Lemma (§2.9)

** Fixed-Point Sets (Invariant Sets)
For a group element $g \in G$, the *fixed-point set* is the collection of states in $X$ left completely unchanged by $g$:
$$ X^g = \{x \in X \mid g \cdot x = x\} $$

** Burnside's Lemma (Cauchy-Frobenius Theorem)
#+begin_quote
*Theorem (Burnside's Lemma):*
The number of distinct physical orbits $|X/G|$ (configurations inequivalent under symmetry) is the average number of points fixed by the group elements:
$$ |X/G| = \frac{1}{|G|} \sum_{g \in G} |X^g| $$
#+end_quote

*** Worked Example: Counting Physically Distinct 3-Bead Necklaces
Suppose we have a necklace of 3 beads arranged in a circle. Each bead can be colored either *Red* or *Blue* ($2$ colors).
How many physically distinct necklaces can be made under rotational symmetry?
1. *State Space $X$:*
   Before considering symmetry, each of the 3 beads has 2 color choices:
   $$ |X| = 2^3 = 8 \text{ possible colorings} $$
   Represented by tuples: $(R, R, R), (R, R, B), (R, B, R), (B, R, R), (R, B, B), (B, R, B), (B, B, R), (B, B, B)$.
2. *Symmetry Group $G$:*
   The rotational symmetry of the 3-bead circle is the cyclic group $C_3 = \{R_0, R_{120}, R_{240}\}$ ($|G| = 3$).
3. *Count Fixed Points for Each Group Element:*
   - *Identity $R_0$ (0° rotation):* Leaves all 8 colorings unchanged:
     $$ |X^{R_0}| = 8 $$
   - *Rotation $R_{120}$ (120° rotation):* For a coloring to remain unchanged when rotated by $120^\circ$, bead 1 must match bead 2, bead 2 must match bead 3, and bead 3 must match bead 1. All 3 beads must have the exact same color!
     Only two states qualify: $(R, R, R)$ and $(B, B, B)$.
     $$ |X^{R_{120}}| = 2 $$
   - *Rotation $R_{240}$ (240° rotation):* Similarly, all 3 beads must be the same color:
     $$ |X^{R_{240}}| = 2 $$
4. *Apply Burnside's Lemma:*
   $$ |X/G| = \frac{1}{3} \left( |X^{R_0}| + |X^{R_{120}}| + |X^{R_{240}}| \right) = \frac{1}{3} (8 + 2 + 2) = \frac{12}{3} = 4 $$
*Conclusion:* There are exactly *4 physically distinct necklaces*:
1. All Red: $(R, R, R)$
2. All Blue: $(B, B, B)$
3. Two Red, One Blue: $(R, R, B) \sim (R, B, R) \sim (B, R, R)$ (orbit of size 3)
4. One Red, Two Blue: $(R, B, B) \sim (B, R, B) \sim (B, B, R)$ (orbit of size 3)
Total configurations: $1 + 1 + 3 + 3 = 8$. Orbits: $4$. Burnside gives 4 immediately!

* Normal Subgroups vs. General Subgroups: Why Normality Matters (§2.3)

One of the most common points of confusion is: *Why can't we always turn $G/H$ into a group?*

** The Problem with General Subgroups
Let $H \le G$. We want to multiply cosets by multiplying their representatives:
$$ (g_1 H) \cdot (g_2 H) \stackrel{?}{=} (g_1 g_2) H $$
Is this well-defined?
Choose different representatives $g_1 h_1 \in g_1 H$ and $g_2 h_2 \in g_2 H$.
Their product is:
$$ (g_1 h_1)(g_2 h_2) = g_1 (h_1 g_2) h_2 = g_1 g_2 (g_2^{-1} h_1 g_2) h_2 $$
For this to belong to $(g_1 g_2) H$, the term $g_2^{-1} h_1 g_2$ *must belong to $H$*!

** Normal Subgroups ($N \triangleleft G$)
This requirement is precisely the definition of a *normal subgroup*:
$$ g N g^{-1} = N, \quad \forall g \in G \iff g N = N g, \quad \forall g \in G $$
#+begin_quote
*Summary Rule:*
- If $H$ is *not normal*, left cosets $gH \neq Hg$ right cosets. The coset space $G/H$ is a *geometric set / manifold*, but *not a group*.
- If $N$ is *normal* ($N \triangleleft G$), left cosets equal right cosets. The quotient space $G/N$ is a *brand new group* (the Quotient Group)!
#+end_quote

*** Worked Example: A Non-Normal Subgroup vs. A Normal Subgroup in $S_3$
Let $G = S_3$.
1. *Non-Normal Subgroup $H = \{e, (12)\}$:*
   Take $g = (123)$.
   - Left coset: $(123)H = \{(123), (13)\}$.
   - Right coset: $H(123) = \{(123), (23)\}$.
   Since $(123)H \neq H(123)$, $H$ is *not normal*.
   Coset multiplication fails:
   $[(123)H] \cdot [(123)H]$:
   Using representatives $(123)$ and $(123)$: $(123)(123) = (132) \in (132)H$.
   Using representatives $(13)$ and $(13)$: $(13)(13) = e \in eH$.
   Different representatives yield different cosets! Thus $S_3 / H$ is *not a group*.
2. *Normal Subgroup $N = A_3 = \{e, (123), (132)\}$ (Alternating Group):*
   $A_3$ has index $[S_3 : A_3] = 6/3 = 2$.
   Any subgroup of index 2 is *always normal*!
   There are only two cosets:
   - $N$ (the even permutations).
   - $\tau N = \{(12), (13), (23)\}$ (the odd permutations).
   Left cosets equal right cosets: $\tau N = N \tau$.
   Multiplication table of cosets:
   - $N \cdot N = N$ (Even $\times$ Even = Even).
   - $N \cdot (\tau N) = \tau N$ (Even $\times$ Odd = Odd).
   - $(\tau N) \cdot (\tau N) = N$ (Odd $\times$ Odd = Even).
   The quotient $S_3 / A_3$ is a valid 2-element group isomorphic to $\mathbb{Z}_2$:
   $$ S_3 / A_3 \cong \mathbb{Z}_2 \quad \checkmark $$

* The Center of a Group ($Z(G)$) and Physical Spin (§2.2, §2.9)

The *center* $Z(G)$ is the set of elements commuting with everything:
$$ Z(G) = \{z \in G \mid z g = g z, \, \forall g \in G\} $$
- The center is *always an Abelian normal subgroup*: $Z(G) \triangleleft G$.
- Therefore, the quotient group $G / Z(G)$ is *always a valid group* (called the *inner automorphism group* $\mathrm{Inn}(G)$).

*** Worked Example: The Double Cover $\mathrm{SU}(2) \to \mathrm{SO}(3)$
In quantum physics, spin-$1/2$ states live in the group $\mathrm{SU}(2)$ ($2 \times 2$ unitary matrices with determinant 1):
$$ \mathrm{SU}(2) = \left\{ \begin{pmatrix} \alpha & \beta \\ -\beta^* & \alpha^* \end{pmatrix} \;\middle|\; |\alpha|^2 + |\beta|^2 = 1 \right\} $$
1. *Center of $\mathrm{SU}(2)$:*
   What matrices commute with all Pauli matrices? Only scalar multiples of the identity:
   $z = c \mathbf{1}_2$. Det condition $\det(c\mathbf{1}_2) = c^2 = 1 \implies c = \pm 1$.
   $$ Z(\mathrm{SU}(2)) = \{\mathbf{1}_2, -\mathbf{1}_2\} \cong \mathbb{Z}_2 $$
2. *Physical Meaning of $-\mathbf{1}_2$:*
   A $360^\circ$ ($2\pi$) rotation of a fermion produces $-\mathbf{1}_2$, negating the wavefunction ($|\psi\rangle \to -|\psi\rangle$). It takes a $720^\circ$ ($4\pi$) rotation to return to $+\mathbf{1}_2$.
3. *Quotient Group:*
   In classical 3D space, a $360^\circ$ rotation is identical to no rotation at all. Thus classical spatial rotations $\mathrm{SO}(3)$ correspond to $\mathrm{SU}(2)$ with the signs identified:
   $$ \mathrm{SU}(2) / \{\mathbf{1}_2, -\mathbf{1}_2\} \cong \mathrm{SO}(3) $$
   *$\mathrm{SU}(2)$ is the universal double cover of the 3D rotation group $\mathrm{SO}(3)$!*

* Homomorphisms and the First Isomorphism Theorem (§2.3)

** Kernel, Image, and Normality
Let $\phi : G \to H$ be a homomorphism ($\phi(a b) = \phi(a) \phi(b)$).
- *Kernel:* $\mathrm{Ker}(\phi) = \{g \in G \mid \phi(g) = e_H\}$.
  *Crucial Fact:* $\mathrm{Ker}(\phi)$ is *always a normal subgroup* of $G$ ($\mathrm{Ker}(\phi) \triangleleft G$).
- *Image:* $\mathrm{Im}(\phi) = \{\phi(g) \mid g \in G\} \le H$ is a subgroup of $H$.

** The First Isomorphism Theorem
#+begin_quote
*Theorem (First Isomorphism Theorem):*
$$ G / \mathrm{Ker}(\phi) \cong \mathrm{Im}(\phi) $$
#+end_quote
*Intuition:* Taking the quotient $G / \mathrm{Ker}(\phi)$ "collapses" everything that mapped to the identity into a single point. What remains corresponds one-to-one with the image!

*** Worked Example: Wrapping the Real Line onto the Circle
Consider the map from real addition $(\mathbb{R}, +)$ to the complex unit circle $(S^1, \cdot)$:
$$ \phi : \mathbb{R} \to S^1, \quad \phi(x) = e^{i x} $$
1. *Check Homomorphism:*
   $$ \phi(x + y) = e^{i(x+y)} = e^{ix} e^{iy} = \phi(x) \cdot \phi(y) \quad \checkmark $$
2. *Find the Kernel:*
   What real numbers map to the identity $1 \in S^1$?
   $$ e^{ix} = 1 \iff x = 2\pi k \quad (k \in \mathbb{Z}) $$
   $$ \mathrm{Ker}(\phi) = 2\pi\mathbb{Z} = \{\dots, -4\pi, -2\pi, 0, 2\pi, 4\pi, \dots\} $$
3. *Find the Image:*
   As $x$ ranges over $\mathbb{R}$, $e^{ix}$ covers the entire circle $S^1$: $\mathrm{Im}(\phi) = S^1$.
4. *Apply First Isomorphism Theorem:*
   $$ \mathbb{R} / 2\pi\mathbb{Z} \cong S^1 $$
   *The quotient of the continuous real line by a discrete $2\pi$ translation grid is the circle!*
