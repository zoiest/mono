* 1. Equivalence Relations, Partitions, and Quotient Spaces (§2.9)

** 1.1 Equivalence Relations
A binary relation $\sim$ on a set $X$ is an *equivalence relation* if it satisfies three axioms for all $x, y, z \in X$:
1. *Reflexivity:* $x \sim x$
2. *Symmetry:* $x \sim y \implies y \sim x$
3. *Transitivity:* $x \sim y \text{ and } y \sim z \implies x \sim z$

** 1.2 Equivalence Classes and Quotient Spaces
- *Equivalence Class:* The subset of all elements equivalent to $x \in X$:
  $$ [x] = \{y \in X \mid y \sim x\} $$
- *Fundamental Partition Theorem:*
  Every equivalence relation on $X$ partitions $X$ into disjoint, non-empty equivalence classes:
  $$ X = \bigcup_{i} [x_i], \quad [x_i] \cap [x_j] = \emptyset \quad (i \neq j) $$
- *Quotient Space:* The set of all equivalence classes:
  $$ X / \sim \; = \{[x] \mid x \in X\} $$
  The canonical projection map is $\pi : X \to X/\sim, \, x \mapsto [x]$.

* 2. Group Actions: Translations, Conjugation, and Orbits (§2.9)

Let $G$ be a group acting on a set $X$ via a left action $\cdot : G \times X \to X$ satisfying $e \cdot x = x$ and $g_1 \cdot (g_2 \cdot x) = (g_1 g_2) \cdot x$.

** 2.1 Canonical Actions of $G$ on Itself ($X = G$)
When the set $X$ is the group $G$ itself, three fundamental actions arise:
1. *Left Translation:*
   $$ L_g : G \to G, \quad L_g(x) = g x $$
2. *Right Translation:*
   $$ R_g : G \to G, \quad R_g(x) = x g^{-1} $$
   /(The inverse $g^{-1}$ ensures the left-action compatibility: $R_{g_1 g_2}(x) = x(g_1 g_2)^{-1} = x g_2^{-1} g_1^{-1} = R_{g_1}(R_{g_2}(x))$)/.
3. *Conjugation (Inner Automorphism):*
   $$ C_g : G \to G, \quad C_g(x) = g x g^{-1} $$

** 2.2 Orbits and the Orbit Space $X/G$
- *Orbit of a point $x \in X$:* The set of all destinations of $x$ under the group action:
  $$ \mathcal{O}_x = G \cdot x = \{g \cdot x \mid g \in G\} \subseteq X $$
- *Orbit Equivalence Relation:*
  Define $x \sim y \iff \exists g \in G \text{ such that } y = g \cdot x$.
  - Reflexive: $x = e \cdot x \implies x \sim x$.
  - Symmetric: $y = g \cdot x \implies x = g^{-1} \cdot y \implies y \sim x$.
  - Transitive: $y = g \cdot x$ and $z = h \cdot y \implies z = (hg) \cdot x \implies x \sim z$.
- *Orbit Space:* The quotient space of orbits:
  $$ X/G = \{\mathcal{O}_x \mid x \in X\} $$
  The orbits partition $X$ into disjoint subsets: $X = \bigsqcup \mathcal{O}_x$.

** 2.3 Transitive Actions and Homogeneous Spaces
- An action of $G$ on $X$ is called *transitive* if there is only a single orbit:
  $$ \forall x, y \in X, \quad \exists g \in G \quad \text{such that} \quad y = g \cdot x $$
  In this case, $\mathcal{O}_x = X$ for any $x \in X$, and $X/G$ consists of a single point.
- *Homogeneous Space:* A space $X$ equipped with a transitive $G$-action. Every point in $X$ looks geometrically identical.
  - /Example:/ The 2-sphere $S^2$ is a homogeneous space under $\mathrm{SO}(3)$: any unit vector can be rotated into any other unit vector.

* 3. Conjugacy Classes and the Class Equation (§2.9)

** 3.1 Conjugacy Classes
When $G$ acts on itself by conjugation $C_g(x) = g x g^{-1}$, the orbits are called *conjugacy classes*:
$$ \mathrm{Cl}(x) = \{g x g^{-1} \mid g \in G\} $$
- Two elements $a, b \in G$ are *conjugate* ($a \sim b$) if $\exists g \in G$ such that $b = g a g^{-1}$.
- In matrix groups, conjugacy corresponds to similarity transformations ($B = P A P^{-1}$), meaning conjugate matrices share the same eigenvalues, trace, and determinant.
- In symmetric groups $S_n$, conjugate elements have identical cycle structures.

** 3.2 The Class Equation
Since conjugacy classes partition $G$:
$$ |G| = |Z(G)| + \sum_{i} |\mathrm{Cl}(x_i)| $$
where $Z(G)$ is the center (elements whose conjugacy class has size 1) and the sum runs over representatives of non-central conjugacy classes.

* 4. Cosets, Quotient Spaces $G/H$, and Lagrange's Theorem (§2.2)

** 4.1 Left and Right Cosets
Let $H \le G$ be a subgroup.
- *Left Coset of $H$ containing $g$:*
  $$ g H = \{g h \mid h \in H\} $$
- *Right Coset of $H$ containing $g$:*
  $$ H g = \{h g \mid h \in H\} $$
- *Properties:*
  1. $e \in H \implies g \in gH$.
  2. Two left cosets are either identical or disjoint: $g_1 H = g_2 H \iff g_1^{-1} g_2 \in H$.
  3. Every coset has the same cardinality as $H$: $|gH| = |H|$ (via the bijection $h \mapsto gh$).
  4. The left cosets partition $G$:
     $$ G = \bigsqcup_{i=1}^k g_i H $$

** 4.2 The Quotient Space $G/H$ and Index $[G:H]$
- The set of all left cosets is the *quotient space*:
  $$ G/H = \{gH \mid g \in G\} $$
- The *index* of $H$ in $G$, denoted $[G : H]$, is the number of distinct left cosets:
  $$ [G : H] = |G/H| $$

** 4.3 Lagrange's Theorem
#+begin_quote
*Theorem (Lagrange):*
If $G$ is a finite group and $H$ is a subgroup of $G$, then the order of $H$ divides the order of $G$:
$$ |G| = [G : H] \cdot |H| $$
#+end_quote
*Immediate Corollaries:*
1. *Element Order Divisibility:* For any $g \in G$, $\mathrm{ord}(g)$ divides $|G|$ (since $\mathrm{ord}(g) = |\langle g \rangle|$).
2. *Groups of Prime Order:* If $|G| = p$ (prime), then $G$ has no non-trivial proper subgroups, and $G \cong \mathbb{Z}_p$ is cyclic.
3. *Euler-Fermat Theorem:* In modular arithmetic, $a^{\phi(n)} \equiv 1 \pmod n$.

* 5. Isotropy Groups and the Orbit-Stabilizer Theorem (§2.9)

** 5.1 Isotropy Group (Stabilizer / Little Group)
Let $G$ act on $X$, and let $x \in X$.
The *isotropy group* (or *stabilizer*, *little group*) of $x$ is:
$$ G_x \equiv \mathrm{Stab}_G(x) = \{g \in G \mid g \cdot x = x\} $$

#+begin_quote
*Theorem:* The isotropy group $G_x$ is a subgroup of $G$ ($G_x \le G$).
#+end_quote
*Proof:*
1. *Identity:* By definition of group action, $e \cdot x = x$, so $e \in G_x$.
2. *Closure:* Let $g, h \in G_x$. Then $(gh) \cdot x = g \cdot (h \cdot x) = g \cdot x = x$, so $gh \in G_x$.
3. *Inverses:* Let $g \in G_x$, so $g \cdot x = x$. Acting on the left with $g^{-1}$:
   $$ g^{-1} \cdot (g \cdot x) = g^{-1} \cdot x \implies (g^{-1} g) \cdot x = g^{-1} \cdot x \implies e \cdot x = g^{-1} \cdot x \implies x = g^{-1} \cdot x $$
   Thus $g^{-1} \in G_x$. $\blacksquare$

** 5.2 The Orbit-Stabilizer Theorem
#+begin_quote
*Theorem (Orbit-Stabilizer Theorem):*
Let $G$ be a group acting on a set $X$. For any point $x \in X$, there is a natural canonical bijection between the orbit $\mathcal{O}_x$ and the quotient space of left cosets $G / G_x$:
$$ \mathcal{O}_x \cong G / G_x $$
Consequently, for a finite group $G$:
$$ |\mathcal{O}_x| = [G : G_x] = \frac{|G|}{|G_x|} $$
#+end_quote
*Proof:*
Define the mapping $f : G / G_x \to \mathcal{O}_x$ by $f(g G_x) = g \cdot x$.
1. *Well-defined:* If $g_1 G_x = g_2 G_x$, then $g_2^{-1} g_1 \in G_x \implies (g_2^{-1} g_1) \cdot x = x$.
   Applying $g_2$ gives $g_1 \cdot x = g_2 \cdot x$, so $f(g_1 G_x) = f(g_2 G_x)$.
2. *Injective:* If $g_1 \cdot x = g_2 \cdot x$, then $(g_2^{-1} g_1) \cdot x = x \implies g_2^{-1} g_1 \in G_x \implies g_1 G_x = g_2 G_x$.
3. *Surjective:* Any element in $\mathcal{O}_x$ is of the form $g \cdot x = f(g G_x)$.
Hence $f$ is a bijection. $\blacksquare$

** 5.3 Application: Homogeneous Spaces as Coset Manifolds
When $G$ acts transitively on $X$, $X = \mathcal{O}_x$ for any $x \in X$, yielding the geometric isomorphism:
$$ X \cong G / G_x $$
- /Example:/ $\mathrm{SO}(3)$ acts transitively on $S^2$. The stabilizer of the North Pole $\vec{e}_3$ is $\mathrm{SO}(2)$ (rotations around the $z$-axis).
  Therefore, the 2-sphere is realized as the coset manifold:
  $$ S^2 \cong \mathrm{SO}(3) / \mathrm{SO}(2) $$

* 6. Fixed Points and Burnside's Lemma (§2.9)

** 6.1 Fixed-Point Sets (Invariant Sets)
For a group element $g \in G$, the *invariant set* (or *fixed-point set*) is the subset of $X$ left fixed by $g$:
$$ X^g = \{x \in X \mid g \cdot x = x\} $$

** 6.2 Burnside's Lemma (Cauchy-Frobenius Lemma)
#+begin_quote
*Theorem (Burnside's Lemma):*
Let a finite group $G$ act on a finite set $X$. The number of distinct orbits $|X/G|$ is equal to the average number of points fixed by elements of $G$:
$$ |X/G| = \frac{1}{|G|} \sum_{g \in G} |X^g| $$
#+end_quote
*Proof:*
Count the set of pairs $S = \{(g, x) \in G \times X \mid g \cdot x = x\}$ in two ways:
$$ \sum_{g \in G} |X^g| = |S| = \sum_{x \in X} |G_x| $$
By the Orbit-Stabilizer Theorem, $|G_x| = \frac{|G|}{|\mathcal{O}_x|}$. Therefore:
$$ \sum_{g \in G} |X^g| = \sum_{x \in X} \frac{|G|}{|\mathcal{O}_x|} = |G| \sum_{x \in X} \frac{1}{|\mathcal{O}_x|} $$
In the sum $\sum_{x \in \mathcal{O}} \frac{1}{|\mathcal{O}|}$, there are $|\mathcal{O}|$ terms each equal to $\frac{1}{|\mathcal{O}|}$, so each orbit contributes exactly $1$.
Thus $\sum_{x \in X} \frac{1}{|\mathcal{O}_x|} = |X/G|$, which completes the proof. $\blacksquare$

* 7. Normal Subgroups and Quotient Groups (§2.3)

** 7.1 Normal Subgroup Definition
A subgroup $N \le G$ is called a *normal subgroup* (denoted $N \triangleleft G$) if it is invariant under conjugation by all elements of $G$:
$$ g N g^{-1} = N, \quad \forall g \in G $$
Equivalently:
- $g N = N g$ for all $g \in G$ (left cosets equal right cosets).
- $g n g^{-1} \in N$ for all $g \in G$ and all $n \in N$.

** 7.2 Quotient Group $G/N$
If and only if $N$ is normal, the quotient space $G/N$ can be endowed with a natural, well-defined group multiplication:
$$ (g_1 N) \cdot (g_2 N) \equiv (g_1 g_2) N $$
- *Well-definedness:* If $g_1' = g_1 n_1$ and $g_2' = g_2 n_2$, then:
  $$ g_1' g_2' = g_1 n_1 g_2 n_2 = g_1 g_2 (g_2^{-1} n_1 g_2) n_2 = (g_1 g_2) n_3 \in (g_1 g_2) N $$
  because normality guarantees $g_2^{-1} n_1 g_2 \in N$.
- *Group Properties of $G/N$:*
  - Identity: $e N = N$.
  - Inverses: $(g N)^{-1} = g^{-1} N$.
  - Associativity: Inherited from $G$.

* 8. The Center of a Group (§2.2, §2.9)

The *center* of a group $G$, denoted $Z(G)$, is the set of elements that commute with every element of $G$:
$$ Z(G) = \{z \in G \mid z g = g z, \, \forall g \in G\} $$

#+begin_quote
*Theorem:* The center $Z(G)$ is an Abelian normal subgroup of $G$ ($Z(G) \triangleleft G$).
#+end_quote
*Proof:*
1. $e \in Z(G)$ trivially.
2. If $z_1, z_2 \in Z(G)$, $(z_1 z_2) g = z_1 (z_2 g) = z_1 (g z_2) = (z_1 g) z_2 = g (z_1 z_2) \implies z_1 z_2 \in Z(G)$.
3. If $z \in Z(G)$, $z g = g z \implies g z^{-1} = z^{-1} g \implies z^{-1} \in Z(G)$.
4. *Normality:* For any $g \in G$ and $z \in Z(G)$, $g z g^{-1} = z g g^{-1} = z \in Z(G)$.
5. *Abelian:* By definition, all elements in $Z(G)$ commute with all elements of $G$, and hence with each other. $\blacksquare$

* 9. Homomorphisms and the First Isomorphism Theorem (§2.3)

** 9.1 Kernel and Image
Let $\phi : G \to H$ be a group homomorphism ($\phi(a b) = \phi(a) \phi(b)$).
- *Image:*
  $$ \mathrm{Im}(\phi) = \{\phi(g) \mid g \in G\} \le H $$
- *Kernel:*
  $$ \mathrm{Ker}(\phi) = \{g \in G \mid \phi(g) = e_H\} $$

#+begin_quote
*Theorem:* The kernel $\mathrm{Ker}(\phi)$ is always a *normal subgroup* of $G$ ($\mathrm{Ker}(\phi) \triangleleft G$).
#+end_quote
*Proof:*
Let $k \in \mathrm{Ker}(\phi)$ and $g \in G$. Then:
$$ \phi(g k g^{-1}) = \phi(g) \phi(k) \phi(g^{-1}) = \phi(g) e_H \phi(g)^{-1} = \phi(g) \phi(g)^{-1} = e_H $$
Hence $g k g^{-1} \in \mathrm{Ker}(\phi)$, proving normality. $\blacksquare$

** 9.2 The First Isomorphism Theorem
#+begin_quote
*Theorem (First Isomorphism Theorem):*
Let $\phi : G \to H$ be a group homomorphism. Then the quotient group $G / \mathrm{Ker}(\phi)$ is isomorphic to the image $\mathrm{Im}(\phi)$:
$$ G / \mathrm{Ker}(\phi) \cong \mathrm{Im}(\phi) $$
#+end_quote
*Proof:*
Define $\Phi : G / \mathrm{Ker}(\phi) \to \mathrm{Im}(\phi)$ by $\Phi(g \mathrm{Ker}(\phi)) = \phi(g)$.
1. *Well-defined and Injective:*
   $g_1 \mathrm{Ker}(\phi) = g_2 \mathrm{Ker}(\phi) \iff g_2^{-1} g_1 \in \mathrm{Ker}(\phi) \iff \phi(g_2^{-1} g_1) = e_H \iff \phi(g_1) = \phi(g_2)$.
2. *Homomorphism:*
   $\Phi(g_1 N \cdot g_2 N) = \Phi(g_1 g_2 N) = \phi(g_1 g_2) = \phi(g_1)\phi(g_2) = \Phi(g_1 N)\Phi(g_2 N)$.
3. *Surjective:* Every element in $\mathrm{Im}(\phi)$ is of the form $\phi(g) = \Phi(g N)$.
Thus $\Phi$ is an isomorphism. $\blacksquare$
