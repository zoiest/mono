* 1. Definition of a Group and the Algebraic Hierarchy

** 1.1 The Group Axioms
Consider an arbitrary non-empty set $G$ and an internal composition law (binary operation)
$$ \cdot : G \times G \to G, \quad (a, b) \mapsto a \cdot b $$
The algebraic pair $(G, \cdot)$ is defined to be a *group* if the following four conditions simultaneously hold:

- *G0 (Closure / Well-defined composition):*
  For all $a, b \in G$, the product $a \cdot b \in G$.
- *G1 (Associativity):*
  For all $a, b, c \in G$,
  $$ a \cdot (b \cdot c) = (a \cdot b) \cdot c $$
- *G2 (Existence of Identity / Unit Element):*
  There exists an element $e \in G$ (often denoted $1$ or $e$) such that for all $a \in G$:
  $$ a \cdot e = e \cdot a = a $$
- *G3 (Existence of Inverse):*
  For every element $a \in G$, there exists an element $a^{-1} \in G$ such that:
  $$ a \cdot a^{-1} = a^{-1} \cdot a = e $$

When the binary operation is clear from the context, we simply refer to the group as $G$ and omit the symbol $\cdot$, writing $ab$ instead of $a \cdot b$.

** 1.2 Abelian (Commutative) Groups
A group $(G, \cdot)$ is said to be *Abelian* (or *commutative*) if it satisfies the additional axiom:
- *AG4 (Commutativity):*
  For all $a, b \in G$,
  $$ a \cdot b = b \cdot a $$
Abelian groups are named after the Norwegian mathematician Niels Henrik Abel.

** 1.3 The Hierarchy of Algebraic Structures
Before groups, several weaker structures are defined in abstract algebra by relaxing axioms:
- *Magma:* A set $M$ equipped with a well-defined binary operation closed on $M$ (satisfies G0).
- *Semigroup:* An associative magma (satisfies G0, G1).
- *Monoid:* A semigroup with an identity element (satisfies G0, G1, G2).
- *Group:* A monoid in which every element has an inverse (satisfies G0, G1, G2, G3).
- *Abelian Group:* A group with commutative multiplication (satisfies G0–G3, AG4).

| Algebraic Structure | Closure (G0) | Associativity (G1) | Identity (G2) | Inverses (G3) | Commutativity (AG4) |
|---------------------+--------------+--------------------+---------------+---------------+---------------------|
| Magma               | Yes          | No                 | No            | No            | Optional            |
| Semigroup           | Yes          | Yes                | No            | No            | Optional            |
| Monoid              | Yes          | Yes                | Yes           | No            | Optional            |
| *Group*             | *Yes*        | *Yes*              | *Yes*         | *Yes*         | Optional            |
| *Abelian Group*     | *Yes*        | *Yes*              | *Yes*         | *Yes*         | *Yes*               |

* 2. Elementary Theorems and Properties

** 2.1 Uniqueness of the Identity Element
#+begin_quote
*Theorem:* In any group $G$, the identity element $e$ is unique.
#+end_quote
*Proof:*
Suppose both $e$ and $e'$ are identity elements in $G$.
1. Since $e$ is an identity: $e \cdot e' = e'$.
2. Since $e'$ is an identity: $e \cdot e' = e$.
Therefore, $e = e \cdot e' = e'$, proving $e = e'$. $\blacksquare$

** 2.2 Uniqueness of Inverses
#+begin_quote
*Theorem:* For every element $a \in G$, its inverse $a^{-1}$ is unique.
#+end_quote
*Proof:*
Suppose both $b$ and $b'$ are inverses of $a$. Then $ba = e$ and $ab' = e$.
Consider the product $b a b'$:
$$ b = be = b(ab') = (ba)b' = eb' = b' $$
Thus $b = b'$. $\blacksquare$

** 2.3 Algebraic Consequences
- *Inverse of an Inverse:*
  $$ (a^{-1})^{-1} = a $$
- *Inverse of a Product (Shoe-Sock Theorem):*
  $$ (a \cdot b)^{-1} = b^{-1} \cdot a^{-1} $$
  *Proof:* $(ab)(b^{-1}a^{-1}) = a(b b^{-1})a^{-1} = a e a^{-1} = a a^{-1} = e$. Since inverses are unique, $(ab)^{-1} = b^{-1}a^{-1}$.
- *Left and Right Cancellation Laws:*
  - Left cancellation: $a b = a c \implies b = c$ (multiply on left by $a^{-1}$).
  - Right cancellation: $b a = c a \implies b = c$ (multiply on right by $a^{-1}$).

* 3. Orders of Groups and Elements

** 3.1 Group Order
- The *order of a group* $G$, denoted $|G|$, is the cardinality of the underlying set.
- If $|G| < \infty$, $G$ is called a *finite group*.
- If $|G| = \aleph_0$ (countably infinite), $G$ is a *discrete group* (e.g., $(\mathbb{Z}, +)$).
- If $G$ is uncountably infinite and parameterized by continuous parameters, $G$ is a *continuous group* (e.g., $(\mathbb{R}, +)$, $\mathrm{SO}(3)$).
- The *trivial group* $Z_1 = \{e\}$ has order $|Z_1| = 1$. There is no group of order 0 because G2 demands the existence of at least $e$.

** 3.2 Element Order
Let $g \in G$. We define powers of $g$:
$$ g^0 = e, \quad g^n = \underbrace{g \cdot g \cdots g}_{n \text{ times}}, \quad g^{-n} = (g^{-1})^n $$
The *order of an element* $g$, denoted $\mathrm{ord}(g)$ or $|g|$, is the smallest positive integer $n \in \mathbb{Z}^+$ such that:
$$ g^n = e $$
If no such positive integer exists, $g$ is said to have *infinite order*.
In a finite group $G$, every element has finite order (by Lagrange's theorem, $\mathrm{ord}(g)$ must divide $|G|$).

* 4. Cayley Tables and the Rearrangement Theorem

** 4.1 Cayley Table (Multiplication Table)
For a finite group $G = \{g_1, g_2, \dots, g_n\}$, the Cayley table is an $n \times n$ grid whose $(i, j)$-entry is the group product $g_i \cdot g_j$:

#+caption: Cayley table for the cyclic group $\mathbb{Z}_2 = \{e, a\}$ where $a^2 = e$
| $\cdot$ | $e$ | $a$ |
|---------+-----+-----|
| $e$     | $e$ | $a$ |
| $a$     | $a$ | $e$ |

** 4.2 The Rearrangement Theorem (Latin Square Property)
#+begin_quote
*Theorem (Rearrangement Theorem):*
In the Cayley table of a finite group $G$, each element of $G$ appears *exactly once* in each row and *exactly once* in each column.
#+end_quote
*Proof:*
Consider the row corresponding to $g \in G$. The entries in this row are $g g_1, g g_2, \dots, g g_n$.
1. *All entries are distinct:* Suppose $g g_j = g g_k$. By left cancellation, $g_j = g_k$.
2. *Every element appears:* Let $h \in G$. The equation $g x = h$ has the unique solution $x = g^{-1}h \in G$. Thus $h = g(g^{-1}h)$ appears in the row.
An identical argument using right multiplication shows each element appears exactly once in each column. $\blacksquare$

*Consequence:* The Cayley table forms a *Latin square*. A group is Abelian if and only if its Cayley table is symmetric under reflection across the main diagonal.

* 5. Abstract Groups vs. Realizations and Representations

An *abstract group* is defined entirely by the multiplication law between elements without reference to what the elements represent.
A *realization* of an abstract group $G$ is an injective group homomorphism from $G$ to a concrete mathematical set equipped with a binary operation.

** 5.1 Realizations of the Cyclic Group $\mathbb{Z}_2$
The abstract group $\mathbb{Z}_2 = \{e, a\}$ with $a^2 = e$ can be realized in many contexts:
1. *Modular arithmetic:* $(\{0, 1\}, + \pmod 2)$ with $e \mapsto 0$, $a \mapsto 1$.
2. *Multiplicative signs:* $(\{1, -1\}, \times)$ with $e \mapsto 1$, $a \mapsto -1$.
3. *Boolean logic:* $(\{\text{FALSE}, \text{TRUE}\}, \text{XOR})$ with $e \mapsto \text{FALSE}$, $a \mapsto \text{TRUE}$.
4. *Permutations:* In the symmetric group $S_2$, $e \mapsto (1)(2)$, $a \mapsto (12)$.
5. *Linear representations (matrices):*
   $$ D(e) = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}, \quad D(a) = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix} \quad \text{or} \quad D(a) = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & -1 \end{pmatrix} $$
Realizations via linear transformations on vector spaces are called *group representations* (see [[id:ef2abac8-273c-404c-8140-d048794366d9][Complex Vector Spaces and Representations]]).

* 6. Direct Product of Groups

Given two groups $(G_1, \cdot_1)$ and $(G_2, \cdot_2)$, their *external direct product* is:
$$ G_1 \times G_2 = \{ (g_1, g_2) \mid g_1 \in G_1, g_2 \in G_2 \} $$
with the componentwise composition law:
$$ (g_1, g_2) \cdot (g_1', g_2') = (g_1 \cdot_1 g_1', g_2 \cdot_2 g_2') $$
- *Identity element:* $e_{G_1 \times G_2} = (e_1, e_2)$
- *Inverses:* $(g_1, g_2)^{-1} = (g_1^{-1}, g_2^{-1})$
- *Order:* If $G_1$ and $G_2$ are finite, $|G_1 \times G_2| = |G_1| \cdot |G_2|$.

* 7. Examples of Groups and Non-Groups

** 7.1 Standard Examples of Groups
1. $(\mathbb{Z}, +)$: Discrete, countably infinite Abelian group. Identity is $0$, inverse of $a$ is $-a$.
2. $(\mathbb{R}, +)$ and $(\mathbb{C}, +)$: Continuous Abelian groups of dimensions 1 and 2, respectively.
3. $(\mathbb{R} \setminus \{0\}, \cdot)$ and $(\mathbb{C} \setminus \{0\}, \cdot)$: Multiplicative groups of non-zero numbers. Identity is $1$, inverse of $x$ is $1/x$.
4. $(\mathrm{Mat}(n \times m, \mathbb{R}), +)$: Continuous Abelian group of dimension $nm$ (isomorphic to $\mathbb{R}^{nm}$).
5. $\mathrm{GL}(n, \mathbb{R}) = \{M \in \mathrm{Mat}(n, \mathbb{R}) \mid \det M \neq 0\}$: General linear group under matrix multiplication. Identity is $\mathbf{1}_n$. Non-Abelian for $n \ge 2$.
6. *Dihedral Groups* $D_n$: Symmetry groups of regular $n$-sided polygons. $|D_n| = 2n$ ($n$ rotations, $n$ reflections). Non-Abelian for $n \ge 3$.
7. *Orthogonal Group* $O(2)$: The group of $2 \times 2$ real matrices preserving distances in $\mathbb{R}^2$:
   $$ A = \begin{pmatrix} \cos\alpha & \sin\alpha \\ \mp\sin\alpha & \pm\cos\alpha \end{pmatrix}, \quad \alpha \in \mathbb{R} $$

** 7.2 Counterexamples: Sets That Are NOT Groups
1. *$(\mathbb{N}, +)$ (Natural numbers with addition):*
   Not a group. It is closed and associative, and has identity $0$, but no non-zero element has an additive inverse in $\mathbb{N}$. It is merely a *monoid*.
2. *$(\mathbb{R}^3, \times)$ (Vector cross product):*
   Not a group. The cross product $\vec{a} \times \vec{b}$ is closed in $\mathbb{R}^3$, so it is a *magma*. However, it is not associative:
   $$ \vec{a} \times (\vec{b} \times \vec{c}) \neq (\vec{a} \times \vec{b}) \times \vec{c} $$
   Moreover, there is no identity element.
3. *$(\mathbb{R}^3, \cdot)$ (Vector scalar / dot product):*
   *Not even a magma.* The binary operation $\vec{a} \cdot \vec{b} \in \mathbb{R}$ maps $\mathbb{R}^3 \times \mathbb{R}^3 \to \mathbb{R}$, which is not internal to $\mathbb{R}^3$.
4. *$\{M \in \mathrm{Mat}(n, \mathbb{R}) \mid \det M \neq 0\}$ with matrix addition:*
   *Not a magma.* The sum of two invertible matrices need not be invertible. For instance, $\mathbf{1}_n + (-\mathbf{1}_n) = \mathbf{0}$, which has determinant $0$ and lies outside the set.
