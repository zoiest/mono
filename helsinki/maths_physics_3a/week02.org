* 1. Subgroups and Subgroup Criteria (§2.2)

** 1.1 Definition of Subgroup
A non-empty subset $H \subseteq G$ of a group $(G, \cdot)$ is a *subgroup* (denoted $H \le G$) if $H$ forms a group under the restricted operation of $G$.
- *Proper Subgroup:* $H < G$ where $H \neq G$.
- *Trivial Subgroups:* The identity $\{e\}$ and $G$ itself.

** 1.2 Subgroup Criteria
Instead of checking all 4 group axioms, one can use compact tests:
1. *One-Step Subgroup Test:*
   $$ H \le G \iff \forall a, b \in H, \quad a \cdot b^{-1} \in H $$
2. *Finite Subgroup Criterion:*
   If $H$ is a *finite* non-empty subset of $G$:
   $$ H \le G \iff \forall a, b \in H, \quad a \cdot b \in H $$
   *(For finite sets, closure alone automatically guarantees the existence of inverses!)*

*** Worked Example 1.1: Applying Subgroup Tests
1. *Test that $\mathrm{SL}(n, \mathbb{R}) \le \mathrm{GL}(n, \mathbb{R})$ using the One-Step Test:*
   Let $A, B \in \mathrm{SL}(n, \mathbb{R})$, so $\det A = 1$ and $\det B = 1$.
   Compute $\det(A B^{-1})$:
   $$ \det(A B^{-1}) = \det(A) \det(B^{-1}) = \det(A) (\det B)^{-1} = 1 \cdot 1^{-1} = 1 $$
   Since $\det(A B^{-1}) = 1$, $A B^{-1} \in \mathrm{SL}(n, \mathbb{R})$.
   By the One-Step Subgroup Test, $\mathrm{SL}(n, \mathbb{R})$ is a subgroup of $\mathrm{GL}(n, \mathbb{R})$. $\checkmark$
2. *Test the Finite Subgroup Criterion on $H = \{1, -1, i, -i\} \subset \mathbb{C}^\times$:*
   $H$ is finite ($|H| = 4$). We only need to check closure under multiplication:
   $i^2 = -1 \in H$, $i^3 = -i \in H$, $i^4 = 1 \in H$, $(-1)^2 = 1 \in H$, etc.
   Since products of any two elements in $H$ remain in $H$, $H$ is automatically a subgroup of $(\mathbb{C} \setminus \{0\}, \times)$ without explicitly verifying inverses! $\checkmark$

* 2. Permutation Theory, Cycles, and Alternating Groups (§2.5)

** 2.1 Cycle Decompositions and Transpositions
Let $S_n$ be the symmetric group on $\{1, 2, \dots, n\}$.
- *Cycle of Length $k$ ($k$-cycle):* $(a_1 \, a_2 \, \dots \, a_k)$ maps $a_1 \mapsto a_2 \mapsto \dots \mapsto a_k \mapsto a_1$.
  - Disjoint cycles share no common elements and *always commute*: $\sigma \tau = \tau \sigma$.
  - Every permutation decomposes uniquely into a product of disjoint cycles.
- *Transposition (Swap / 2-cycle):* A swap $(a \, b)$.
  - Any $k$-cycle can be written as a product of $k-1$ transpositions:
    $$ (a_1 \, a_2 \, a_3 \, \dots \, a_k) = (a_1 \, a_k)(a_1 \, a_{k-1}) \cdots (a_1 \, a_3)(a_1 \, a_2) $$
  - Consequently, *every permutation is a product of transpositions*.

** 2.2 Signature Homomorphism and $A_n$
The *signature* (or parity) of a permutation $\pi$ is:
$$ \mathrm{sgn}(\pi) = (-1)^N $$
where $N$ is the number of transpositions in any decomposition of $\pi$.
- *Homomorphism:* $\mathrm{sgn}(\pi_1 \pi_2) = \mathrm{sgn}(\pi_1) \mathrm{sgn}(\pi_2)$.
- *Signature of a $k$-cycle:*
  $$ \mathrm{sgn}(a_1 \, a_2 \, \dots \, a_k) = (-1)^{k-1} $$
  - *Odd-length cycles are EVEN permutations!* (e.g. $\mathrm{sgn}(123) = (-1)^2 = +1$).
  - *Even-length cycles are ODD permutations!* (e.g. $\mathrm{sgn}(12) = (-1)^1 = -1$).
- *Alternating Group $A_n$:* The normal subgroup of all even permutations ($\mathrm{Ker}(\mathrm{sgn})$):
  $$ |A_n| = \frac{n!}{2} $$

*** Worked Example 2.1: Cycle Decomposition, Signature, and 3-Cycles
Consider the permutation in Cauchy two-line notation in $S_6$:
$$ \pi = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 & 3 & 4 & 5 & 6 \\ 3 & 5 & 4 & 1 & 2 & 6 \end{pmatrix} $$
1. *Disjoint Cycle Decomposition:*
   - Start with 1: $1 \mapsto 3 \mapsto 4 \mapsto 1 \implies (1 \, 3 \, 4)$.
   - Next unused element 2: $2 \mapsto 5 \mapsto 2 \implies (2 \, 5)$.
   - Remaining element 6: $6 \mapsto 6 \implies (6)$ (fixed point).
   $$ \pi = (1 \, 3 \, 4)(2 \, 5) $$
2. *Transposition Decomposition and Signature:*
   - $(1 \, 3 \, 4) = (1 \, 4)(1 \, 3)$ (2 transpositions).
   - $(2 \, 5)$ is 1 transposition.
   $$ \pi = (1 \, 4)(1 \, 3)(2 \, 5) \quad (\text{total } N = 3 \text{ transpositions}) $$
   $$ \mathrm{sgn}(\pi) = (-1)^3 = -1 \quad (\text{Odd permutation!}) $$
3. *Writing an Even Permutation as a Product of 3-Cycles:*
   Now consider the even permutation $\tau = (1 \, 2 \, 3 \, 4 \, 5) \in A_5$.
   Expand into transpositions:
   $$ \tau = (1 \, 5)(1 \, 4)(1 \, 3)(1 \, 2) $$
   Group into pairs and apply the 3-cycle identity $(ab)(bc) = (abc)$:
   - Pair 1: $(1 \, 5)(1 \, 4) = (1 \, 5 \, 4)$.
   - Pair 2: $(1 \, 3)(1 \, 2) = (1 \, 3 \, 2)$.
   $$ \tau = (1 \, 5 \, 4)(1 \, 3 \, 2) \quad (\text{a product of two 3-cycles!}) $$

** 2.3 Permutation Matrices
To each permutation $\pi \in S_n$, we associate an $n \times n$ matrix $D(\pi) \in \mathrm{GL}(n, \mathbb{R})$ defined by:
$$ [D(\pi)]_{ij} = \delta_{i, \pi(j)} $$
- *Properties:*
  1. $D(\pi_1 \pi_2) = D(\pi_1) D(\pi_2)$ (Homomorphism).
  2. $D(\pi)^T D(\pi) = \mathbf{1}_n$ (Orthogonal matrix).
  3. $\det D(\pi) = \mathrm{sgn}(\pi) = \pm 1$.

*** Worked Example 2.2: Permutation Matrices in $S_3$
In $S_3$, consider the transposition $\pi_1 = (1 \, 2)$ and the 3-cycle $\pi_2 = (1 \, 2 \, 3)$:
$$ D((1 \, 2)) = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad D((1 \, 2 \, 3)) = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix} $$
Compute determinants:
- $\det D((1 \, 2)) = 0 - 1(1) + 0 = -1 = \mathrm{sgn}((1 \, 2))$. $\checkmark$
- $\det D((1 \, 2 \, 3)) = 1(1 - 0) = +1 = \mathrm{sgn}((1 \, 2 \, 3))$. $\checkmark$

* 3. Integer Partitions and Young Diagrams (§2.6)

** 3.1 Integer Partitions
An *integer partition* of $N \in \mathbb{N}$ is a representation of $N$ as a sum of positive integers in non-increasing order:
$$ N = \lambda_1 + \lambda_2 + \dots + \lambda_k, \quad \lambda_1 \ge \lambda_2 \ge \dots \ge \lambda_k \ge 1 $$
The total number of partitions is denoted $p(N)$.

** 3.2 Young Diagrams and Physical Quantum States
A *Young diagram* represents a partition by rows of empty square boxes:
- Row $i$ has $\lambda_i$ boxes.
- *Total boxes:* $N$.

#+begin_quote
*The Tri-Fold Correspondence:*
$$ \text{Partitions } \lambda \vdash N \longleftrightarrow \text{Young Diagrams of size } N \longleftrightarrow \text{Conjugacy Classes of } S_N \longleftrightarrow \text{Irreducible Reps of } S_N $$
#+end_quote

*** Worked Example 3.1: All Partitions and Young Diagrams for $N = 4$ ($p(4) = 5$)
There are exactly 5 partitions of $4$:

1. *$\lambda = (4)$:* Four boxes in a single row:
   $$\begin{array}{|c|c|c|c|} \hline \; & \; & \; & \; \\ \hline \end{array}$$
   - *Conjugacy Class:* 4-cycles $(1234)$ (order 4, size 6).
   - *Quantum Physics:* *Totally Symmetric* state (Bosons, e.g. photons, helium-4 atoms).
2. *$\lambda = (3, 1)$:* Three boxes, then one box:
   $$\begin{array}{|c|c|c|} \hline \; & \; & \; \\ \hline \multicolumn{1}{|c|}{\;} \\ \cline{1-1} \end{array}$$
   - *Conjugacy Class:* 3-cycles $(123)(4)$ (order 3, size 8).
   - *Quantum Physics:* Mixed symmetry (standard 3-dimensional representation).
3. *$\lambda = (2, 2)$:* Two rows of two boxes:
   $$\begin{array}{|c|c|} \hline \; & \; \\ \hline \; & \; \\ \hline \end{array}$$
   - *Conjugacy Class:* Double transpositions $(12)(34)$ (order 2, size 3).
   - *Quantum Physics:* Mixed symmetry (2-dimensional representation).
4. *$\lambda = (2, 1, 1)$:* Two boxes, then two single boxes:
   $$\begin{array}{|c|c|} \hline \; & \; \\ \hline \multicolumn{1}{|c|}{\;} \\ \cline{1-1} \multicolumn{1}{|c|}{\;} \\ \cline{1-1} \end{array}$$
   - *Conjugacy Class:* Single transpositions $(12)(3)(4)$ (order 2, size 6).
   - *Quantum Physics:* Mixed symmetry (3-dimensional representation).
5. *$\lambda = (1, 1, 1, 1)$:* Four boxes in a single column:
   $$\begin{array}{|c|} \hline \; \\ \hline \; \\ \hline \; \\ \hline \; \\ \hline \end{array}$$
   - *Conjugacy Class:* Identity $(1)(2)(3)(4) = e$ (order 1, size 1).
   - *Quantum Physics:* *Totally Antisymmetric* state (Fermions, Pauli Exclusion Principle, Slater determinants).

*Check Class Sizes:* $6 + 8 + 3 + 6 + 1 = 24 = 4! = |S_4|$.

* 4. Cayley's Classification Theorem (§2.5)

#+begin_quote
*Theorem (Cayley):*
Every finite group $G$ of order $n$ is isomorphic to a subgroup of the symmetric group $S_n$:
$$ G \hookrightarrow S_n $$
#+end_quote
*Intuition:* Every group element $g$ can be viewed as an operator that permutes the $n$ entries of the group's own Cayley table via left multiplication $L_g(x) = g x$.

*** Worked Example 4.1: Embedding the Klein Four-Group $V_4 \hookrightarrow S_4$
Let $V_4 = \{e, a, b, c\}$ where $a^2 = b^2 = c^2 = e$ and $ab = ba = c$.
Label the 4 elements as indices: $1 = e$, $2 = a$, $3 = b$, $4 = c$.
Compute the permutation induced by left multiplication $L_g(x) = g x$:
- *For element $e$ ($1$):* Leaves all elements unchanged:
  $$ L_e = (1)(2)(3)(4) = e \in S_4 $$
- *For element $a$ ($2$):*
  $a \cdot e = a$ ($1 \mapsto 2$), $a \cdot a = e$ ($2 \mapsto 1$), $a \cdot b = c$ ($3 \mapsto 4$), $a \cdot c = b$ ($4 \mapsto 3$).
  $$ L_a = (1 \, 2)(3 \, 4) \in S_4 $$
- *For element $b$ ($3$):*
  $b \cdot e = b$ ($1 \mapsto 3$), $b \cdot a = c$ ($2 \mapsto 4$), $b \cdot b = e$ ($3 \mapsto 1$), $b \cdot c = a$ ($4 \mapsto 2$).
  $$ L_b = (1 \, 3)(2 \, 4) \in S_4 $$
- *For element $c$ ($4$):*
  $c \cdot e = c$ ($1 \mapsto 4$), $c \cdot a = b$ ($2 \mapsto 3$), $c \cdot b = a$ ($3 \mapsto 2$), $c \cdot c = e$ ($4 \mapsto 1$).
  $$ L_c = (1 \, 4)(2 \, 3) \in S_4 $$
*Conclusion:* The subgroup $\{e, \, (12)(34), \, (13)(24), \, (14)(23)\} \le S_4$ is an exact, faithful copy of $V_4$ embedded inside $S_4$!

* 5. Continuous Groups and Lie Groups (§2.8)

** 5.1 Lie Groups and Lie Algebras
A *Lie group* is a group that is also a smooth manifold.
- *Group Dimension:* The dimension $\dim G = d$ is the number of independent continuous real parameters needed to specify an element near the identity.
- *Lie Algebra $\mathfrak{g} = T_e G$:* The tangent space at the identity element $e = \mathbf{1}$.
  Infinitesimally, any group element near identity can be expanded as:
  $$ g(\epsilon) = \mathbf{1} + \epsilon X + \mathcal{O}(\epsilon^2), \quad X \in \mathfrak{g} $$
  $$ \dim G = \dim_{\mathbb{R}} \mathfrak{g} $$

* 6. Classical Matrix Lie Groups and Real Dimensions (§2.8)

#+caption: Classical Matrix Groups and Their Lie Algebra Dimensions
| Group | Invariance Condition | Lie Algebra Condition | Real Dimension | Physical Application |
|---+---+---+:---:|---|
| $\mathrm{GL}(n, \mathbb{R})$ | $\det M \neq 0$ | Arbitrary real matrix | $n^2$ | General coordinate frames |
| $\mathrm{GL}(n, \mathbb{C})$ | $\det M \neq 0$ | Arbitrary complex matrix | $2n^2$ | Complex linear systems |
| $\mathrm{SL}(n, \mathbb{R})$ | $\det M = 1$ | Traceless: $\mathrm{Tr}(X) = 0$ | $n^2 - 1$ | Volume-preserving flows |
| $\mathrm{O}(n)$ & $\mathrm{SO}(n)$ | $M^T M = \mathbf{1}_n$ | Skew-symmetric: $X^T = -X$ | $\frac{n(n-1)}{2}$ | Spatial rotations |
| $\mathrm{U}(n)$ | $U^\dagger U = \mathbf{1}_n$ | Skew-Hermitian: $X^\dagger = -X$ | $n^2$ | Quantum state evolution |
| $\mathrm{SU}(n)$ | $U^\dagger U = \mathbf{1}, \, \det U = 1$ | Skew-Hermitian + Traceless | $n^2 - 1$ | Electroweak & Strong gauge groups ($\mathrm{SU}(2), \mathrm{SU}(3)$) |
| $\mathrm{Sp}(2n, \mathbb{R})$ | $M^T \Omega M = \Omega$ | $\Omega X$ is symmetric | $n(2n + 1)$ | Classical Hamiltonian mechanics |

*** Worked Example 6.1: Deriving the Dimensions of $\mathrm{SO}(3)$ and $\mathrm{SU}(2)$
1. *$\mathrm{SO}(3)$ (3D Spatial Rotations):*
   - Group condition: $R^T R = \mathbf{1}_3$ and $\det R = 1$.
   - Lie algebra $\mathfrak{so}(3)$: Expand $R = \mathbf{1} + \epsilon X$:
     $$ (\mathbf{1} + \epsilon X^T)(\mathbf{1} + \epsilon X) = \mathbf{1} + \epsilon(X^T + X) + \mathcal{O}(\epsilon^2) = \mathbf{1} \implies X^T = -X $$
     The generators are $3 \times 3$ real *skew-symmetric* matrices.
   - Diagonal must be zero. Upper triangle has $\frac{3(3-1)}{2} = 3$ independent entries:
     $$ J_x = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & -1 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}, \quad J_y = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \\ -1 & 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}, \quad J_z = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & -1 & 0 \\ 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix} $$
   - Therefore:
     $$ \dim \mathrm{SO}(3) = 3 $$
2. *$\mathrm{SU}(2)$ (Spin-1/2 Symmetries):*
   - Group condition: $U^\dagger U = \mathbf{1}_2$ and $\det U = 1$.
   - Lie algebra $\mathfrak{su}(2)$: Expand $U = \mathbf{1} + \epsilon X$:
     $$ X^\dagger = -X \quad (\text{skew-Hermitian}) \quad \text{and} \quad \mathrm{Tr}(X) = 0 \quad (\text{traceless}) $$
   - Standard physics basis: $X_k = -\frac{i}{2} \sigma_k$ where $\sigma_k$ are the 3 Hermitian Pauli matrices:
     $$ \sigma_x = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}, \quad \sigma_y = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & -i \\ i & 0 \end{pmatrix}, \quad \sigma_z = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & -1 \end{pmatrix} $$
   - Since there are 3 Pauli matrices, there are 3 independent real parameters:
     $$ \dim \mathrm{SU}(2) = 2^2 - 1 = 3 $$

* 7. Group Actions on Sets (§2.8, §2.9)

** 7.1 Left Action vs. Right Action
- *Left Action:* $g_1 \cdot (g_2 \cdot x) = (g_1 g_2) \cdot x$.
- *Right Action:* $(x \cdot g_1) \cdot g_2 = x \cdot (g_1 g_2)$.

*** Worked Example 7.1: Active vs. Passive Transformations in Physics
In field theory, how does a spatial rotation $R \in \mathrm{SO}(3)$ act on a scalar field $\phi(x)$?
- *Active Transformation on Coordinates (Left Action):*
  $$ x \mapsto x' = R x $$
- *Passive Transformation on the Field $\phi$:*
  The value of the rotated field $\phi'$ at the new point $x'$ must equal the value of the original field at the old point $x$:
  $$ \phi'(x') = \phi(x) \implies \phi'(R x) = \phi(x) \implies \phi'(x) = \phi(R^{-1} x) $$
- *Why is the inverse $R^{-1}$ necessary?*
  Let us test successive transformations $R_1$ followed by $R_2$:
  $$ (\phi'_{R_1})'_{R_2}(x) = \phi'_{R_1}(R_2^{-1} x) = \phi(R_1^{-1}(R_2^{-1} x)) = \phi((R_2 R_1)^{-1} x) = \phi'_{R_2 R_1}(x) $$
  Because $(R_2 R_1)^{-1} = R_1^{-1} R_2^{-1}$, the inverse ensures that the induced action on function space preserves the group multiplication order!
