In physics, a *symmetry* is a transformation that leaves the equations of motion or Hamiltonian invariant. When we combine two symmetries sequentially, we obtain another symmetry.
Abstract algebra classifies these systems by asking: *What minimum rules does this combination operation satisfy?*

* The Algebraic Hierarchy of Sets with Binary Operations (§2.1)

Let $S$ be a non-empty set and $\cdot : S \times S \to S$ a binary operation that maps each ordered pair $(a, b) \in S \times S$ to a unique element $a \cdot b \in S$.

** Axioms of Algebraic Structures

#+caption: The Core Axioms
#+begin_quote
- *(G0) Closure (Internal Operation):* $\forall a, b \in S \implies a \cdot b \in S$.
- *(G1) Associativity:* $\forall a, b, c \in S \implies (a \cdot b) \cdot c = a \cdot (b \cdot c)$.
- *(G2) Identity Element:* $\exists e \in S$ such that $\forall a \in S \implies a \cdot e = e \cdot a = a$.
- *(G3) Inverse Element:* $\forall a \in S, \, \exists a^{-1} \in S$ such that $a \cdot a^{-1} = a^{-1} \cdot a = e$.
- *(AG4) Commutativity (Abelian Property):* $\forall a, b \in S \implies a \cdot b = b \cdot a$.
#+end_quote

*** Worked Example: Diagnosing Algebraic Structures
Let us test various sets and operations to pinpoint their exact algebraic classification:

1. *$(\mathbb{Z}, -)$ (Integers under Subtraction):*
   - *Closure (G0):* For any $a, b \in \mathbb{Z}$, $a - b \in \mathbb{Z}$. $\checkmark$
   - *Associativity (G1):* $(5 - 3) - 2 = 2 - 2 = 0$, but $5 - (3 - 2) = 5 - 1 = 4 \neq 0$. Fails! $\times$
   - *Conclusion:* $(\mathbb{Z}, -)$ is a *Magma*, but *not a Semigroup*.
2. *$(2\mathbb{Z}, \times)$ (Even Integers under Multiplication):*
   - *Closure (G0):* $(2k)(2m) = 2(2km) \in 2\mathbb{Z}$. $\checkmark$
   - *Associativity (G1):* Ordinary multiplication is associative. $\checkmark$
   - *Identity (G2):* The candidate identity is $1$, but $1 \notin 2\mathbb{Z}$. No even integer $e$ satisfies $a \cdot e = a$ for all $a \in 2\mathbb{Z}$ (e.g. $2e = 2 \implies e = 1 \notin 2\mathbb{Z}$). Fails! $\times$
   - *Conclusion:* $(2\mathbb{Z}, \times)$ is a *Semigroup*, but *not a Monoid*.
3. *$(\mathbb{R} \setminus \{-1\}, \star)$ with $x \star y = x + y + x y$:*
   - *Closure:* If $x, y \neq -1$, then $x \star y = (x+1)(y+1) - 1 \neq -1$ because $(x+1)(y+1) \neq 0$. $\checkmark$
   - *Associativity:* $(x \star y) \star z = (x+1)(y+1)(z+1) - 1 = x \star (y \star z)$. $\checkmark$
   - *Identity:* Find $e$: $x \star e = x \implies x + e + xe = x \implies e(1 + x) = 0 \implies e = 0$. $\checkmark$
   - *Inverse:* Find $x^{-1}$: $x \star x^{-1} = 0 \implies (x+1)(x^{-1}+1) - 1 = 0 \implies x^{-1} = \frac{1}{x+1} - 1 = -\frac{x}{x+1}$.
     Since $x \neq -1$, $x^{-1}$ is well-defined and $x^{-1} \neq -1$. $\checkmark$
   - *Commutativity:* $x \star y = x + y + xy = y + x + yx = y \star x$. $\checkmark$
   - *Conclusion:* $(\mathbb{R} \setminus \{-1\}, \star)$ is an *Abelian Group*!

** Fundamental Properties of Groups
- *Uniqueness of Identity and Inverses:* Follows directly from cancellation:
  $$ a \cdot b = a \cdot c \implies b = c \quad (\text{left}), \qquad b \cdot a = c \cdot a \implies b = c \quad (\text{right}) $$
- *Shoe-Sock Theorem:* The inverse of a product reverses order:
  $$ (a \cdot b)^{-1} = b^{-1} \cdot a^{-1} $$
  *(Intuition: To undo putting on socks then shoes, you must first remove shoes, then remove socks!)*

* Realizations of Abstract Groups via Homomorphisms (§2.1, §2.3)

** Abstract Groups vs. Concrete Realizations
An *abstract group* is defined entirely by its multiplication rules (its Cayley table), independent of what the objects are.
A *realization* is a concrete physical or mathematical system (matrices, rotations, permutations) that obeys those rules.

** Homomorphisms and Isomorphisms
A map $\phi : G \to H$ is a *homomorphism* if it preserves the group operation:
$$ \phi(a \cdot b) = \phi(a) \ast \phi(b), \quad \forall a, b \in G $$
- *Kernel:* $\mathrm{Ker}(\phi) = \{g \in G \mid \phi(g) = e_H\}$. Measures how many elements collapse to the identity.
- *Isomorphism:* A bijective homomorphism ($G \cong H$). Two isomorphic groups are algebraically identical.

*** Worked Example: The Determinant Homomorphism
Consider the map $\phi : \mathrm{GL}(n, \mathbb{R}) \to (\mathbb{R} \setminus \{0\}, \times)$ defined by:
$$ \phi(M) = \det(M) $$
1. *Homomorphism property:* By the determinant multiplication theorem:
   $$ \phi(M_1 M_2) = \det(M_1 M_2) = \det(M_1) \cdot \det(M_2) = \phi(M_1) \phi(M_2) \quad \checkmark $$
2. *Kernel:*
   $$ \mathrm{Ker}(\phi) = \{M \in \mathrm{GL}(n, \mathbb{R}) \mid \det(M) = 1\} = \mathrm{SL}(n, \mathbb{R}) $$
   The kernel is precisely the *Special Linear Group* $\mathrm{SL}(n, \mathbb{R})$!
   Since the kernel of any homomorphism is a normal subgroup, this immediately proves $\mathrm{SL}(n, \mathbb{R}) \triangleleft \mathrm{GL}(n, \mathbb{R})$!

* Symmetric Groups $S_n$ and Cyclic Groups $\mathbb{Z}_n$ (§2.4)

** The Symmetric Group $S_n$
The symmetric group $S_n$ consists of all permutations of $n$ objects under function composition:
- Order: $|S_n| = n!$.
- *Cycle Notation:* $(a_1 \, a_2 \, \dots \, a_k)$ sends $a_1 \mapsto a_2 \mapsto \dots \mapsto a_k \mapsto a_1$.
- *Non-Abelian:* For $n \ge 3$, permutations do *not* commute in general.

*** Worked Example: Cycle Multiplication in $S_3$
Let $\pi_1 = (1 \, 2)$ (swap 1 and 2) and $\pi_2 = (2 \, 3)$ (swap 2 and 3).
Recall: permutations compose *from right to left* ($\pi_1 \pi_2(x) = \pi_1(\pi_2(x))$):
- Start with 1: $\pi_2(1) = 1 \implies \pi_1(1) = 2$. (So $1 \mapsto 2$).
- Next with 2: $\pi_2(2) = 3 \implies \pi_1(3) = 3$. (So $2 \mapsto 3$).
- Next with 3: $\pi_2(3) = 2 \implies \pi_1(2) = 1$. (So $3 \mapsto 1$).
Therefore:
$$ (1 \, 2)(2 \, 3) = (1 \, 2 \, 3) $$
Now compute the reverse order $\pi_2 \pi_1 = (2 \, 3)(1 \, 2)$:
- $1 \mapsto 2 \mapsto 3$
- $3 \mapsto 3 \mapsto 2$
- $2 \mapsto 1 \mapsto 1$
Therefore:
$$ (2 \, 3)(1 \, 2) = (1 \, 3 \, 2) \neq (1 \, 2 \, 3) $$
*Conclusion:* $(12)(23) \neq (23)(12)$. $S_3$ is the smallest non-Abelian group!

** Cyclic Groups $\mathbb{Z}_n$ and Roots of Unity
A group is *cyclic* if a single generator $g$ produces all elements: $\mathbb{Z}_n = \{e, g, g^2, \dots, g^{n-1}\}$.
- *Complex Roots of Unity Realization:*
  Let $\omega = e^{2\pi i / n}$. The set $U_n = \{1, \omega, \omega^2, \dots, \omega^{n-1}\}$ under complex multiplication is isomorphic to $\mathbb{Z}_n$.

*** Worked Example: $\mathbb{Z}_4$ as Roots of Unity
For $n = 4$, $\omega = e^{2\pi i / 4} = e^{i\pi/2} = i$.
- Elements: $\{1, i, i^2, i^3\} = \{1, i, -1, -i\}$.
- Geometrically, multiplying by $i$ rotates points in the complex plane counterclockwise by $90^\circ$.
- Group table:
  $i \cdot i = -1$, $i \cdot (-1) = -i$, $i \cdot (-i) = 1$.
  This is a 1-to-1 realization of the abstract cyclic group $\mathbb{Z}_4$.

* Orders of Elements and Groups (§2.1, §2.4)

- *Group Order $|G|$:* Number of elements in $G$.
- *Element Order $\mathrm{ord}(g)$:* Smallest integer $m \ge 1$ such that $g^m = e$.
  - If no such $m$ exists, $g$ has infinite order.
  - *Key Property:* $g^k = e \iff \mathrm{ord}(g) \mid k$.

*** Worked Example: Orders in $\mathbb{Z}_6$ vs. $S_3$ (Both have Order 6)
Both $\mathbb{Z}_6$ and $S_3$ have 6 elements ($|G| = 6$). Let us examine the orders of their elements:
1. *Cyclic Group $\mathbb{Z}_6 = \{0, 1, 2, 3, 4, 5\}$ (under addition modulo 6):*
   - $\mathrm{ord}(0) = 1$
   - $\mathrm{ord}(1) = 6$ ($1+1+1+1+1+1 \equiv 0$)
   - $\mathrm{ord}(2) = 3$ ($2+2+2 \equiv 0$)
   - $\mathrm{ord}(3) = 2$ ($3+3 \equiv 0$)
   - $\mathrm{ord}(4) = 3$ ($4+4+4 \equiv 0$)
   - $\mathrm{ord}(5) = 6$
   *Element orders present: $\{1, 2, 3, 6\}$. There is an element of order 6.*
2. *Symmetric Group $S_3 = \{e, (12), (13), (23), (123), (132)\}$:*
   - $\mathrm{ord}(e) = 1$
   - $\mathrm{ord}((12)) = \mathrm{ord}((13)) = \mathrm{ord}((23)) = 2$ (transpositions squared give identity)
   - $\mathrm{ord}((123)) = \mathrm{ord}((132)) = 3$
   *Element orders present: $\{1, 2, 3\}$. The maximum element order is 3!*
*Takeaway:* Even though $|\mathbb{Z}_6| = |S_3| = 6$, they cannot be isomorphic because $S_3$ has no element of order 6 and is non-Abelian!

* Small Finite Groups ($N \le 8$) and Direct Products (§2.4)

** External Direct Product $G_1 \times G_2$
Given groups $G_1$ and $G_2$, their direct product is the Cartesian product $G_1 \times G_2$ with component-wise multiplication:
$$ (g_1, g_2) \cdot (h_1, h_2) = (g_1 h_1, \, g_2 h_2) $$
- Order: $|G_1 \times G_2| = |G_1| \cdot |G_2|$.
- *Coprime Criterion:* $\mathbb{Z}_m \times \mathbb{Z}_n \cong \mathbb{Z}_{mn} \iff \gcd(m, n) = 1$.
  - /Example:/ $\mathbb{Z}_2 \times \mathbb{Z}_3 \cong \mathbb{Z}_6$ (since $\gcd(2, 3) = 1$).
  - /Example:/ $\mathbb{Z}_2 \times \mathbb{Z}_2 \not\cong \mathbb{Z}_4$ (since $\gcd(2, 2) = 2 \neq 1$).

** The Klein Four-Group $V_4 \cong \mathbb{Z}_2 \times \mathbb{Z}_2$
The smallest non-cyclic group is $V_4 = \{e, a, b, c\}$:
- Every non-identity element has order 2: $a^2 = b^2 = c^2 = e$.
- $a b = ba = c, \quad b c = cb = a, \quad c a = ac = b$.

*** Worked Example: Physical Realization of $V_4$ (Rectangle Symmetries)
Consider a non-square rectangle centered at the origin in $\mathbb{R}^2$:
- $e$: Identity (do nothing).
- $h$: Horizontal reflection across the $x$-axis ($y \mapsto -y$): $h^2 = e$.
- $v$: Vertical reflection across the $y$-axis ($x \mapsto -x$): $v^2 = e$.
- $r$: Rotation by $180^\circ$ ($x \mapsto -x, y \mapsto -y$): $r = h v = v h$, $r^2 = e$.
Cayley table:
| $\cdot$ | $e$ | $h$ | $v$ | $r$ |
|---+---+---+---+---|
| $e$ | $e$ | $h$ | $v$ | $r$ |
| $h$ | $h$ | $e$ | $r$ | $v$ |
| $v$ | $v$ | $r$ | $e$ | $h$ |
| $r$ | $r$ | $v$ | $h$ | $e$ |
This table is symmetric across the main diagonal (Abelian), and every diagonal entry is $e$ ($g^2 = e$).

* Cayley Tables and the Rearrangement Theorem (§2.1)

#+begin_quote
*Theorem (Rearrangement Theorem):*
In the Cayley table of any group, every element appears *exactly once in every row* and *exactly once in every column* (Latin Square property).
#+end_quote
*Why?* If an element $y$ appeared twice in row $g$, then $g \cdot x_1 = y$ and $g \cdot x_2 = y \implies g x_1 = g x_2 \implies x_1 = x_2$ (by left cancellation). Thus all entries in a row must be distinct!

* Dihedral Groups $D_n$ and Symmetries of Regular Polygons (§2.4)

The dihedral group $D_n$ is the symmetry group of a regular $n$-sided polygon in $\mathbb{R}^2$.
- *Order:* $|D_n| = 2n$ ($n$ rotations + $n$ reflections).
- *Generators and Relations Presentation:*
  $$ D_n = \langle a, b \mid a^n = e, \quad b^2 = e, \quad bab = a^{-1} \iff ba = a^{n-1}b \rangle $$
  where $a$ is rotation by $2\pi/n$ and $b$ is reflection.

*** Worked Example: Symmetries of a Square ($D_4$)
A square has $n = 4$ vertices, so $|D_4| = 2 \times 4 = 8$ elements.
- Rotation generator: $a$ (counterclockwise rotation by $90^\circ$, order 4).
- Reflection generator: $b$ (horizontal reflection, order 2).
- The 8 elements in normal form $a^k b^m$ ($k \in \{0, 1, 2, 3\}, m \in \{0, 1\}$):
  - *4 Rotations:* $e$ ($0^\circ$), $a$ ($90^\circ$), $a^2$ ($180^\circ$), $a^3$ ($270^\circ$).
  - *4 Reflections:* $b$, $ab$, $a^2b$, $a^3b$.
- Commutation rule: $ba = a^3 b$ (conjugating rotation reverses its direction: $bab = a^{-1} = a^3$).
- Is $D_4$ Abelian? $ba = a^3b \neq ab$. No! Dihedral groups $D_n$ are non-Abelian for all $n \ge 3$.

* Continuous and Discrete Spherical Symmetries on $S^2$

Consider the 2-sphere $S^2 = \{(x, y, z) \in \mathbb{R}^3 \mid x^2 + y^2 + z^2 = 1\}$ with full isometry group $\mathrm{O}(3)$.
What subgroup of $\mathrm{O}(3)$ preserves two marked points $p_1, p_2 \in S^2$?

*** Worked Example: Antipodal vs. Non-Antipodal Marked Points
1. *Antipodal Points ($p_2 = -p_1$, North & South Poles):*
   - *Distinguishable points:* Must fix the $z$-axis pointwise $\implies$ Continuous 2D rotations around the $z$-axis plus vertical reflections:
     $$ G = \mathrm{O}(2) \quad (\text{continuous 1D Lie group}) $$
   - *Indistinguishable points:* Points can also be swapped ($p_1 \leftrightarrow p_2$) via horizontal reflection $z \mapsto -z$ or $\pi$-rotation around an equatorial axis:
     $$ G = \mathrm{O}(2) \times \mathbb{Z}_2 \cong \mathrm{D}_{\infty h} $$
2. *Non-Antipodal Points ($p_1 \neq \pm p_2$):*
   $p_1$ and $p_2$ are separated by an angle $\theta \in (0, \pi)$ and span a unique plane $\Pi_{12}$ through the origin.
   - *Distinguishable points:* Any rotation fixing two non-collinear vectors is the identity. The only non-trivial isometry is reflection across the plane $\Pi_{12}$:
     $$ G = \{e, \sigma_{\Pi}\} \cong \mathbb{Z}_2 \quad (\text{discrete, order 2}) $$
   - *Indistinguishable points:* Points can also be swapped by a $180^\circ$ rotation about the angle bisector in $\Pi_{12}$:
     $$ G = \{e, \, \sigma_{\Pi}, \, R_\pi, \, \sigma_{\perp}\} \cong \mathbb{Z}_2 \times \mathbb{Z}_2 = V_4 \quad (\text{discrete Klein four-group, order 4}) $$
