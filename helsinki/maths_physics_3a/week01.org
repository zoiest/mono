* 1. The Algebraic Hierarchy of Sets with Binary Operations (§2.1)

In physics, symmetries form sets equipped with a rule of combination (composition of transformations). The mathematical framework formalizes this through an algebraic hierarchy based on four core axioms.

Let $S$ be a non-empty set and $\cdot : S \times S \to S$ a binary operation that maps each ordered pair $(a, b) \in S \times S$ to a unique element $a \cdot b \in S$.

** 1.1 Axioms of Algebraic Structures

#+caption: The Four Core Axioms
#+begin_quote
- *(G0) Closure (Internal Operation):*
  $$ \forall a, b \in S, \quad a \cdot b \in S $$
- *(G1) Associativity:*
  $$ \forall a, b, c \in S, \quad (a \cdot b) \cdot c = a \cdot (b \cdot c) $$
- *(G2) Identity Element:*
  $$ \exists e \in S \quad \text{such that} \quad \forall a \in S, \quad a \cdot e = e \cdot a = a $$
- *(G3) Inverse Element:*
  $$ \forall a \in S, \quad \exists a^{-1} \in S \quad \text{such that} \quad a \cdot a^{-1} = a^{-1} \cdot a = e $$
- *(AG4) Commutativity (Abelian Property):*
  $$ \forall a, b \in S, \quad a \cdot b = b \cdot a $$
#+end_quote

** 1.2 Hierarchy of Structures

By successively adding axioms, we construct a hierarchy:
$$ \text{Magma} \xrightarrow{+\text{G1}} \text{Semigroup} \xrightarrow{+\text{G2}} \text{Monoid} \xrightarrow{+\text{G3}} \text{Group} \xrightarrow{+\text{AG4}} \text{Abelian Group} $$

1. *Magma:* Satisfies *(G0)* only. A set with an internal binary operation.
   - /Example:/ $(\mathbb{R}, \star)$ with $x \star y = x^y$ is *not* a magma on all of $\mathbb{R}$ because $0^0$ or $(-2)^{1/2}$ are undefined or complex; restricted to $\mathbb{R}_{>0}$, it is a magma, but non-associative: $(2^3)^2 = 8^2 = 64 \neq 2^{(3^2)} = 2^9 = 512$.
2. *Semigroup:* Satisfies *(G0)* and *(G1)* (Closure + Associativity).
   - /Example:/ $(\mathbb{N}, +)$ where $\mathbb{N} = \{1, 2, 3, \dots\}$ (without 0). Addition is associative, but there is no identity in $\mathbb{N}$.
3. *Monoid:* Satisfies *(G0)*, *(G1)*, and *(G2)* (Closure + Associativity + Identity).
   - /Example:/ $(\mathbb{N} \cup \{0\}, +)$ with identity $0$.
   - /Example:/ $(\mathbb{N}, \gcd)$ with identity (if defined on $\mathbb{N} \cup \{0\}$, $\gcd(n, 0) = n$). However, elements do not possess inverses.
4. *Group:* Satisfies *(G0)*, *(G1)*, *(G2)*, and *(G3)*.
   - /Example:/ $(\mathbb{Z}, +)$ with identity $0$ and inverse $-n$.
   - /Example:/ $(\mathbb{Q} \setminus \{0\}, \cdot)$ with identity $1$ and inverse $1/q$.
5. *Abelian (Commutative) Group:* A group satisfying *(AG4)*.
   - /Example:/ $(\mathbb{R}^n, +)$, cyclic groups $\mathbb{Z}_n$, $(\mathbb{C} \setminus \{0\}, \cdot)$.
   - /Non-Abelian Groups:/ General linear group $\mathrm{GL}(n, \mathbb{R})$ for $n \ge 2$, permutation groups $S_n$ for $n \ge 3$.

** 1.3 Fundamental Properties of Groups

From the group axioms, several foundational theorems follow immediately:
- *Uniqueness of Identity:* If $e, e' \in G$ both satisfy (G2), then $e = e \cdot e' = e'$.
- *Uniqueness of Inverses:* If $b, c$ are both inverses of $a$, then $b = b \cdot e = b \cdot (a \cdot c) = (b \cdot a) \cdot c = e \cdot c = c$.
- *Double Inverse and Product Inverse:*
  $$ (a^{-1})^{-1} = a, \qquad (a \cdot b)^{-1} = b^{-1} \cdot a^{-1} $$
- *Cancellation Laws:*
  $$ a \cdot b = a \cdot c \implies b = c \quad (\text{left}), \qquad b \cdot a = c \cdot a \implies b = c \quad (\text{right}) $$

* 2. Realizations of Abstract Groups via Homomorphisms (§2.1, §2.3)

** 2.1 Abstract Groups vs. Concrete Realizations
An *abstract group* is defined entirely by its set of elements and its multiplication table (or presentation), independent of what the elements physically represent.
A *realization* (or representation) of an abstract group $G$ is a concrete group $H$ (whose elements are matrices, geometric motions, permutations, or differential operators) together with a structure-preserving map.

** 2.2 Group Homomorphisms and Isomorphisms
Let $(G, \cdot)$ and $(H, \ast)$ be groups.
- *Homomorphism:* A mapping $\phi : G \to H$ such that:
  $$ \phi(a \cdot b) = \phi(a) \ast \phi(b), \quad \forall a, b \in G $$
  Immediate consequences:
  1. $\phi(e_G) = e_H$
  2. $\phi(a^{-1}) = [\phi(a)]^{-1}$
- *Isomorphism:* A bijective homomorphism ($\phi$ is both injective and surjective).
  - If an isomorphism exists, $G$ and $H$ are called *isomorphic*, denoted $G \cong H$.
  - Isomorphic groups have identical algebraic structure; they differ only in the labeling of their elements.
- *Kernel and Image:*
  $$ \mathrm{Ker}(\phi) = \{g \in G \mid \phi(g) = e_H\} \triangleleft G, \qquad \mathrm{Im}(\phi) = \{\phi(g) \mid g \in G\} \le H $$
  The homomorphism is injective (a *monomorphism* or *faithful realization*) if and only if $\mathrm{Ker}(\phi) = \{e_G\}$.

* 3. Symmetric Groups $S_n$ and Cyclic Groups $\mathbb{Z}_n$ (§2.4)

** 3.1 The Symmetric Group $S_n$
Let $X = \{1, 2, \dots, n\}$ be a finite set.
- *Permutation:* A bijection $\pi : X \to X$.
- *Symmetric Group $S_n$:* The set of all permutations of $n$ elements, equipped with function composition:
  $$ (\pi_1 \circ \pi_2)(k) = \pi_1(\pi_2(k)) $$
- *Order:* The number of elements in $S_n$ is:
  $$ |S_n| = n! $$
- *Cauchy Two-Line Notation:*
  $$ \pi = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 & \cdots & n \\ \pi(1) & \pi(2) & \cdots & \pi(n) \end{pmatrix} $$
- *Cycle Notation:* A cycle $(a_1 \, a_2 \, \dots \, a_k)$ maps $a_1 \mapsto a_2 \mapsto \dots \mapsto a_k \mapsto a_1$, leaving all other elements invariant.
  - Every permutation in $S_n$ decomposes uniquely into a product of disjoint cycles.
  - Non-Abelian for all $n \ge 3$ (e.g., in $S_3$, $(12)(23) = (123) \neq (23)(12) = (132)$).

** 3.2 Cyclic Groups $\mathbb{Z}_n$
A group $G$ is *cyclic* if it can be generated by a single element $g \in G$:
$$ G = \langle g \rangle = \{g^k \mid k \in \mathbb{Z}\} $$
- *Finite Cyclic Group $\mathbb{Z}_n$:* If $g^n = e$ and $n$ is the smallest positive integer with this property:
  $$ \mathbb{Z}_n = \{e, g, g^2, \dots, g^{n-1}\} \cong (\mathbb{Z} / n\mathbb{Z}, +) $$
- *Properties:*
  1. Every cyclic group is *Abelian*: $g^p g^q = g^{p+q} = g^q g^p$.
  2. Any subgroup of a cyclic group is cyclic.
- *Complex Roots of Unity Realization:*
  Let $\omega = e^{2\pi i / n} \in \mathbb{C}$. The set of $n$-th roots of unity:
  $$ U_n = \{1, \omega, \omega^2, \dots, \omega^{n-1}\} $$
  forms a group under ordinary complex multiplication that is isomorphic to $\mathbb{Z}_n$ via $\phi(g^k) = \omega^k = e^{2\pi i k / n}$.

* 4. Orders of Elements and Groups (§2.1, §2.4)

** 4.1 Group Order $|G|$
The *order of a group*, denoted $|G|$ or $\mathrm{ord}(G)$, is the cardinality of the underlying set:
- If $|G| < \infty$, $G$ is a *finite group*.
- If $|G| = \infty$, $G$ is an *infinite group* (discrete, e.g. $\mathbb{Z}$, or continuous, e.g. $\mathrm{SO}(2)$).

** 4.2 Element Order $\mathrm{ord}(g)$
Let $g \in G$.
- The *order of an element* $g$, denoted $\mathrm{ord}(g)$, is the smallest positive integer $m \in \mathbb{Z}^+$ such that:
  $$ g^m = e $$
- If no such positive integer exists, $g$ is said to have *infinite order*.
- *Subgroup generated by $g$:* The set $\langle g \rangle = \{g^k \mid k \in \mathbb{Z}\}$ forms a cyclic subgroup of $G$ with $|\langle g \rangle| = \mathrm{ord}(g)$.
- *Divisibility Property:* For any $k \in \mathbb{Z}$, $g^k = e \iff \mathrm{ord}(g) \mid k$.

* 5. Small Finite Groups ($N \le 8$) and Direct Products (§2.4)

** 5.1 External Direct Product $G_1 \times G_2$
Given two groups $(G_1, \cdot_1)$ and $(G_2, \cdot_2)$:
- The *direct product* $G_1 \times G_2$ is the Cartesian product set $G_1 \times G_2$ equipped with component-wise multiplication:
  $$ (g_1, g_2) \cdot (h_1, h_2) = (g_1 \cdot_1 h_1, \, g_2 \cdot_2 h_2) $$
- Identity: $(e_1, e_2)$.
- Inverse: $(g_1, g_2)^{-1} = (g_1^{-1}, g_2^{-1})$.
- Order: $|G_1 \times G_2| = |G_1| \cdot |G_2|$.
- Commutativity: $G_1 \times G_2$ is Abelian if and only if both $G_1$ and $G_2$ are Abelian.

** 5.2 Classification of Small Finite Groups

| Order $N$ | Group $G$ | Structure / Isomorphism | Type | Notes |
|---+---+---+---+---|

| 2 | $\mathbb{Z}_2$ | Cyclic group | Abelian | Prime order |
| 3 | $\mathbb{Z}_3$ | Cyclic group | Abelian | Prime order |
| 4 | $\mathbb{Z}_4$ | Cyclic group | Abelian | Generated by element of order 4 |
| 4 | $V_4$ | Klein four-group $\mathbb{Z}_2 \times \mathbb{Z}_2$ | Abelian | All non-identity elements have order 2 |
| 5 | $\mathbb{Z}_5$ | Cyclic group | Abelian | Prime order |
| 6 | $\mathbb{Z}_6$ | $\mathbb{Z}_2 \times \mathbb{Z}_3$ | Abelian | Cyclic ($\gcd(2,3)=1$) |

** 5.3 The Klein Four-Group $V_4$
The Klein four-group $V_4 \cong \mathbb{Z}_2 \times \mathbb{Z}_2 = \{e, a, b, c\}$ is the smallest non-cyclic group:
- Every non-identity element satisfies:
  $$ a^2 = b^2 = c^2 = e $$
- Pairwise products satisfy:
  $$ ab = ba = c, \quad bc = cb = a, \quad ca = ac = b $$
- Physical realization: Symmetries of a non-square rectangle (horizontal reflection $h$, vertical reflection $v$, $\pi$-rotation $r = hv$).

* 6. Cayley Tables and the Rearrangement Theorem (§2.1)

** 6.1 Cayley Multiplication Table
For a finite group $G = \{g_1, g_2, \dots, g_n\}$, the group structure is displayed in an $n \times n$ table where the cell at row $i$ and column $j$ contains $g_i \cdot g_j$.

** 6.2 The Rearrangement Theorem (Latin Square Property)
#+begin_quote
*Theorem (Rearrangement Theorem):*
In the Cayley table of a finite group $G$, each group element appears *exactly once in every row* and *exactly once in every column*.
#+end_quote
*Proof:*
Consider row $g \in G$. The entries in row $g$ are $\{g \cdot x \mid x \in G\}$.
1. If $g \cdot x_1 = g \cdot x_2$, multiplying on the left by $g^{-1}$ gives $x_1 = x_2$. Thus all elements in the row are distinct.
2. Since $|G| = n$ and there are $n$ distinct entries, the set $\{g \cdot x \mid x \in G\}$ must equal $G$.
The identical argument using right cancellation applies to each column $\{x \cdot g \mid x \in G\}$. $\blacksquare$

Consequently, every group Cayley table is a *Latin square*. (The converse is not true in general, because a Latin square need not satisfy associativity).

* 7. Dihedral Groups $D_n$ and Symmetries of Regular Polygons (§2.4)

** 7.1 Geometric Definition
The dihedral group $D_n$ is the symmetry group (isometry group) of a regular $n$-sided polygon in the Euclidean plane $\mathbb{R}^2$.
- The polygon is preserved by:
  1. *Rotations:* Counterclockwise rotations around the center by multiples of $2\pi/n$:
     $$ r_k = \text{rot}\left(\frac{2\pi k}{n}\right), \quad k = 0, 1, \dots, n-1 $$
  2. *Reflections:* Reflections across symmetry axes passing through the center.

** 7.2 Proof of Group Order $|D_n| = 2n$
- Label the vertices cyclically as $1, 2, \dots, n$.
- Any symmetry is an isometry of $\mathbb{R}^2$ preserving the polygon:
  1. Vertex $1$ must be mapped to one of the $n$ vertices: $n$ choices.
  2. Because distance between adjacent vertices must be preserved, adjacent vertex $2$ must map to one of the two neighbors of the image of vertex $1$: $2$ choices.
  3. Once the images of vertex $1$ and vertex $2$ are determined, the images of all remaining vertices $3, \dots, n$ are uniquely fixed by edge-length preservation.
- Hence, the total number of symmetries cannot exceed:
  $$ |D_n| \le n \times 2 = 2n $$
- Since we can explicitly construct $n$ rotations $r^k$ ($0 \le k \le n-1$) and $n$ reflections $s r^k$ ($0 \le k \le n-1$), the group has *exactly* $2n$ elements:
  $$ |D_n| = 2n $$

** 7.3 Generators and Relations Presentation
The dihedral group $D_n$ is generated by a rotation $a$ (by $2\pi/n$) and a reflection $b$:
$$ D_n = \langle a, b \mid a^n = e, \quad b^2 = e, \quad bab = a^{-1} \rangle $$
- The reflection-conjugation relation $bab = a^{-1}$ is equivalent to:
  $$ ba = a^{-1} b = a^{n-1} b $$
- All $2n$ elements can be written uniquely in normal form:
  $$ D_n = \{ a^k b^m \mid 0 \le k \le n-1, \, m \in \{0, 1\} \} $$

** 7.4 When is $D_n$ Abelian?
- If $D_n$ is Abelian, then $ba = ab$.
- From the dihedral relation, $ba = a^{-1}b$.
- Therefore, $ab = a^{-1}b \implies a = a^{-1} \implies a^2 = e$.
- Since $a$ has order $n$, $a^2 = e$ requires $n \mid 2$.
- Thus, $D_n$ is Abelian *if and only if* $n = 1$ or $n = 2$:
  - $n = 1$: $D_1 \cong \mathbb{Z}_2$ (bilateral symmetry of a 2-gon / isosceles segment).
  - $n = 2$: $D_2 \cong \mathbb{Z}_2 \times \mathbb{Z}_2 = V_4$ (symmetries of a rectangle).
  - For all $n \ge 3$: $a \neq a^{-1}$, so $ba \neq ab$, and $D_n$ is *strictly non-Abelian*.

* 8. Continuous and Discrete Symmetry Groups on Spheres

In physical systems (e.g., central potentials, marked spherical shells), one investigates the subgroups of the orthogonal group $\mathrm{O}(3)$ that preserve designated geometric features.

** 8.1 The 2-Sphere $S^2$ and $\mathrm{O}(3)$
The 2-sphere is defined by:
$$ S^2 = \{(x, y, z) \in \mathbb{R}^3 \mid x^2 + y^2 + z^2 = 1\} $$
- The full continuous symmetry group leaving $S^2$ invariant is the full orthogonal group $\mathrm{O}(3) = \mathrm{SO}(3) \times \mathbb{Z}_2$ (rotations and spatial inversion $\vec{x} \mapsto -\vec{x}$).
- Dimension of the group manifold: $\dim \mathrm{O}(3) = \frac{3(3-1)}{2} = 3$.

** 8.2 Invariance Groups with Marked Points
When points $p_1, p_2 \in S^2$ are marked on the sphere, the symmetry group is restricted to the subgroup of $\mathrm{O}(3)$ preserving the set $\{p_1, p_2\}$.

*** Case 1: Antipodal Points ($p_2 = -p_1$)
Rotate coordinates so $p_1 = (0, 0, 1)$ (North Pole) and $p_2 = (0, 0, -1)$ (South Pole).
- *Distinguishable Points:* Any symmetry must fix $p_1$ and $p_2$ individually:
  - Rotations about the $z$-axis by any angle $\theta \in [0, 2\pi)$: $\mathrm{SO}(2)$.
  - Reflections across any vertical plane containing the $z$-axis: adding these gives the full 1D orthogonal group $\mathrm{O}(2)$.
  - Symmetries: $\mathrm{O}(2)$ (continuous, 1-parameter Lie group).
- *Indistinguishable Points:* The symmetry operation can either fix each pole or swap $p_1 \leftrightarrow p_2$:
  - The pole-swapping transformation is given by a $\pi$-rotation about any axis in the equatorial $xy$-plane (or spatial inversion $\vec{x} \mapsto -\vec{x}$).
  - Symmetries: $\mathrm{O}(2) \times \mathbb{Z}_2$ (or $\mathrm{O}(2) \rtimes \mathbb{Z}_2 \cong \mathrm{D}_{\infty h}$).

*** Case 2: Non-Antipodal Points ($p_1 \neq \pm p_2$)
Let $\theta \in (0, \pi)$ be the geodesic angular separation between $p_1$ and $p_2$.
There is a unique great circle passing through $p_1$ and $p_2$, defining a unique plane $\Pi_{12}$ through the origin.
- *Distinguishable Points:*
  - No continuous rotation can preserve two non-antipodal fixed points (as rotations have only a single 1D axis).
  - The only non-trivial isometry of $\mathbb{R}^3$ fixing $p_1$ and $p_2$ individually is reflection $\sigma$ across the plane $\Pi_{12}$.
  - Symmetries: Discrete group $\{e, \sigma\} \cong \mathbb{Z}_2$.
- *Indistinguishable Points:*
  - In addition to $\sigma$, a symmetry can swap $p_1 \leftrightarrow p_2$.
  - The rotation $R_\pi$ by $\pi$ around the angle bisector of $\vec{p}_1$ and $\vec{p}_2$ swaps the two points.
  - The composition $\sigma' = \sigma \circ R_\pi$ is reflection across the plane perpendicular to the segment $p_1 p_2$ bisecting it.
  - Symmetries: $\{e, \sigma, R_\pi, \sigma'\} \cong \mathbb{Z}_2 \times \mathbb{Z}_2 \cong V_4$ (discrete Klein four-group).
