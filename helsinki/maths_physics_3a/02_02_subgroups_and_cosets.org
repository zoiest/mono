* Subgroups

** Formal Definition
Let $(G, \cdot)$ be a group. A subset $H \subseteq G$ is called a *subgroup* of $G$ (denoted $H \le G$, or $H < G$ if proper) if $H$ forms a group under the restricted group operation of $G$.
Specifically:
1. $e \in H$ (the identity of $G$ belongs to $H$).
2. For all $h_1, h_2 \in H$, $h_1 \cdot h_2 \in H$ (closure under multiplication).
3. For all $h \in H$, $h^{-1} \in H$ (closure under inversion).

** Subgroup Criteria
Instead of checking all group axioms, subgroups are verified by compact criteria:

#+begin_quote
*Theorem (One-Step Subgroup Criterion):*
A non-empty subset $H \subseteq G$ is a subgroup of $G$ if and only if:
$$ \forall h_1, h_2 \in H, \quad h_1 h_2^{-1} \in H $$
#+end_quote
*Proof:*
- $(\implies)$ If $H \le G$, then $h_2 \in H \implies h_2^{-1} \in H$, and by closure $h_1 h_2^{-1} \in H$.
- $(\impliedby)$ Assume $h_1 h_2^{-1} \in H$ for all $h_1, h_2 \in H$:
  1. Since $H \neq \emptyset$, pick $h \in H$. Setting $h_1 = h$ and $h_2 = h$ gives $h h^{-1} = e \in H$.
  2. Setting $h_1 = e$ and $h_2 = h$ gives $e h^{-1} = h^{-1} \in H$ (closure under inverses).
  3. For any $a, b \in H$, since $b^{-1} \in H$, setting $h_1 = a$ and $h_2 = b^{-1}$ gives $a (b^{-1})^{-1} = ab \in H$ (closure under product).
  Associativity is inherited directly from $G$. Thus $H \le G$. $\blacksquare$

#+begin_quote
*Theorem (Finite Subgroup Criterion):*
Let $H$ be a non-empty *finite* subset of a group $G$. Then $H \le G$ if and only if $H$ is closed under multiplication:
$$ \forall h_1, h_2 \in H, \quad h_1 h_2 \in H $$
#+end_quote
*Proof:*
Let $h \in H$. Consider the set of powers $\{h, h^2, h^3, \dots\}$. By closure, all powers lie in $H$. Since $H$ is finite, there must exist integers $j > k \ge 1$ such that $h^j = h^k$. By cancellation, $h^{j-k} = e \in H$. Since $j - k \ge 1$, if $j - k = 1$ then $h = e \implies h^{-1} = e \in H$. If $j - k > 1$, then $h \cdot h^{j-k-1} = e$, so $h^{-1} = h^{j-k-1} \in H$. Hence $H$ contains inverses and is a subgroup. $\blacksquare$

** Trivial and Proper Subgroups
- *Trivial Subgroups:* Every group $G$ has at least two subgroups:
  1. The identity subgroup $\{e\}$.
  2. The group itself $G$.
  (For the trivial group $Z_1 = \{e\}$, these two coincide.)
- *Proper Subgroups:* Any subgroup $H \le G$ such that $H \neq \{e\}$ and $H \neq G$. For a finite group $G$, $1 < |H| < |G|$.

* The Center of a Group

** Definition
The *center* of a group $G$, denoted $Z(G)$, is the set of all elements that commute with every element of $G$:
$$ Z(G) = \{ z \in G \mid z g = g z, \quad \forall g \in G \} $$

** Key Properties
1. $Z(G)$ is always an *Abelian subgroup* of $G$:
   - Identity $e \in Z(G)$ since $eg = ge = g$.
   - Inverses: If $z \in Z(G)$, $zg = gz \implies g z^{-1} = z^{-1} g$, so $z^{-1} \in Z(G)$.
   - Closure: If $z_1, z_2 \in Z(G)$, $(z_1 z_2) g = z_1 (g z_2) = g (z_1 z_2)$.
2. $Z(G)$ is always a *normal subgroup* of $G$: for all $g \in G$ and $z \in Z(G)$, $g z g^{-1} = z g g^{-1} = z \in Z(G)$.
3. If $G$ is Abelian, then $Z(G) = G$.
4. If $Z(G) = \{e\}$, the group $G$ is said to have a *trivial center* (or to be *centerless*).
   - Examples of centerless groups: $S_3$, $\mathrm{SO}(3)$, $G_2$, $F_4$, $E_8$.
   - Examples with non-trivial center: $D_4$ ($Z \cong \mathbb{Z}_2$), $Q_8$ ($Z \cong \mathbb{Z}_2$), $\mathrm{SU}(N)$ ($Z \cong \mathbb{Z}_N$), $\mathrm{U}(N)$ ($Z \cong \mathrm{U}(1)$).

* Cosets and Equivalence Relations

** Left and Right Cosets
Let $H \le G$ be a subgroup, and let $g \in G$.
- The *left coset* of $H$ containing $g$ is:
  $$ gH = \{ gh \mid h \in H \} $$
- The *right coset* of $H$ containing $g$ is:
  $$ Hg = \{ hg \mid h \in H \} $$

** Partition of the Group into Disjoint Cosets
Define a relation on $G$ with respect to $H$:
$$ x \sim_L y \iff x^{-1}y \in H \iff y \in xH $$
#+begin_quote
*Theorem:* The relation $\sim_L$ is an equivalence relation on $G$:
1. *Reflexive:* $x^{-1}x = e \in H \implies x \sim_L x$.
2. *Symmetric:* $x \sim_L y \implies x^{-1}y \in H \implies (x^{-1}y)^{-1} = y^{-1}x \in H \implies y \sim_L x$.
3. *Transitive:* $x \sim_L y$ and $y \sim_L z \implies x^{-1}y \in H$ and $y^{-1}z \in H$. By closure, $(x^{-1}y)(y^{-1}z) = x^{-1}z \in H \implies x \sim_L z$.
#+end_quote

*Consequence (Theorem 2.1 in text):*
The left cosets $\{gH \mid g \in G\}$ form a partition of $G$ into mutually disjoint subsets:
$$ \text{Either } g_1 H = g_2 H \quad \text{or} \quad g_1 H \cap g_2 H = \emptyset $$
Every element $g \in G$ belongs to exactly one left coset (namely $gH$, since $e \in H \implies g = ge \in gH$).

** Bijection Between $H$ and Any Coset
#+begin_quote
*Theorem:* For any $g \in G$, there is a bijection between $H$ and the left coset $gH$.
#+end_quote
*Proof:*
Define $f_g : H \to gH$ by $f_g(h) = gh$.
1. *Surjective:* By definition, every element in $gH$ is of the form $gh$ for some $h \in H$.
2. *Injective:* Suppose $f_g(h_1) = f_g(h_2) \implies gh_1 = gh_2$. Multiplying on the left by $g^{-1}$ yields $h_1 = h_2$.
Therefore, $f_g$ is a bijection. $\blacksquare$

*Consequence:* All left cosets (and right cosets) have the exact same cardinality:
$$ |gH| = |Hg| = |H| $$

* Lagrange's Theorem

#+begin_quote
*Theorem 2.3 (Lagrange's Theorem):*
If $G$ is a finite group and $H$ is a subgroup of $G$, then the order $|H|$ of the subgroup is a divisor of the order $|G|$ of the group:
$$ |G| = [G : H] \cdot |H| $$
where $[G : H]$ is the *index* of $H$ in $G$ (the number of distinct left cosets).
#+end_quote
*Proof:*
Since the left cosets partition $G$ into $k = [G:H]$ pairwise disjoint subsets, and each coset has cardinality $|H|$:
$$ |G| = \sum_{i=1}^k |g_i H| = \sum_{i=1}^k |H| = k \cdot |H| = [G:H] \cdot |H| $$
Since $|G|$ and $|H|$ are positive integers, $|H|$ divides $|G|$. $\blacksquare$

** Fundamental Corollaries of Lagrange's Theorem

*** Corollary 1: Order of Elements Divides Group Order
For any element $g \in G$, the cyclic subgroup generated by $g$,
$$ \langle g \rangle = \{e, g, g^2, \dots, g^{m-1}\}, \quad m = \mathrm{ord}(g) $$
is a subgroup of $G$ with $|\langle g \rangle| = \mathrm{ord}(g)$.
By Lagrange's theorem, $|\langle g \rangle|$ divides $|G|$.
Therefore:
$$ \mathrm{ord}(g) \text{ divides } |G| $$

*** Corollary 2: Euler-Fermat Type Identity
For any finite group $G$ and any $g \in G$:
$$ g^{|G|} = e $$
*Proof:* Since $\mathrm{ord}(g)$ divides $|G|$, $|G| = k \cdot \mathrm{ord}(g)$ for some $k \in \mathbb{Z}^+$. Thus $g^{|G|} = (g^{\mathrm{ord}(g)})^k = e^k = e$. $\blacksquare$

*** Corollary 3: Groups of Prime Order are Uniquely Cyclic
#+begin_quote
*Corollary:* If $p$ is a prime number, then any group $G$ of order $p$ is isomorphic to the cyclic group $\mathbb{Z}_p$.
#+end_quote
*Proof:*
Pick any element $g \in G$ with $g \neq e$.
Let $m = \mathrm{ord}(g)$. Since $g \neq e$, $m > 1$.
By Corollary 1, $m$ must divide $|G| = p$.
Because $p$ is prime and $m > 1$, we must have $m = p$.
Thus $\langle g \rangle = \{e, g, g^2, \dots, g^{p-1}\}$ contains $p$ distinct elements, so $\langle g \rangle = G$.
Hence $G$ is cyclic and isomorphic to $\mathbb{Z}_p$. $\blacksquare$

* Coset Spaces and Group Actions

The set of left cosets is denoted by the *quotient space* (or coset space):
$$ G/H = \{ gH \mid g \in G \} $$
Similarly, the right coset space is $H \backslash G = \{ Hg \mid g \in G \}$.
- The coset space $G/H$ is a fundamental example of a $G$-space on which $G$ acts transitively via $L_g(g'H) = (gg')H$ (see [[id:0c71e7cb-1a90-4ce5-bc72-38c37afda295][Groups Acting on a Set]]).
- Under what conditions does $G/H$ form a *group*?
  Only when $H$ is a *normal subgroup* ($gH = Hg$ for all $g \in G$). In that case, $G/H$ is the *quotient group* (see [[id:a9605612-c8a4-42ff-8115-a96f70083b3e][Homomorphisms, Isomorphisms, and Quotients]]).
