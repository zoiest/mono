* Financial Securities and State-Dependent Payoffs

** Limitations of Deterministic Valuation
In Chapters 2 and 3, cash flows were assumed to be deterministic constants.
However, most financial contracts yield payoffs that are uncertain (stochastic) and depend on the state of the broader economy.
Examples include:
- Shares of equity in corporations (dividends and terminal share prices are uncertain).
- Defaultable corporate loans and bonds (repayment depends on the solvency of the borrower).
- Derivative contracts (options, swaps, forwards) whose payoff is explicitly conditioned on underlying asset prices.

** Subjective Expectation vs. Market Equilibrium
A naive attempt to value a risky payoff $D$ payable at time $1$ is to compute its discounted mathematical expectation under the physical probability measure $\mathbb{P}$:
$$ V(0) = \frac{\mathbb{E}[D]}{1 + i'} \tag{4.2} $$
where $i'$ denotes the subjective "hurdle rate" or "required return on equity" of a specific investor.
However, Formula (4.2) suffers from major conceptual deficiencies:
1. Different market participants possess different risk preferences and subjective beliefs, yielding divergent values for $i'$.
2. It fails to account for whether the risk in $D$ can be hedged or replicated using other existing assets.
3. It does not prevent market arbitrage.

To overcome these limitations, modern financial economics establishes pricing rules based on *market consistency* and the *absence of arbitrage*.

* Financial Derivatives as Contingent Claims

A *contingent claim* (or derivative) is a financial contract whose payoff at future date $T$ is determined as a function of the price path or terminal price of underlying primary assets.

** Standard Derivative Contracts

1. *Forward Contract:*
   An agreement at time $0$ to buy an underlying asset with price $S(T)$ at maturity $T$ for a pre-determined delivery price $K$.
   - Payoff at maturity:
     $$ X = S(T) - K $$

2. *European Call Option:*
   Gives the holder the right (but not the obligation) to buy the underlying asset at expiration date $T$ for strike price $K$.
   - Payoff at maturity:
     $$ X = \max(S(T) - K, 0) = (S(T) - K)^+ $$

3. *European Put Option:*
   Gives the holder the right (but not the obligation) to sell the underlying asset at expiration date $T$ for strike price $K$.
   - Payoff at maturity:
     $$ X = \max(K - S(T), 0) = (K - S(T))^+ $$

4. *Put-Call Parity:*
   By considering the combined portfolio of holding one call and writing one put with the same strike $K$ and maturity $T$:
   $$ (S(T) - K)^+ - (K - S(T))^+ = S(T) - K $$
   This portfolio exactly replicates a forward contract. Absence of arbitrage requires that their present values match today.

5. *Currency and Commodity Options:*
   Contracts giving the right to exchange foreign currencies or physical commodities at guaranteed future exchange rates.
