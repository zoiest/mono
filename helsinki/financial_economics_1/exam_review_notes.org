* Probability Theory Primer (Exercise-Level Foundation)

/If you haven't taken a formal probability theory course yet, don't worry: everything you need for Financial Economics I can be understood concretely using finite sets, simple averages, and vectors./

** States of Nature and the Sample Space $\Omega$
In finance, future asset prices are uncertain. We model all possible "states of the world" at time $t = 1$ by a finite sample space:
$$ \Omega = \{\omega_1, \omega_2, \dots, \omega_M\} $$
- Each $\omega_m$ represents one specific scenario (e.g., $\omega_1 = \text{"Boom"}$, $\omega_2 = \text{"Normal"}$, $\omega_3 = \text{"Recession"}$).
- Each state has a physical (real-world) probability $\mathbb{P}(\{\omega_m\}) = p_m > 0$.
- Probabilities must sum to $1$:
  $$ \sum_{m=1}^M p_m = p_1 + p_2 + \dots + p_M = 1 $$

** Random Variables as Payoff Vectors
A *random variable* $X$ is simply a rule that assigns a real number $X(\omega_m)$ to each possible state $\omega_m$.
In our finite setting, $X$ is just a column vector of $M$ numbers:
$$ X = \begin{pmatrix} X(\omega_1) \\ X(\omega_2) \\ \vdots \\ X(\omega_M) \end{pmatrix} \in \mathbb{R}^M $$

** Almost Surely (a.s.) and Events of Probability 1

#+begin_quote
*The Plain English Meaning of "Almost Surely":*
When a mathematician or financial economist says a statement holds *"almost surely"* (abbreviated *a.s.*, or in Finnish /melkein varmasti/), they mean:
$$ \mathbb{P}(\text{Statement Holds}) = 1 $$
That is, the statement is true with *100% certainty* (probability 1). Any possible exceptions can only happen on an event with zero probability ($\mathbb{P} = 0$).
#+end_quote

*** The Finite Market Simplification (Crucial for Exams!)
In all one-period and multi-period tree models in this course, we assume the sample space is finite:
$$ \Omega = \{\omega_1, \omega_2, \dots, \omega_M\} \quad \text{with } \mathbb{P}(\{\omega_m\}) > 0 \text{ for all } m = 1, \dots, M $$
Because *every single state $\omega_m$ has strictly positive probability*, there are *no zero-probability states*.
Therefore, throughout this course:
$$ \mathbf{\text{Property holds almost surely (a.s.)}} \iff \mathbf{\text{Property holds in EVERY state } \omega_m \in \Omega!} $$

*** Why do textbooks and exam questions write "a.s." instead of "always"?
1. *Universal Mathematical Rigor:* In advanced probability and continuous-time finance (e.g., Brownian motion, Black-Scholes), individual outcomes have probability $0$. Stating theorems with "almost surely" ensures the results are mathematically sound across both discrete and continuous settings.
2. *Equivalence of Measures ($\mathbb{P} \sim \mathbb{Q}$):* Two probability measures $\mathbb{P}$ and $\mathbb{Q}$ are equivalent if they agree on what is impossible: $\mathbb{P}(A) = 0 \iff \mathbb{Q}(A) = 0$. Hence, an event that holds \(\mathbb{P}\)-almost surely also holds \(\mathbb{Q}\)-almost surely!

** Mathematical Expectation (Expected Value)
The *expected value* $\mathbb{E}[X]$ is the probability-weighted average of the values $X$ can take:
$$ \mathbb{E}[X] = \sum_{m=1}^M \mathbb{P}(\{\omega_m\}) X(\omega_m) = p_1 X(\omega_1) + p_2 X(\omega_2) + \dots + p_M X(\omega_M) $$

  *Crucial Properties of Expectation:*
1. *Linearity:* For any constants $a, b \in \mathbb{R}$ and random variables $X, Y$:
   $$ \mathbb{E}[aX + bY] = a\mathbb{E}[X] + b\mathbb{E}[Y] $$
2. *Expectation of a Constant:* If $c$ is a constant, $\mathbb{E}[c] = c$.
3. *Positivity:* If $X(\omega_m) \ge 0$ for all $m$, then $\mathbb{E}[X] \ge 0$. If in addition $X(\omega_m) > 0$ for at least one state with $p_m > 0$, then $\mathbb{E}[X] > 0$.

** Variance and Covariance (Measuring Risk and Co-movement)
- *Variance* measures the dispersion (squared deviation) around the expected value:
  $$ \operatorname{Var}(X) = \mathbb{E}\left[(X - \mathbb{E}[X])^2\right] = \mathbb{E}[X^2] - (\mathbb{E}[X])^2 $$
  - *Properties:* $\operatorname{Var}(X) \ge 0$. For constant $c$, $\operatorname{Var}(c) = 0$, and $\operatorname{Var}(aX + b) = a^2 \operatorname{Var}(X)$.
- *Covariance* measures how two random variables vary together:
  $$ \operatorname{Cov}(X, Y) = \mathbb{E}\left[(X - \mathbb{E}[X])(Y - \mathbb{E}[Y])\right] = \mathbb{E}[XY] - \mathbb{E}[X]\mathbb{E}[Y] $$
  - *Properties:*
    - $\operatorname{Cov}(X, X) = \operatorname{Var}(X)$.
    - Symmetry: $\operatorname{Cov}(X, Y) = \operatorname{Cov}(Y, X)$.
    - Bilinearity: $\operatorname{Cov}(aX + bY, Z) = a\operatorname{Cov}(X, Z) + b\operatorname{Cov}(Y, Z)$.
    - Variance of a sum: $\operatorname{Var}(aX + bY) = a^2 \operatorname{Var}(X) + b^2 \operatorname{Var}(Y) + 2ab\operatorname{Cov}(X, Y)$.

** Independence
Two random variables $X$ and $Y$ are *independent* if knowledge of one provides no information about the other.
- If $X$ and $Y$ are independent:
  $$ \mathbb{E}[XY] = \mathbb{E}[X] \mathbb{E}[Y] \implies \operatorname{Cov}(X, Y) = 0 $$

** Information Filtrations and Conditional Expectation in Trees
In a multi-period model ($t = 0, 1, \dots, T$), information is revealed gradually over time.
- At time $0$, we only know the starting point (root node).
- At time $1$, nature selects a branch, so we know which sub-group of states we are in.
- A *filtration* $\mathbb{F} = (\mathcal{F}_0, \mathcal{F}_1, \dots, \mathcal{F}_T)$ represents this increasing knowledge:
  $$ \mathcal{F}_0 \subseteq \mathcal{F}_1 \subseteq \dots \subseteq \mathcal{F}_T $$
- *Conditional Expectation $\mathbb{E}[X \mid \mathcal{F}_t]$:*
  At time $t$, given that we have arrived at a specific node/event $A \in \mathcal{F}_t$, what is the best estimate of the future payoff $X$?
  $$ \mathbb{E}[X \mid A] = \sum_{\omega \in A} X(\omega) \mathbb{P}(\{\omega\} \mid A) = \sum_{\omega \in A} X(\omega) \frac{\mathbb{P}(\{\omega\})}{\mathbb{P}(A)} $$
  *Intuition:* $\mathbb{E}[X \mid \mathcal{F}_t]$ is simply the expected value calculated over the remaining possible future branches emanating from your current node at time $t$!

** Martingales: The Mathematical Formalization of a Fair Game

*** Intuition: The "Fair Game"
In economics and probability, a stochastic process models a "fair game" if an investor's expected future wealth—given everything known up to today—is exactly equal to their current wealth. There is neither an expected upward drift (free money) nor an expected downward drift (house edge).

*** Formal Mathematical Definition
Let $(\Omega, \mathcal{F}, \mathbb{P})$ be a probability space equipped with a filtration $\mathbb{F} = (\mathcal{F}_t)_{t=0}^T$.
A real-valued stochastic process $M = (M_t)_{t=0}^T$ is an \((\mathbb{F}, \mathbb{P})\)-*martingale* if it satisfies three axioms:
1. *Adapted:* $M_t$ is \(\mathcal{F}_t\)-measurable for every $t \in \{0, \dots, T\}$. (At time $t$, the value of $M_t$ is fully determined by the information available up to time $t$; no peering into the future).
2. *Integrable:* $\mathbb{E}[|M_t|] < \infty$ for all $t \in \{0, \dots, T\}$ (no infinite payoffs or undefined expectations).
3. *Martingale Property:*
   \begin{equation}
   \mathbb{E}[M_{t+1} \mid \mathcal{F}_t] = M_t \quad \text{for all } t = 0, \dots, T-1
   \end{equation}
   By induction, this implies $\mathbb{E}[M_u \mid \mathcal{F}_t] = M_t$ for any future horizon $u \ge t$.

*** Submartingales and Supermartingales (The "Drift" Conditions)
When a process exhibits an upward or downward bias, it is classified as a sub- or supermartingale:
#+caption: Classification of Martingale Types
#+ATTR_LATEX: :environment tabularx :width \textwidth :align l p{0.32\textwidth} p{0.25\textwidth} X
| Process Type | Defining Mathematical Condition | Expected Drift | Intuitive Analogy |
|--------------+---------------------------------+----------------+-------------------|
| *Martingale* | $\mathbb{E}[M_{t+1} \mid \mathcal{F}_t] = M_t$ | Zero drift | Fair coin toss / No-arbitrage pricing |
| *Submartingale* | $\mathbb{E}[M_{t+1} \mid \mathcal{F}_t] \ge M_t$ | Upward ($\ge 0$) | Stock portfolio earning a positive risk premium |
| *Supermartingale* | $\mathbb{E}[M_{t+1} \mid \mathcal{F}_t] \le M_t$ | Downward ($\le 0$) | Casino gambler's wealth facing a house edge |

#+begin_quote
*Memory Aid for Exams:*
- *Submartingale:* "Sub" rises from the floor $\implies$ drifts *upward* ($\mathbb{E}[M_{t+1}\mid\mathcal{F}_t] \ge M_t$).
- *Supermartingale:* "Super" falls from above $\implies$ drifts *downward* ($\mathbb{E}[M_{t+1}\mid\mathcal{F}_t] \le M_t$).
#+end_quote

*** Three Crucial Martingale Properties for Exam Calculations

1. *Constant Unconditional Expectation:*
   By the Law of Total Expectation (taking the expectation of both sides conditioning on $\mathcal{F}_0$):
   \begin{equation}
   \mathbb{E}[M_t] = \mathbb{E}[M_0] \quad \text{for all } t \in \{0, \dots, T\}
   \end{equation}
   If a process starts at $M_0$, its expected value at *any* future time $t$ remains $M_0$.

2. *The Tower Property (Law of Iterated Conditional Expectations):*
   For any two information levels $\mathcal{F}_s \subseteq \mathcal{F}_t$ (with $s \le t$):
   \begin{equation}
   \mathbb{E}[\mathbb{E}[X \mid \mathcal{F}_t] \mid \mathcal{F}_s] = \mathbb{E}[X \mid \mathcal{F}_s]
   \end{equation}
   *Golden Rule:* When conditioning successively on nested information sets, the *smaller information set always dominates*! (Coarsening finer information back down to $s$ discards the intermediate updates).

3. *Uncorrelated / Unforecastable Increments:*
   Define the one-step martingale increment $\Delta M_{t+1} = M_{t+1} - M_t$. The martingale property is equivalent to:
   \begin{equation}
   \mathbb{E}[\Delta M_{t+1} \mid \mathcal{F}_t] = 0
   \end{equation}
   Future changes have conditional expectation zero. Furthermore, martingale increments across non-overlapping intervals are uncorrelated: $\operatorname{Cov}(\Delta M_{t+1}, \Delta M_{s+1}) = 0$ for $s < t$.

*** Calculating Conditional Expectations and Martingale Verification on Trees
In discrete binomial trees, verifying the martingale property at any node $A \in \mathcal{F}_t$ reduces to a simple weighted sum across outgoing branches:
- Let the node at time $t$ have current value $M_t(A)$.
- Let the two successor branches at time $t+1$ be "Up" (payoff $M_{t+1}^u$) and "Down" (payoff $M_{t+1}^d$), with branch probabilities $q$ and $1-q$.
- The conditional expectation is simply:
  \begin{equation}
  \mathbb{E}[M_{t+1} \mid \mathcal{F}_t](A) = q M_{t+1}^u + (1-q) M_{t+1}^d
  \end{equation}
  The process is a martingale at node $A$ if and only if $q M_{t+1}^u + (1-q) M_{t+1}^d = M_t(A)$.

*** Why Martingales are the Foundation of Financial Economics
Why does financial economics care so deeply about martingales?
- *Physical Reality ($\mathbb{P}$):* Real-world stock prices are *not* martingales. Risk-averse investors demand a positive risk premium, so $\mathbb{E}_\mathbb{P}[S(t+1) \mid \mathcal{F}_t] > (1+i) S(t)$. The stock price has a positive drift (a submartingale).
- *Risk-Neutral World ($\mathbb{Q}$):* The First Fundamental Theorem of Asset Pricing (FTAP) guarantees that absence of arbitrage is equivalent to the existence of an *Equivalent Martingale Measure* $\mathbb{Q}$.
  Under $\mathbb{Q}$, when asset prices are *discounted* by the risk-free rate, all risk premia vanish:
  \begin{equation}
  S_n^*(t) := \frac{S_n(t)}{(1+i)^t} \implies \mathbb{E}_\mathbb{Q}\left[ S_n^*(t+1) \;\middle|\; \mathcal{F}_t \right] = S_n^*(t)
  \end{equation}
  Equivalently:
  \begin{equation}
  S_n(t) = \frac{1}{1+i} \mathbb{E}_\mathbb{Q}[S_n(t+1) \mid \mathcal{F}_t]
  \end{equation}
- *General Asset Pricing & Replicating Wealth:*
  The discounted wealth $V^*(t) = \frac{V(t)}{(1+i)^t}$ of *any* self-financing trading strategy is an \((\mathbb{F}, \mathbb{Q})\)-martingale. This means pricing any derivative payoff $X$ at maturity $T$ is mathematically effortless:
  \begin{equation}
  \frac{V(t)}{(1+i)^t} = \mathbb{E}_\mathbb{Q}\left[ \frac{X}{(1+i)^T} \;\middle|\; \mathcal{F}_t \right] \implies V(t) = (1+i)^{-(T-t)} \mathbb{E}_\mathbb{Q}[X \mid \mathcal{F}_t]
  \end{equation}
  At $t=0$, this gives the universal pricing formula: $V(0) = (1+i)^{-T} \mathbb{E}_\mathbb{Q}[X]$.

* Arbitrage Theory & Fixed Income (Deterministic & Stochastic Rates)

** Universal Definition of Arbitrage
An *arbitrage opportunity* is a financial trading strategy that:
1. Costs non-positive capital to enter at time $0$ ($S(0)\theta \le 0$),
2. Has *zero risk of loss* in every future state ($S(1,\omega)\theta \ge 0$ for all $\omega$),
3. Has a *strictly positive chance of making a profit* ($S(0)\theta < 0$ or $\mathbb{P}(S(1)\theta > 0) > 0$).

#+begin_quote
  *Equivalence:* If a riskless bank deposit exists, receiving cash at time $0$ ($S(0)\theta < 0$) can be deposited into the bank to produce guaranteed positive terminal wealth with zero initial outlay. Thus, both forms of arbitrage are equivalent.
#+end_quote

** Deterministic Interest Rate Model & Present Value
Let $\Delta > 0$ be the period length. Over period $[(j-1)\Delta, j\Delta)$, the annualized rate is $i_j > -1$.
A deposit $C$ accumulates over period $j$ to $C(1+i_j)^\Delta$. Over $n$ periods, it grows to:
$$ C \prod_{k=1}^n (1+i_k)^\Delta $$

*** Theorem 2.1 (Arbitrage-Free Price of Cash Flows)
For a deterministic cash flow $B(\Delta), B(2\Delta), \dots, B(T\Delta)$, the unique arbitrage-free price at time $0$ is:
$$ V(0) = \sum_{j=1}^T \left[\prod_{k=1}^j (1+i_k)\right]^{-\Delta} B(j\Delta) $$

*** Arbitrage Exploitation Strategy on the Exam
- *If Market Price $V > V(0)$ (Overpriced):*
  1. Sell (short) the cash flow at market price $V$, pocketing $+V$.
  2. Create $T$ separate bank deposit accounts: into account $j$, deposit $D_j = [\prod_{k=1}^j (1+i_k)]^{-\Delta} B(j\Delta)$. Total initial cost is $\sum D_j = V(0)$.
  3. Immediate riskless profit at $t = 0$: $V - V(0) > 0$.
  4. At each future date $j\Delta$, account $j$ has grown to exactly $D_j \prod_{k=1}^j (1+i_k)^\Delta = B(j\Delta)$, which pays off the contractual obligation. Future net balance is identically $0$.
- *If Market Price $V < V(0)$ (Underpriced):*
  1. Buy the cash flow at price $V$.
  2. Fund the purchase by borrowing $V$ at time $0$ via fractional loans matched to future payments.
  3. Repayments at each date $j\Delta$ are strictly less than $B(j\Delta)$, leaving guaranteed positive cash inflows at future dates with zero initial capital.

** Term Structure and Forward Rates
- *Spot rate $s_j$:* Annualized rate for an investment from $0$ to $j\Delta$. Capital $C$ grows to $C(1+s_j)^{j\Delta}$.
- *Zero-Coupon Bond (ZCB):* Pays $1$ at maturity $j\Delta$. Price at $t = 0$:
  $$ P(0, j\Delta) = (1+s_j)^{-j\Delta} $$
- *Forward rate $f_j$:* Annualized rate contracted at time $0$ for borrowing/lending during the future interval $[(j-1)\Delta, j\Delta)$.

*** Forward Rate Derivation & Multi-Period Relation
By comparing a direct spot deposit with a rolled-over forward deposit:
$$ (1+s_j)^{j\Delta} = (1+s_{j-1})^{(j-1)\Delta} (1+f_j)^\Delta \implies f_j = \left[\frac{(1+s_j)^{j\Delta}}{(1+s_{j-1})^{(j-1)\Delta}}\right]^{1/\Delta} - 1 $$
By telescoping multiplication:
$$ (1+s_k)^{k\Delta} = \prod_{j=1}^k (1+f_j)^\Delta $$

*** Arbitrage-Free Condition: $f_j \ge 0$
Assuming cash can be held without storage cost (earning at least $0\%$ nominal interest):
$$ \text{No Arbitrage} \iff f_j \ge 0 \quad \forall j \ge 1 \iff (1+s_k)^k \ge (1+s_j)^j \quad \forall k \ge j $$

  *The Arbitrage Strategy if $f_j < 0$ (Problem Set 2, Exercise 2.1):*
If $f_j < 0$, then $(1+s_j)^j < (1+s_{j-1})^{j-1}$.
1. At $t = 0$, borrow amount $C$ for $j$ periods at spot rate $s_j$. (Liability at $t = j$: $C(1+s_j)^j$).
2. Deposit $C$ for $j-1$ periods at spot rate $s_{j-1}$.
3. At $t = j-1$, withdraw the deposit: you have $C(1+s_{j-1})^{j-1}$.
4. Hold this money as physical cash until $t = j$ (earning $0\%$).
5. At $t = j$, repay the loan: Net profit is:
   $$ C(1+s_{j-1})^{j-1} - C(1+s_j)^j > 0 $$
Zero cash spent at $t = 0$, zero liabilities remain, guaranteed risk-free profit!

** Duration, Convexity, and Immunization Theory
Let a cash flow have inflows $A_j$ and liabilities $L_j$. The net surplus under flat yield $s$ is:
$$ f(s) = V_A(s) - V_L(s) = \sum_{j=1}^T (A_j - L_j)(1+s)^{-j} $$

- *Macaulay Duration $D(s)$:* Weighted average time to payment:
  $$ D(s) = \frac{\sum_{j=1}^T j A_j (1+s)^{-j}}{\sum_{j=1}^T A_j (1+s)^{-j}} = \sum_{j=1}^T j \cdot w_j, \quad w_j = \frac{A_j(1+s)^{-j}}{V_A(s)} $$
- *Modified Duration $D^*(s)$:*
  $$ D^*(s) = \frac{D(s)}{1+s} = -\frac{1}{V(s)}\frac{dV(s)}{ds} \implies \frac{\Delta V}{V} \approx -D^*(s) \Delta s $$
- *Duration of a Constant Annuity $B(1) = \dots = B(T) = A$ (Problem Set 3, Ex 3.1):*
  $$ D(s) = \frac{1+s}{s} - \frac{T}{(1+s)^T - 1} $$

*** Redington Immunization (Theorem 3.2)
To protect net surplus against small interest rate shifts around $s_0$, require:
1. *Value Matching:* $V_A(s_0) = V_L(s_0) \implies f(s_0) = 0$.
2. *Duration Matching:* $D_A(s_0) = D_L(s_0) \implies f'(s_0) = 0$.
3. *Convexity Dominance:* $C_A(s_0) > C_L(s_0) \implies f''(s_0) > 0$.
Under these conditions, $s_0$ is a strict local minimum: any small shock $\Delta s \ne 0$ makes $f(s_0 + \Delta s) \approx \frac{1}{2} f''(s_0)(\Delta s)^2 > 0$ (surplus increases!).

*** Mathematical Property: Duration Decreases with Interest Rate (Ex 3.2)
Let $M(s) = \frac{\sum j^2 A_j (1+s)^{-j}}{g(s)}$. Treating $w_j$ as a probability distribution:
$$ D(s) = \mathbb{E}_w[J], \quad M(s) = \mathbb{E}_w[J^2] $$
By non-negativity of variance:
$$ \operatorname{Var}_w(J) = M(s) - D(s)^2 \ge 0 \implies M(s) \ge D(s)^2 $$
Differentiating duration directly:
$$ \frac{d D(s)}{ds} = -\frac{1}{1+s}\left[M(s) - D(s)^2\right] = -\frac{1}{1+s} \operatorname{Var}_w(J) < 0 $$
Thus, *duration is strictly decreasing in $s$*.

#+begin_quote
  *Conceptual Note on Page 22 (Calculation Excluded):*
Classical Redington immunization assumes a flat yield curve that shifts by a constant parallel amount. As shown conceptually on page 22 of the lecture notes, assuming that interest rates of all maturities always move by the identical constant parallel amount permits an arbitrage opportunity (constructing a butterfly of maturities that captures positive convexity for free). In realistic models, parallel shifts are restricted to eliminate arbitrage.
#+end_quote

* The One-Period Market Model & FTAP 1

** Model Setup
- Finite state space $\Omega = \{\omega_1, \dots, \omega_M\}$, with physical probabilities $\mathbb{P}(\{\omega_m\}) > 0$.
- $N$ traded securities:
  - Time $0$ price vector: $S(0) = (S_1(0), \dots, S_N(0))^T \in \mathbb{R}^N$.
  - Time $1$ payoff matrix: $S(1) \in \mathbb{R}^{M \times N}$, where row $m$ is $S(1, \omega_m)^T$.
- Portfolio: $\theta = (\theta_1, \dots, \theta_N)^T \in \mathbb{R}^N$.
  - Initial cost: $S(0)\theta = \sum_{n=1}^N \theta_n S_n(0)$.
  - Terminal payoff in state $\omega_m$: $(S(1)\theta)_m = \sum_{n=1}^N \theta_n S_n(1, \omega_m)$.

** Definition 5.1: Arbitrage Opportunity in the One-Period Model

#+begin_quote
  *Definition 5.1 (Arbitrage Opportunity):*
An *arbitrage opportunity* exists if there is a portfolio $\theta \in \mathbb{R}^N$ such that:
\begin{equation}
S(0)\theta \le 0
\end{equation}
and
\begin{equation}
S(1)\theta \ge 0 \quad \text{a.s.} \quad \text{and} \quad \mathbb{P}(S(1)\theta > 0) > 0.
\end{equation}
The market model is defined to be *arbitrage-free* if no arbitrage opportunity exists.
#+end_quote

*** Anatomy and Economic Interpretation of Definition 5.1
Definition 5.1 mathematically captures the intuitive idea of a *"free lunch"* or a *"money-making machine"*:
1. *$S(0)\theta \le 0$ (Non-positive initial cost):*
   The portfolio requires zero net investment upfront ($S(0)\theta = 0$), or it generates immediate positive cash in your pocket today ($S(0)\theta < 0$).
2. *$S(1)\theta \ge 0$ a.s. (Zero risk of loss):*
   In every possible state of nature $\omega_m \in \Omega$ (since physical probabilities $\mathbb{P}(\{\omega_m\}) > 0$), the terminal payoff satisfies $(S(1)\theta)_m \ge 0$. You can /never/ lose money at time $1$.
3. *$\mathbb{P}(S(1)\theta > 0) > 0$ (Strictly positive probability of gain):*
   There is at least one state $\omega_m$ where $(S(1)\theta)_m > 0$. You have a genuine chance of walking away with strictly positive profit.

*** Equivalence of $S(0)\theta \le 0$ and $S(0)\theta = 0$ (Remark 5.2)
#+begin_quote
  *Remark 5.2 from the Lecture Notes:*
The condition $S(0)\theta \le 0$ in Definition 5.1 is mathematically equivalent to the condition $S(0)\theta = 0$ whenever a risk-free bank account or bond exists (or by scaling cash).
#+end_quote
*Proof of Equivalence:*
- Suppose a portfolio $\theta$ achieves $S(0)\theta < 0$ and $S(1)\theta \ge 0$ a.s. You pocket cash $C = -S(0)\theta > 0$ upfront at time $0$.
- Invest this positive cash $C$ into Security 1 (the riskless bond with interest rate $i \ge 0$, where $S_1(0)=1, S_1(1)=1+i$).
- Define the augmented portfolio $\tilde{\theta} = \theta + C e_1$ (where $e_1 = (1, 0, \dots, 0)^T$).
- Initial cost:
  $$ S(0)\tilde{\theta} = S(0)\theta + C S_1(0) = -C + C(1) = 0 $$
- Terminal payoff in every state $\omega_m$:
  $$ S(1, \omega_m)\tilde{\theta} = S(1, \omega_m)\theta + C(1+i) \ge 0 + C(1+i) > 0 $$
The new portfolio $\tilde{\theta}$ costs exactly $0$ today and yields strictly positive wealth in /every single state/! Thus, admitting an arbitrage with $S(0)\theta < 0$ is completely equivalent to admitting an arbitrage with $S(0)\theta = 0$.

** State Price Vectors, Deflators, and Risk-Neutral Measures

#+begin_quote
  *Definition 5.2 (Pricing Kernel / State Space Deflator):*
A strictly positive random variable $\varphi > 0$ defined on $\Omega$ is called a *pricing kernel* (or *state space deflator*) if:
$$ S_n(0) = \mathbb{E}[\varphi S_n(1)] \quad \text{for all } n = 1, \dots, N $$
#+end_quote

- *State Price Vector $\psi$:*
  In a finite market $\Omega = \{\omega_1, \dots, \omega_M\}$, define the state prices by $\psi_m = \varphi(\omega_m) \mathbb{P}(\{\omega_m\})$.
  Then $\psi = (\psi_1, \dots, \psi_M)^T \gg 0$ (meaning $\psi_m > 0$ for all $m$), and the pricing relationship becomes the linear system:
  $$ S(0) = S(1)^T \psi $$
- *Equivalent Martingale Measure $\mathbb{Q}$:*
  When Security 1 is a risk-free bond with interest rate $i$ ($S_1(0)=1, S_1(1)=1+i$), setting $n=1$ yields $1 = (1+i)\mathbb{E}[\varphi] \implies \mathbb{E}[\varphi] = \frac{1}{1+i} = \sum_{m=1}^M \psi_m$.
  We define risk-neutral probabilities $q_m = (1+i)\psi_m = \frac{\varphi(\omega_m)}{\mathbb{E}[\varphi]} \mathbb{P}(\{\omega_m\})$.
  Since $q_m > 0$ and $\sum q_m = 1$, $\mathbb{Q}$ is a probability measure equivalent to $\mathbb{P}$ ($\mathbb{Q} \sim \mathbb{P}$), and:
  $$ S_n(0) = \frac{1}{1+i} \mathbb{E}_\mathbb{Q}[S_n(1)] \quad \text{for all } n = 1, \dots, N $$

** The First Fundamental Theorem of Asset Pricing (FTAP 1)

#+begin_quote
*Theorem 5.3 (First Fundamental Theorem of Asset Pricing):*
The one-period market model is *arbitrage-free* if and only if there exists at least one strictly positive state price vector $\psi \gg 0$ (or equivalently, a strictly positive deflator $\varphi > 0$, or an equivalent martingale measure $\mathbb{Q} \sim \mathbb{P}$).
#+end_quote

*** Mathematical Separation Tools: Lemma 5.4 & Corollary 5.5
The proof of Theorem 5.3 relies on two classical results in convex analysis:

#+begin_quote
*Lemma 5.4 (Hahn-Banach Separation Theorem for $\mathbb{R}^n$):*
Let $A, B \subset \mathbb{R}^n$ be non-empty, disjoint convex sets. If $A$ is compact and $B$ is closed, there exists a linear functional $f(x) = z^T x$ and a constant $\gamma \in \mathbb{R}$ such that:
\begin{equation}
f(x) < \gamma \le f(y) \quad \text{for all } x \in A, \; y \in B
\end{equation}
#+end_quote

#+begin_quote
*Corollary 5.5 (Variant of Farkas' Lemma for Linear Subspaces):*
Let $V \subseteq \mathbb{R}^K$ be a linear subspace, and let $\mathbb{R}_+^K = \{x \in \mathbb{R}^K \mid x_j \ge 0 \; \forall j\}$ denote the non-negative orthant.
Then exactly one of the following two statements holds:
1. $V \cap (\mathbb{R}_+^K \setminus \{0\}) \ne \emptyset$ (i.e., $V$ contains a non-zero non-negative vector).
2. There exists a strictly positive vector $z \gg 0$ ($z_j > 0$ for all $j = 1, \dots, K$) orthogonal to $V$:
   \begin{equation}
   z^T y = 0 \quad \text{for all } y \in V
   \end{equation}
#+end_quote

*** How to Prove Theorem 5.3 on the Exam (Complete Step-by-Step Proof)

#+begin_quote
*Exam Strategy:*
The proof requires demonstrating two directions:
1. $\implies$ (*Absence of Arbitrage $\implies$ Existence of $\psi \gg 0$*): Set up the payoff subspace $V$ in dimension $K = M + 1$, equate no-arbitrage with $V \cap \mathbb{R}_+^{M+1} = \{0\}$, apply Corollary 5.5 to get orthogonal vector $z \gg 0$, and normalize by $z_0 > 0$ to define $\psi_m = z_m / z_0 > 0$.
2. $\impliedby$ (*Existence of $\psi \gg 0 \implies$ Absence of Arbitrage*): Assume an arbitrage portfolio $\theta$ exists, evaluate $S(0)\theta = \psi^T (S(1)\theta) > 0$, and derive an immediate contradiction with $S(0)\theta \le 0$.
#+end_quote

*Part 1: ($\implies$) Absence of Arbitrage Implies Existence of $\psi \gg 0$*

1. *Define the Dimension and Net Payoff Mapping:*
   Set dimension $K = M + 1$.
   Define the linear mapping $L: \mathbb{R}^N \to \mathbb{R}^{M+1}$ for any portfolio $\theta \in \mathbb{R}^N$:
   \begin{equation}
   L(\theta) = \begin{pmatrix} -S(0)\theta \\ S(1, \omega_1)\theta \\ \vdots \\ S(1, \omega_M)\theta \end{pmatrix} \in \mathbb{R}^{M+1}
   \end{equation}
   Because $L$ is a linear map, its image $V := L(\mathbb{R}^N) = \{L(\theta) \mid \theta \in \mathbb{R}^N\}$ is a *linear subspace* of $\mathbb{R}^{M+1}$.

2. *Geometric Characterization of Arbitrage:*
   Recall Definition 5.1: A portfolio $\theta$ is an arbitrage opportunity if:
   - $S(0)\theta \le 0 \iff -S(0)\theta \ge 0$,
   - $S(1, \omega_m)\theta \ge 0$ for all $m = 1, \dots, M$,
   - With at least one strict inequality: $-S(0)\theta > 0$ or $S(1, \omega_m)\theta > 0$ for some $m$.
   This is mathematically identical to stating that the vector $L(\theta)$ lies in $\mathbb{R}_+^{M+1} \setminus \{0\}$.
   Therefore:
   \begin{equation}
   \text{The market is arbitrage-free} \iff V \cap (\mathbb{R}_+^{M+1} \setminus \{0\}) = \emptyset \iff V \cap \mathbb{R}_+^{M+1} = \{0\}
   \end{equation}

3. *Apply Corollary 5.5 (Separation):*
   Because $V$ is a linear subspace and $V \cap \mathbb{R}_+^{M+1} = \{0\}$, statement (1) of Corollary 5.5 fails.
   Therefore, statement (2) must hold: there exists a strictly positive vector:
   \begin{equation}
   z = (z_0, z_1, \dots, z_M)^T \in \mathbb{R}^{M+1}, \quad z_j > 0 \quad \forall j = 0, 1, \dots, M
   \end{equation}
   such that $z$ is orthogonal to the entire subspace $V$:
   \begin{equation}
   z^T L(\theta) = 0 \quad \text{for all } \theta \in \mathbb{R}^N
   \end{equation}

4. *Expand the Orthogonality Condition:*
   Evaluating the dot product $z^T L(\theta) = 0$:
   \begin{equation}
   -z_0 (S(0)\theta) + \sum_{m=1}^M z_m (S(1, \omega_m)\theta) = 0
   \end{equation}
   Rearranging gives:
   \begin{equation}
   z_0 S(0)\theta = \left(\sum_{m=1}^M z_m S(1, \omega_m)\right)\theta \quad \text{for all } \theta \in \mathbb{R}^N
   \end{equation}

5. *Equate Coefficients and Normalize by $z_0$:*
   Because this identity holds for *all* choices of $\theta \in \mathbb{R}^N$, the coefficient vectors must be identical:
   \begin{equation}
   z_0 S(0) = \sum_{m=1}^M z_m S(1, \omega_m) = S(1)^T \begin{pmatrix} z_1 \\ \vdots \\ z_M \end{pmatrix}
   \end{equation}
   Since $z \gg 0$, we know $z_0 > 0$. Dividing both sides by $z_0$:
   \begin{equation}
   S(0) = \sum_{m=1}^M \frac{z_m}{z_0} S(1, \omega_m)
   \end{equation}
   Define the state prices:
   \begin{equation}
   \psi_m := \frac{z_m}{z_0} \quad \text{for } m = 1, \dots, M
   \end{equation}
   Since $z_m > 0$ and $z_0 > 0$, we have $\psi_m > 0$ strictly for all $m$.
   Thus, $\psi = (\psi_1, \dots, \psi_M)^T \gg 0$ is a strictly positive state price vector satisfying $S(0) = S(1)^T \psi$.
   Furthermore, defining $\varphi(\omega_m) = \frac{\psi_m}{\mathbb{P}(\{\omega_m\})} > 0$ gives $S(0) = \mathbb{E}[\varphi S(1)]$.

*Part 2: ($\impliedby$) Existence of $\psi \gg 0$ Implies Absence of Arbitrage*

1. *Assume Existence of $\psi \gg 0$:*
   Suppose there exists a strictly positive state price vector $\psi = (\psi_1, \dots, \psi_M)^T \gg 0$ such that $S(0) = S(1)^T \psi$.

2. *Proof by Contradiction:*
   Suppose for contradiction that an arbitrage opportunity $\theta \in \mathbb{R}^N$ exists.
   By Definition 5.1:
   - $S(0)\theta \le 0$,
   - $(S(1)\theta)_m \ge 0$ for all $m = 1, \dots, M$,
   - $(S(1)\theta)_{m^*} > 0$ for at least one state $\omega_{m^*}$ (since $\mathbb{P}(S(1)\theta > 0) > 0$).

3. *Evaluate Portfolio Cost via State Prices:*
   Substitute the pricing formula $S(0) = S(1)^T \psi$ into the cost $S(0)\theta$:
   \begin{equation}
   S(0)\theta = (S(1)^T \psi)^T \theta = \psi^T (S(1)\theta) = \sum_{m=1}^M \psi_m (S(1)\theta)_m
   \end{equation}
   Examine each term in this sum:
   - For all $m$: $\psi_m > 0$ and $(S(1)\theta)_m \ge 0$, so $\psi_m (S(1)\theta)_m \ge 0$.
   - For state $m^*$: $\psi_{m^*} > 0$ and $(S(1)\theta)_{m^*} > 0$, so $\psi_{m^*} (S(1)\theta)_{m^*} > 0$.
   Therefore, the total sum is strictly positive:
   \begin{equation}
   S(0)\theta = \sum_{m=1}^M \psi_m (S(1)\theta)_m \ge \psi_{m^*} (S(1)\theta)_{m^*} > 0
   \end{equation}

4. *Contradiction and Conclusion:*
   We have deduced $S(0)\theta > 0$. But an arbitrage opportunity requires $S(0)\theta \le 0$.
   A real number cannot be both strictly positive and non-positive:
   \begin{equation}
   0 < S(0)\theta \le 0 \quad \implies \text{Contradiction!}
   \end{equation}
   Hence, no arbitrage opportunity can exist. The market is strictly arbitrage-free! $\blacksquare$

** Practical Arbitrage Detection & Problem Set 4 Walkthrough

*** Case 1: Two Securities (Bond + Stock) — Problem Set 4, Exercises 4.1 & 4.2
Let Security 1 be a bond with interest rate $i \ge 0$, and Security 2 be a share with possible payoffs $0 < \alpha_1 < \alpha_2 < \dots < \alpha_M$ and current price $p$.
$$ \text{Market is Arbitrage-Free} \iff p \in \left(\frac{\alpha_1}{1+i}, \frac{\alpha_M}{1+i}\right) $$

- *Necessity ($\implies$, Exercise 4.1):* By FTAP 1, $p = \sum_{m=1}^M \psi_m \alpha_m$. Since $\frac{1}{1+i} = \sum \psi_m$ and $\alpha_1 < \alpha_m < \alpha_M$:
  $$ \frac{\alpha_1}{1+i} = \alpha_1 \sum \psi_m < \sum \psi_m \alpha_m = p < \alpha_M \sum \psi_m = \frac{\alpha_M}{1+i} $$
- *Sufficiency ($\impliedby$, Exercise 4.2 via Definition 5.1):* If $p$ is strictly inside the bounds, any zero-cost portfolio $\theta_1 \frac{1}{1+i} + \theta_2 p = 0 \implies \theta_1 = -(1+i)p\theta_2$.
  Its payoff is $S(1)\theta = \theta_2 [S_2(1) - (1+i)p]$.
  Since $(1+i)p \in (\alpha_1, \alpha_M)$, the term $[S_2(1) - (1+i)p]$ is strictly negative in state $\omega_1$ and strictly positive in state $\omega_M$.
  - If $\theta_2 > 0$, the portfolio loses money in state $\omega_1$: $S(1, \omega_1)\theta < 0$.
  - If $\theta_2 < 0$, the portfolio loses money in state $\omega_M$: $S(1, \omega_M)\theta < 0$.
  Neither choice can satisfy $S(1)\theta \ge 0$ a.s. Thus, no arbitrage opportunity exists!

*** Case 2: Three Securities & Linear System Solving — Problem Set 4, Exercise 4.4
Consider $\Omega = \{\omega_1, \omega_2, \omega_3\}$, riskless rate $i = 0$ ($S_1(0)=1, S_1(1)=(1,1,1)^T$).
Two risky assets have payoffs:
$S_2(1) = (3, 3, 1)^T$, price $p$.
$S_3(1) = (1, 1, 3)^T$, price $q$.

To find all arbitrage-free price pairs $(p, q)$, write the system $S(0) = S(1)^T \psi$:
$$ \begin{cases} 1 = \psi_1 + \psi_2 + \psi_3 \\ p = 3(\psi_1 + \psi_2) + \psi_3 \\ q = (\psi_1 + \psi_2) + 3\psi_3 \end{cases} $$
Let $a = \psi_1 + \psi_2$ and $b = \psi_3$. The equations become:
$$ a + b = 1, \quad 3a + b = p, \quad a + 3b = q $$
Summing the last two: $p + q = 4(a+b) = 4 \implies q = 4 - p$.
Solving for $a$ and $b$:
$$ a = \frac{p-1}{2}, \quad b = \frac{3-p}{2} $$
For a state price vector to exist, we need $a > 0$ and $b > 0$:
$$ a > 0 \implies p > 1, \quad b > 0 \implies p < 3 $$
Whenever $p \in (1, 3)$, we can choose $\psi_1 = \psi_2 = a/2 > 0$ and $\psi_3 = b > 0$.
  *Conclusion:* The market is arbitrage-free if and only if $p \in (1, 3)$ and $q = 4 - p$.

*** Case 3: Forward Contract Pricing on a Share — Problem Set 4, Exercise 4.3
A contract promises to buy one share at time $1$ for pre-agreed price $\gamma$.
Payoff at $t = 1$: $X = S_2(1) - \gamma S_1(1)$.
By linearity of replication:
$$ V(0) = S_2(0) - \gamma S_1(0) = p - \frac{\gamma}{1+i} $$

*** Case 4: The Law of One Price — Problem Set 4, Exercise 4.5
#+begin_quote
  *Exercise 4.5 Problem Statement:*
Suppose the terminal payoff of Security $N$ is a linear combination of the first $N-1$ securities:
$$ S_N(1) = \theta_1 S_1(1) + \theta_2 S_2(1) + \dots + \theta_{N-1} S_{N-1}(1) $$
Show that when the market is arbitrage-free, the initial price must satisfy:
$$ S_N(0) = \theta_1 S_1(0) + \theta_2 S_2(0) + \dots + \theta_{N-1} S_{N-1}(0) $$
#+end_quote

*Proof 1 (Via FTAP 1 Deflator $\varphi$):*
Because the market is arbitrage-free, Theorem 5.3 guarantees the existence of a positive deflator $\varphi > 0$ such that $S_n(0) = \mathbb{E}[\varphi S_n(1)]$ for all $n = 1, \dots, N$.
Applying this to Security $N$:
$$ S_N(0) = \mathbb{E}[\varphi S_N(1)] = \mathbb{E}\left[\varphi \sum_{n=1}^{N-1} \theta_n S_n(1)\right] = \sum_{n=1}^{N-1} \theta_n \mathbb{E}[\varphi S_n(1)] = \sum_{n=1}^{N-1} \theta_n S_n(0) \quad \blacksquare $$

*Proof 2 (Directly from Definition 5.1 via Arbitrage Portfolio Construction):*
Suppose for contradiction that $S_N(0) \ne \sum_{n=1}^{N-1} \theta_n S_n(0)$:
1. *If $S_N(0) > \sum_{n=1}^{N-1} \theta_n S_n(0)$* (Security $N$ is overpriced): Construct portfolio $\hat{\theta} = (\theta_1, \dots, \theta_{N-1}, -1)^T$ by selling 1 unit of Security $N$ and buying the replicating portfolio $(\theta_1, \dots, \theta_{N-1})$:
   - Initial cash flow: $S(0)\hat{\theta} = \sum_{n=1}^{N-1} \theta_n S_n(0) - S_N(0) < 0$ (positive cash pocketed upfront!).
   - Terminal payoff in every state: $S(1)\hat{\theta} = \sum_{n=1}^{N-1} \theta_n S_n(1) - S_N(1) = 0$ identically.
   This portfolio costs negative capital and has identically zero future liability. By Definition 5.1, this is an arbitrage opportunity!
2. *Case $S_N(0) < \sum_{n=1}^{N-1} \theta_n S_n(0)$ (Security $N$ is underpriced):*
   Take the opposite portfolio $-\hat{\theta} = (-\theta_1, \dots, -\theta_{N-1}, +1)^T$ (buy Security $N$, short the replica).
   Initial cash flow is negative (money pocketed), and terminal liability is identically zero. Again, this violates Definition 5.1!
Therefore, absence of arbitrage *strictly forces* $S_N(0) = \sum_{n=1}^{N-1} \theta_n S_n(0)$. $\blacksquare$

** Complementary Examples in Section 5.1 (Pages 31–33)

*** Example 5.4: Multi-Maturity Zero-Coupon Bonds and Flat Yield Arbitrage
Consider ZCBs of maturities 1, 2, and 3 years. The interest rate in year 1 is $i_1$, and stochastic rates in years 2 and 3 are $I_2$ and $I_3$.
Under a flat yield curve assumption, prices at $k=0$ and $k=1$ satisfy:
$$ S_1(0) = \frac{1}{1+i_1}, \quad S_2(0) = \frac{1}{(1+i_1)^2}, \quad S_3(0) = \frac{1}{(1+i_1)^3} $$
$$ S_1(1) = 1, \quad S_2(1) = \frac{1}{1+I_2}, \quad S_3(1) = \frac{1}{(1+I_2)^2} $$
Applying FTAP 1 (Theorem 5.3), a risk-neutral measure $\mathbb{Q}$ must price these assets:
$$ S_2(0) = \frac{1}{1+i_1} \mathbb{E}_\mathbb{Q}[S_2(1)] \implies \mathbb{E}_\mathbb{Q}\left[\frac{1}{1+I_2}\right] = \frac{1}{1+i_1} $$
$$ S_3(0) = \frac{1}{1+i_1} \mathbb{E}_\mathbb{Q}[S_3(1)] \implies \mathbb{E}_\mathbb{Q}\left[\frac{1}{(1+I_2)^2}\right] = \frac{1}{(1+i_1)^2} $$
Comparing these two equations:
$$ \mathbb{E}_\mathbb{Q}\left[\left(\frac{1}{1+I_2}\right)^2\right] = \left(\mathbb{E}_\mathbb{Q}\left[\frac{1}{1+I_2}\right]\right)^2 \implies \operatorname{Var}_\mathbb{Q}\left(\frac{1}{1+I_2}\right) = 0 $$
This is possible *only if $I_2$ is a deterministic constant* \(\mathbb{Q}\)-a.s. (and hence \(\mathbb{P}\)-a.s.)!
  *Takeaway:* If $I_2$ is truly stochastic, no risk-neutral measure can exist, meaning the flat yield curve model *must admit arbitrage*!

*** Example 5.5: Arbitrage Freedom with Continuous Payoff Density
Let Security 1 be a bond ($S_1(0)=1/(1+i), S_1(1)=1$), and Security 2 have continuous density $f(x) > 0$ on $(0, \infty)$ and $S_2(0) = p$.
- If $p \le 0$, buying the stock is an immediate arbitrage.
- If $p > 0$, any zero-cost portfolio $\theta_1/(1+i) + p\theta_2 = 0$ has $S(1)\theta = \theta_2[S_2(1) - (1+i)p]$. Because $S_2(1)$ has full support on $(0, \infty)$, $S_2(1) - (1+i)p$ takes both positive and negative values with positive probability. Thus, no arbitrage exists.
- In Theorem 5.3 terms, when $f(x) = \mu e^{-\mu x}$, a valid deflator can be constructed as an exponential tilting $\varphi(x) = a e^{bx}$ with $b < \mu$.

* Attainability, Market Completeness, & FTAP 2

** Attainability and the Law of One Price
A contingent claim $X \in \mathbb{R}^M$ is *attainable* (or *replicable*) if there exists a portfolio $\theta \in \mathbb{R}^N$ such that:
$$ S(1)\theta = X \quad \left(\text{i.e. } \sum_{n=1}^N \theta_n S_n(1, \omega_m) = X(\omega_m) \quad \forall m\right) $$

*** Theorem 5.6 (Pricing Attainable Claims)
In an arbitrage-free market, the unique price of an attainable claim $X$ is:
$$ V_X(0) = S(0)\theta = \mathbb{E}[\varphi X] = \frac{1}{1+i} \mathbb{E}_\mathbb{Q}[X] $$
  *Every valid pricing kernel $\varphi$ and every risk-neutral measure $\mathbb{Q}$ gives the exact same price for an attainable claim!*

** Market Completeness and FTAP 2
#+begin_quote
  *Definition 5.7 (Market Completeness):*
A market is *complete* if /every/ contingent claim $X \in \mathbb{R}^M$ is attainable:
$$ \operatorname{Im}(S(1)) = \mathbb{R}^M \iff \operatorname{rank}(S(1)) = M $$
This requires that there are at least as many linearly independent assets as states of nature: $N \ge M$.
#+end_quote

#+begin_quote
  *Theorem 5.8 (Second Fundamental Theorem of Asset Pricing - FTAP 2):*
Suppose the market is arbitrage-free. Then:
$$ \text{Market is complete} \iff \text{Pricing kernel } \varphi \text{ (or } \psi\text{, } \mathbb{Q}\text{) is unique} $$
#+end_quote

/(Proof omitted per exam syllabus instructions)./
  *Intuitive Meaning:*
The pricing system $S(1)^T \psi = S(0)$ has $N$ equations and $M$ unknowns ($\psi_1, \dots, \psi_M$).
- If $\operatorname{rank}(S(1)) = M$, the matrix has full column rank $M$, so the solution $\psi \gg 0$ is unique. Every risk is priced uniquely.
- If $\operatorname{rank}(S(1)) < M$ (incomplete market), there are degrees of freedom: multiple deflators exist, and unreplicable claims have a range of arbitrage-free prices $(\underline{V}, \overline{V})$.

** Core Derivative Formulas & Replications

*** Put-Call Parity (Problem Set 5, Exercise 5.1)
Consider a Call option $C = (S(1) - \gamma)^+$ and Put option $P = (\gamma - S(1))^+$.
Notice the algebraic identity for any real numbers:
$$ (S(1) - \gamma)^+ - (\gamma - S(1))^+ = S(1) - \gamma = S_2(1) - \gamma S_1(1) $$
Multiplying by any deflator $\varphi$ and taking expectations:
$$ \mathbb{E}[\varphi C] - \mathbb{E}[\varphi P] = \mathbb{E}[\varphi S_2(1)] - \gamma \mathbb{E}[\varphi S_1(1)] $$
$$ r - q = p - \frac{\gamma}{1+i} \iff r + \frac{\gamma}{1+i} = p + q $$
/(where $r$ is call price, $q$ is put price, $p$ is share price)./

*** Capital Protection / Structured Note (Problem Set 5, Exercise 5.2)
A security guarantees return of its initial purchase price $h$ at time $1$, plus any upside price gain of the share:
$$ S_4(1) = h + (S_2(1) - p)^+ = h S_1(1) + S_3(1) $$
where $S_3$ is an ATM call option with strike $p$ and price $q$.
Using any deflator $\varphi$:
$$ h = S_4(0) = h \mathbb{E}[\varphi S_1(1)] + \mathbb{E}[\varphi S_3(1)] = h \frac{1}{1+i} + q $$
$$ h \left(1 - \frac{1}{1+i}\right) = q \implies h \left(\frac{i}{1+i}\right) = q \implies h = \frac{q(1+i)}{i} $$

*** Butterfly Spread Decomposition (Problem Set 5, Exercise 5.3)
Payoff: $X = (\hat{K} - |S(1) - S(0)|)^+$.
This piecewise linear "tent" payoff peaked at $S(0)$ can be replicated by options with strikes $K_1 = S(0)-\hat{K}$, $K_2 = S(0)$, $K_3 = S(0)+\hat{K}$:
- *Using Calls:*
  $$ X = (S(1) - (S(0)-\hat{K}))^+ - 2(S(1) - S(0))^+ + (S(1) - (S(0)+\hat{K}))^+ $$
- *Using Puts:*
  $$ X = ((S(0)+\hat{K}) - S(1))^+ - 2(S(0) - S(1))^+ + ((S(0)-\hat{K}) - S(1))^+ $$
  *Recipe:* Buy 1 low-strike option, sell 2 middle-strike options, buy 1 high-strike option!

* One-Period Portfolio Selection & Utility Theory

** Utility Functions and Risk Aversion
An investor evaluates terminal wealth $W = V(1)$ through a concave utility function $u(w)$:
- *Non-satiation (Greed):* $u'(w) > 0$ (more wealth is always preferred).
- *Risk Aversion:* $u''(w) < 0$ (strictly concave).
  - By Jensen's inequality: $\mathbb{E}[u(W)] < u(\mathbb{E}[W])$. An investor always prefers the sure expectation over a risky gamble.

*** Arrow-Pratt Risk Aversion Measures
- *Absolute Risk Aversion (ARA):* $R_A(w) = -\frac{u''(w)}{u'(w)}$ (measures aversion to absolute dollar risk).
- *Relative Risk Aversion (RRA):* $R_R(w) = -w \frac{u''(w)}{u'(w)}$ (measures aversion to percentage wealth risk).

#+caption: Standard Utility Function Families
#+ATTR_LATEX: :environment tabularx :width \textwidth :align p{0.22\textwidth} p{0.32\textwidth} p{0.16\textwidth} X
| Utility Name | Formula $u(w)$ | ARA $R_A(w)$ | RRA $R_R(w)$ |
|--------------+----------------+--------------+--------------|
| *Logarithmic* | $\log(w)$ | $1/w$ | $1$ (CRRA) |
| *Power (CRRA)* | $\frac{w^{1-\gamma}-1}{1-\gamma} \; (\gamma > 0, \ne 1)$ | $\gamma/w$ | $\gamma$ (CRRA) |
| *Exponential (CARA)* | $-\frac{1}{\alpha} e^{-\alpha w} \; (\alpha > 0)$ | $\alpha$ (constant) | $\alpha w$ |

** The One-Period Optimization Problem
Given initial wealth $V(0) > 0$, the investor solves:
\begin{equation}
\max_{\theta \in \mathbb{R}^N} \mathbb{E}[u(S(1)\theta)] \quad \text{subject to} \quad S(0)\theta = V(0) \tag{5.10}
\end{equation}

*** First-Order Condition (Euler Equation) via Lagrange Multipliers
Form the Lagrangian:
$$ \mathcal{L}(\theta, \lambda) = \mathbb{E}[u(S(1)\theta)] - \lambda (S(0)\theta - V(0)) $$
Differentiating with respect to $\theta_n$ ($n = 1, \dots, N$):
$$ \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial \theta_n} = \mathbb{E}[u'(S(1)\theta) S_n(1)] - \lambda S_n(0) = 0 $$
$$ \implies S_n(0) = \mathbb{E}\left[\frac{u'(V(1))}{\lambda} S_n(1)\right] \quad \forall n = 1, \dots, N $$

** Endogenous Pricing Kernel
Notice the profound connection to FTAP 1:
Define the random variable:
$$ \varphi = \frac{u'(V(1))}{\lambda} $$
Since $u'(w) > 0$ and $\lambda > 0$, $\varphi > 0$ strictly almost surely.
  *The marginal utility of the optimal portfolio creates an endogenous state price deflator!*

If Security 1 is a riskless bond ($S_1(0)=1, S_1(1)=1+i$):
$$ 1 = \mathbb{E}\left[\frac{u'(V(1))}{\lambda}(1+i)\right] \implies \lambda = (1+i)\mathbb{E}[u'(V(1))] $$
Substituting $\lambda$ back gives the *marginal rate of substitution*:
$$ \varphi = \frac{1}{1+i} \frac{u'(V(1))}{\mathbb{E}[u'(V(1))]} $$

* Multi-Period Market Model: Self-Financing & Martingales

** Multi-Period Setup
- Discrete trading dates $t \in \{0, 1, \dots, T\}$.
- Information filtration $\mathbb{F} = (\mathcal{F}_0, \mathcal{F}_1, \dots, \mathcal{F}_T)$.
- Asset price processes $S(t) = (S_1(t), \dots, S_N(t))^T$, adapted to $\mathbb{F}$.
- Asset 1 is the numeraire (bank account / bond), $S_1(t) > 0$.
  - Discounted prices: $S^*(t) = \frac{S(t)}{S_1(t)}$.

** Predictable Strategies & The Self-Financing Condition
A trading strategy is a sequence of portfolios $\theta(t) = (\theta_1(t), \dots, \theta_N(t))^T$, $t = 1, \dots, T$.
- *Predictability:* $\theta(t)$ is \(\mathcal{F}_{t-1}\)-measurable. The portfolio held during period $(t-1, t]$ must be chosen at time $t-1$.
- *Portfolio Value:* $V(t) = S(t)\theta(t) = \sum_{n=1}^N \theta_n(t) S_n(t)$.

#+begin_quote
  *Definition (Self-Financing Strategy):*
A trading strategy $\theta$ is *self-financing* if at each rebalancing date $t \in \{1, \dots, T-1\}$:
\begin{equation}
S(t)\theta(t+1) = S(t)\theta(t) \tag{5.16}
\end{equation}
  *Intuition:* The value of the new portfolio $\theta(t+1)$ purchased at time $t$ exactly equals the liquidated value of the old portfolio $\theta(t)$. No outside capital is added or removed!
#+end_quote

*** Wealth Increments under Self-Financing
$$ \Delta V(t) = V(t) - V(t-1) = \theta(t) \cdot \Delta S(t) $$
In discounted terms:
\begin{equation}
V^*(t) = V^*(0) + \sum_{k=1}^t \theta(k) \cdot \Delta S^*(k) \tag{5.17}
\end{equation}

** Multi-Period Fundamental Theorems of Asset Pricing

*** Definition 5.9 (Multi-Period Arbitrage)
A self-financing strategy $\theta$ is an arbitrage opportunity if:
$$ V(0) \le 0, \quad V(T) \ge 0 \text{ a.s.}, \quad \text{and } \mathbb{P}(V(T) > 0) > 0 \text{ (or } V(0) < 0\text{)} $$

*** Theorem 5.10 (Multi-Period FTAP 1)
#+begin_quote
  *Theorem 5.10:*
The multi-period market is *arbitrage-free* if and only if there exists a probability measure $\mathbb{Q} \sim \mathbb{P}$ (equivalent martingale measure) such that all discounted asset price processes $S^*(t)$ are \((\mathbb{Q}, \mathbb{F})\)-*martingales*:
\begin{equation}
\mathbb{E}_\mathbb{Q}[S^*(t+1) \mid \mathcal{F}_t] = S^*(t) \quad \forall t = 0, 1, \dots, T-1 \tag{5.18}
\end{equation}
#+end_quote
/(Proof and Lemma 5.12 omitted per exam syllabus instructions)./

*** Theorem 5.14 (Multi-Period FTAP 2)
#+begin_quote
  *Theorem 5.14:*
An arbitrage-free multi-period market is *complete* if and only if the equivalent martingale measure $\mathbb{Q}$ is *unique*.
#+end_quote

** The Binomial Tree Model (Cox-Ross-Rubinstein)
- Bond: $S_1(t) = (1+r)^t$.
- Stock: $S_2(t+1) \in \{u S_2(t), d S_2(t)\}$ with $0 < d < 1+r < u$.
- *Unique Risk-Neutral Probability:*
  $$ q = \frac{1+r-d}{u-d} $$
- *Rollback Valuation (Backward Induction):*
  $$ V(t) = \frac{1}{1+r}\left[q V_{u}(t+1) + (1-q) V_{d}(t+1)\right] $$
- *Replicating Stock Holding (\(\Delta\)-Hedging):*
  $$ \theta_2(t+1) = \frac{V_u(t+1) - V_d(t+1)}{S_2(t)(u - d)} $$

** Walkthrough of Problem Set 6 Exercises

*** Exercise 6.1: Risk-Neutral Measures on a 6-State Two-Period Tree
Two periods ($t = 0, 1, 2$), zero interest ($r = 0, S_1(t) \equiv 1$).
$S_2(0) = 2$, $S_2(1) \in \{1, 2, 3\}$, $S_2(2) = S_2(1)(1+\xi_2)$ with $\xi_2 \in \{-1/2, 1/2\}$.
- In period 2: From every node at $t = 1$, the up/down factors are $3/2$ and $1/2$. The martingale equation gives $q_{up} = 1/2, q_{down} = 1/2$.
- In period 1: Probabilities $p, q, r$ of reaching nodes $1, 2, 3$ must satisfy:
  $$ p + q + r = 1, \quad 1\cdot p + 2\cdot q + 3\cdot r = 2 \implies p = r, \quad q = 1 - 2p \quad \text{with } p \in (0, 1/2) $$
- The risk-neutral measures form a family: $\mathbb{Q} = (p/2, p/2, (1-2p)/2, (1-2p)/2, p/2, p/2)^T$. Because $p$ is not unique, this market is *incomplete*.

*** Exercise 6.5: Extension of Arbitrage Freedom to General Derivatives
For a derivative $S_{N+1}(T)$ with price process $S_{N+1}^*(k) = \mathbb{E}_\mathbb{Q}[S_{N+1}^*(T) \mid \mathcal{F}_k]$:
By the *Tower Property of conditional expectations*:
$$ \mathbb{E}_\mathbb{Q}[S_{N+1}^*(k+1) \mid \mathcal{F}_k] = \mathbb{E}_\mathbb{Q}[\mathbb{E}_\mathbb{Q}[S_{N+1}^*(T) \mid \mathcal{F}_{k+1}] \mid \mathcal{F}_k] = \mathbb{E}_\mathbb{Q}[S_{N+1}^*(T) \mid \mathcal{F}_k] = S_{N+1}^*(k) $$
Thus, $S_{N+1}^*$ is automatically a martingale, proving the extended market is strictly arbitrage-free! $\blacksquare$

** Practice Exercise 1: Fixed-Income Valuation, Cash Flow Replication, and Arbitrage Construction
*Setting (Style of Problem Set 1, Exercises 1.2, 1.3, 1.4):*
Consider a deterministic interest rate market where cash payments occur at discrete time intervals $t = 1, 2, \dots$ (with unit time step $\Delta = 1$).
Two benchmark cash flows, Cash Flow I and Cash Flow II, trade actively in the market:
- Cash Flow I: Pays $B_I(1) = 6$ at $t = 1$ and $B_I(2) = 6$ at $t = 2$. Its current market price is $p = 10$.
- Cash Flow II: Pays $B_{II}(1) = 5$ at $t = 1$ and $B_{II}(2) = 8$ at $t = 2$. Its current market price is $q = 11$.
The market is assumed to be strictly arbitrage-free.

*Questions:*
1. (PS1 Exercise 1.2)
   Show that a target Cash Flow III with payments $B_{III}(1) = 0$ and $B_{III}(2) = 14$ can be replicated using a static portfolio $\theta = (\theta_1, \theta_2)^T$ of Cash Flows I and II.
   Determine the exact portfolio weights $\theta_1, \theta_2$ and the unique arbitrage-free price of Cash Flow III at time $0$.
2. (PS1 Exercise 1.3)
   Suppose deterministic annual spot interest rates are $i_1 = 20\%$ ($0.20$) for year 1 and $i_2 = 25\%$ ($0.25$) for year 2. Future cash flows are valued according to:
   $$ V(0) = \sum_{j=1}^T \left(\prod_{k=1}^j (1+i_k)\right)^{-1} B(j) $$
   Consider a financial contract IV paying $B_{IV}(1) = 12$ and $B_{IV}(2) = 15$.
   a) Compute the theoretical arbitrage-free present value $V_{IV}(0)$.
   b) Suppose contract IV is currently trading in the market at price $P_{IV} = 18$. Does this market admit an arbitrage opportunity? Construct an explicit arbitrage portfolio combining contract IV and the money market (borrowing/lending at rates $i_1, i_2$) that generates immediate cash profit today with zero future net liabilities.
3. (PS1 Exercise 1.4)
   Let $V(0) > 0$ be the initial price of a contract with strictly positive future payments $B(1), \dots, B(T) > 0$. The *internal interest rate* (or yield to maturity) $r$ is defined as the solution to:
   $$ \sum_{j=1}^T (1+r)^{-j} B(j) = V(0) $$
   a) Prove that the internal interest rate $r$ exists and is strictly unique in the interval $(-1, \infty)$.
   b) Prove that $\min(i_1, \dots, i_T) \le r \le \max(i_1, \dots, i_T)$, where $i_j$ are the spot rates used to determine $V(0)$.

*** Solution to Practice Exercise 1

*Part 1: Replication of Cash Flow III*
To replicate Cash Flow III, the portfolio $(\theta_1, \theta_2)$ of Cash Flows I and II must match the payments of Cash Flow III at every date $t = 1$ and $t = 2$:
\begin{equation}
6\theta_1 + 5\theta_2 = 0 \tag{1}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
6\theta_1 + 8\theta_2 = 14 \tag{2}
\end{equation}
From Equation (1):
$$ 6\theta_1 = -5\theta_2 \implies \theta_1 = -\frac{5}{6}\theta_2 $$
Substitute into Equation (2):
$$ 6\left(-\frac{5}{6}\theta_2\right) + 8\theta_2 = 14 \implies -5\theta_2 + 8\theta_2 = 14 \implies 3\theta_2 = 14 \implies \theta_2 = \frac{14}{3} $$
Then compute $\theta_1$:
$$ \theta_1 = -\frac{5}{6}\left(\frac{14}{3}\right) = -\frac{70}{18} = -\frac{35}{9} $$
*Arbitrage-Free Price at Time 0:*
By the Law of One Price (Theorem 2.1 / FTAP 1), because Cash Flow III is identical in all future payouts to holding $-\frac{35}{9}$ units of Cash Flow I and $\frac{14}{3}$ units of Cash Flow II, its time-$0$ price $P_{III}$ must equal the portfolio cost:
$$ P_{III} = \theta_1 p + \theta_2 q = -\frac{35}{9}(10) + \frac{14}{3}(11) = -\frac{350}{9} + \frac{462}{9} = \frac{112}{9} \approx 12.44 \text{ euros} $$

*Part 2: Valuation and Arbitrage Construction*
a) *Theoretical Present Value:*
The discount factors are:
- Year 1 discount factor:
  $$ d_1 = \frac{1}{1+i_1} = \frac{1}{1 + 0.20} = \frac{1}{1.20} = \frac{5}{6} $$
- Year 2 discount factor:
  $$ d_2 = \frac{1}{(1+i_1)(1+i_2)} = \frac{1}{(1.20)(1.25)} = \frac{1}{1.50} = \frac{2}{3} $$
The theoretical present value is:
$$ V_{IV}(0) = B_{IV}(1) d_1 + B_{IV}(2) d_2 = 12\left(\frac{5}{6}\right) + 15\left(\frac{2}{3}\right) = 10 + 10 = 20 \text{ euros} $$

b) *Arbitrage Execution when Market Price $P_{IV} = 18$:*
Since the market price $P_{IV} = 18 < 20 = V_{IV}(0)$, contract IV is underpriced relative to the interest rate market.
*Arbitrage Strategy:*
1. Buy $1$ unit of contract IV at time $0$, requiring an initial outlay of $-18$ euros.
2. Borrow against the future cash flows in the money market:
   - Borrow $10$ euros for 1 year at interest rate $i_1 = 20\%$. Repayment due at $t = 1$: $10 \times (1 + 0.20) = 12$ euros.
   - Borrow $10$ euros for 2 years at compounded rates $i_1, i_2$. Repayment due at $t = 2$: $10 \times (1.20)(1.25) = 15$ euros.
3. *Cash Flow Balance Sheet:*
   - *Time $0$:* Loan proceeds $+10 + 10 = +20$ euros. Purchase contract IV for $-18$ euros. Net cash pocketed:
     $$ +20 - 18 = +2 \text{ euros} > 0 $$
   - *Time $1$:* Receive $+12$ euros from contract IV; repay $-12$ euros on 1-year loan. Net cash:
     $$ +12 - 12 = 0 $$
   - *Time $2$:* Receive $+15$ euros from contract IV; repay $-15$ euros on 2-year loan. Net cash:
     $$ +15 - 15 = 0 $$
The investor pockets $+2$ euros upfront with strictly zero net liabilities at all future dates. This is a pure arbitrage opportunity!

*Part 3: Existence, Uniqueness, and Bounds for the Internal Interest Rate*
a) *Existence and Uniqueness:*
Define the function $g(r)$ on the domain $(-1, \infty)$ by:
$$ g(r) = \sum_{j=1}^T (1+r)^{-j} B(j) - V(0) $$
- *Continuity:* $g(r)$ is a finite sum of continuous rational functions on $(-1, \infty)$, hence $g$ is continuous.
- *Strict Monotonicity:* Because all payments $B(j) > 0$ are strictly positive:
  $$ g\prime(r) = \sum_{j=1}^T (-j)(1+r)^{-j-1} B(j) < 0 \quad \text{for all } r \in (-1, \infty) $$
  Because $g\prime(r) < 0$ strictly, $g(r)$ is strictly decreasing. A strictly decreasing function can have at most one root (proving *uniqueness*).
- *Limiting Boundary Values:*
  $$ \lim_{r \to -1^+} g(r) = \lim_{r \to -1^+} \left( \sum_{j=1}^T (1+r)^{-j} B(j) - V(0) \right) = +\infty $$
  $$ \lim_{r \to +\infty} g(r) = \lim_{r \to +\infty} \left( \sum_{j=1}^T (1+r)^{-j} B(j) - V(0) \right) = 0 - V(0) = -V(0) < 0 $$
  By the Intermediate Value Theorem, there exists at least one $r^* \in (-1, \infty)$ such that $g(r^*) = 0$ (proving *existence*). Combined with strict monotonicity, $r^*$ is unique.

b) *Bounds $\min(i_j) \le r^* \le \max(i_j)$:*
Let $m = \min(i_1, \dots, i_T)$ and $M = \max(i_1, \dots, i_T)$.
For each $j \in \{1, \dots, T\}$, the product of spot rates satisfies:
$$ (1+m)^j \le \prod_{k=1}^j (1+i_k) \le (1+M)^j $$
Inverting these inequalities:
$$ (1+M)^{-j} \le \left(\prod_{k=1}^j (1+i_k)\right)^{-1} \le (1+m)^{-j} $$
Multiplying by $B(j) > 0$ and summing over $j = 1, \dots, T$:
$$ \sum_{j=1}^T (1+M)^{-j} B(j) \le \sum_{j=1}^T \left(\prod_{k=1}^j (1+i_k)\right)^{-1} B(j) \le \sum_{j=1}^T (1+m)^{-j} B(j) $$
By Theorem 2.1, the center term is precisely $V(0) = \sum_{j=1}^T (1+r^*)^{-j} B(j)$. Therefore:
$$ \sum_{j=1}^T (1+M)^{-j} B(j) \le \sum_{j=1}^T (1+r^*)^{-j} B(j) \le \sum_{j=1}^T (1+m)^{-j} B(j) $$
Define $f(r) = \sum_{j=1}^T (1+r)^{-j} B(j)$. Because $f(r)$ is strictly decreasing, $f(M) \le f(r^*) \le f(m)$ immediately implies:
$$ m \le r^* \le M \iff \min(i_1, \dots, i_T) \le r^* \le \max(i_1, \dots, i_T) \quad \blacksquare $$

** Practice Exercise 2: Term Structure, Forward Interest Rates, and Negative Forward Rate Arbitrage
*Setting (Style of Problem Set 2, Exercises 2.1 & 2.2):*
Consider a market with a term structure of spot interest rates $s_j$ defined over discrete compounding intervals of length $\Delta > 0$, where maturity is $j\Delta$ for $j = 1, 2, \dots$.
Recall the definition of the forward interest rate $f_j$ for the period $[(j-1)\Delta, j\Delta)$ agreed upon at time $0$:
$$ (1+f_j)^\Delta = \frac{(1+s_j)^{j\Delta}}{(1+s_{j-1})^{(j-1)\Delta}} \quad \text{for } j \ge 1, \quad \text{with } s_0 = 0. $$
Using the annual standard indexing of Section 3 (where $(1+s_j)^j = \prod_{k=1}^j (1+f_k)$):
$$ f_j = \frac{(1+s_j)^j}{(1+s_{j-1})^{j-1}} - 1 $$

*Questions:*
1. (PS2 Exercise 2.1)
   a) Prove that the following two conditions are mathematically equivalent:
      - *Condition A:* $(1+s_k)^k \ge (1+s_j)^j$ for all integers $k \ge j \ge 1$.

*** Solution to Practice Exercise 2

*Part 1: Equivalence of Monotonic Compounding and Non-Negative Forward Rates*
a) *Proof of Equivalence (Condition A $\iff$ Condition B):*
Recall from Equation (3.5) of the lecture notes that the compounding factor for any maturity $j$ can be telescoped as a product of forward rates:
\begin{equation}
(1+s_j)^j = (1+f_1)(1+f_2)\cdots(1+f_j) = \prod_{m=1}^j (1+f_m) \tag{3}
\end{equation}
- *($\implies$) Condition A implies Condition B:*
  Suppose $(1+s_k)^k \ge (1+s_j)^j$ for all $k \ge j \ge 1$.
  For $j = 1$, by definition $f_1 = s_1 \ge 0$.
  For any $j > 1$, set $k = j$ and compare with $j - 1$:
  $$ (1+s_j)^j \ge (1+s_{j-1})^{j-1} $$
  Dividing both sides by the strictly positive quantity $(1+s_{j-1})^{j-1} > 0$:
  $$ 1 + f_j = \frac{(1+s_j)^j}{(1+s_{j-1})^{j-1}} \ge 1 \implies f_j \ge 0 $$
  Because $j > 1$ was arbitrary, $f_j \ge 0$ for all $j \ge 1$. Thus Condition B holds.

- *($\impliedby$) Condition B implies Condition A:*
  Suppose $f_m \ge 0$ for all $m \ge 1$. Then $1+f_m \ge 1$ for all $m$.
  Let $k \ge j \ge 1$ be arbitrary integers. Using identity (3):
  $$ (1+s_k)^k = \prod_{m=1}^k (1+f_m) = \left(\prod_{m=1}^j (1+f_m)\right) \left(\prod_{m=j+1}^k (1+f_m)\right) = (1+s_j)^j \prod_{m=j+1}^k (1+f_m) $$
  Because each factor in the second product satisfies $1+f_m \ge 1$, their finite product satisfies:
  $$ \prod_{m=j+1}^k (1+f_m) \ge 1 $$
  Multiplying both sides by $(1+s_j)^j > 0$ yields:
  $$ (1+s_k)^k \ge (1+s_j)^j \quad \text{for all } k \ge j \ge 1 $$
  Thus Condition A holds. The two conditions are mathematically equivalent. $\blacksquare$

b) *Why $f_j < 0$ Implies an Arbitrage Opportunity:*
If $f_j < 0$ for some $j > 1$, then by Part 1:
$$ 1 + f_j = \frac{(1+s_j)^j}{(1+s_{j-1})^{j-1}} < 1 \implies (1+s_j)^j < (1+s_{j-1})^{j-1} $$
This condition states that borrowing for $j$ periods requires repaying *less* total compounded interest than borrowing for $j-1$ periods!
*Economic Money Pump:*
An investor can borrow capital $C > 0$ at time $0$ for $j$ periods at spot rate $s_j$, and simultaneously deposit the same capital $C$ for $j-1$ periods at spot rate $s_{j-1}$.
At time $(j-1)\Delta$, the deposit matures and returns $C(1+s_{j-1})^{j-1}$. The investor withdraws this money and simply holds it as risk-free cash until time $j\Delta$ (earning at least $0\%$ interest, or depositing in a bank at the prevailing positive short rate).
At time $j\Delta$, the loan of $C(1+s_j)^j$ is due. The investor pays off the loan using the cash in hand.
The net wealth remaining at time $j\Delta$ is:
$$ C(1+s_{j-1})^{j-1} - C(1+s_j)^j = C \left[ (1+s_{j-1})^{j-1} - (1+s_j)^j \right] > 0 $$
Because this profit is strictly positive with zero initial capital and zero risk of loss, an arbitrage opportunity exists!

*Part 2: Calculation of Forward Interest Rates*
Using the discrete formula $f_j = \frac{(1+s_j)^j}{(1+s_{j-1})^{j-1}} - 1$:
- For $j = 1$ (maturity $1/2$ year):
  $$ f_1 = s_1 = 0.05 \quad (5.00\%) $$
- For $j = 2$ (maturity $1$ year):
  $$ f_2 = \frac{(1+s_2)^2}{1+s_1} - 1 = \frac{(1+0.04)^2}{1+0.05} - 1 = \frac{1.0816}{1.05} - 1 \approx 0.030095 \quad (\approx 3.01\%) $$
- For $j = 3$ (maturity $3/2$ years):
  $$ f_3 = \frac{(1+s_3)^3}{(1+s_2)^2} - 1 = \frac{(1+0.02)^3}{(1+0.04)^2} - 1 = \frac{1.061208}{1.0816} - 1 \approx -0.018854 \quad (\approx -1.89\%) $$
- For $j = 4$ (maturity $2$ years):
  $$ f_4 = \frac{(1+s_4)^4}{(1+s_3)^3} - 1 = \frac{(1+0.02)^4}{(1+0.02)^3} - 1 = 1+0.02 - 1 = 0.02 \quad (2.00\%) $$

*Conclusion:*
Because $f_3 \approx -1.89\% < 0$, the market violates the fundamental no-arbitrage condition. The market is **not arbitrage-free**!

*Part 3: Arbitrage Trade Execution ($C = 10{,}000$ euros)*
Because $f_3 < 0$, we have $(1+s_3)^3 < (1+s_2)^2$:
- $(1+s_2)^2 = (1.04)^2 = 1.081600$
- $(1+s_3)^3 = (1.02)^3 = 1.061208$

*Step-by-Step Arbitrage Execution:*
1. *At Time $t = 0$:*
   - Take a loan of $C = 10{,}000$ euros with maturity $j = 3$ ($1.5$ years) at spot rate $s_3 = 2\%$. (Cash inflow: $+10{,}000$).
   - Deposit $C = 10{,}000$ euros for $j = 2$ ($1.0$ year) at spot rate $s_2 = 4\%$. (Cash outflow: $-10{,}000$).
   - *Net cash flow at $t = 0$:*
     $$ +10{,}000 - 10{,}000 = 0 \text{ euros} $$
2. *At Time $t = 1.0$ year ($j = 2$):*
   - The deposit matures and pays:
     $$ C(1+s_2)^2 = 10{,}000 \times 1.0816 = 10{,}816.00 \text{ euros} $$
   - Hold this amount in cash (earning $0\%$) until $t = 1.5$ years.
3. *At Time $t = 1.5$ years ($j = 3$):*
   - The 3-period loan is due for repayment:
     $$ C(1+s_3)^3 = 10{,}000 \times 1.061208 = 10{,}612.08 \text{ euros} $$
   - Use the cash in hand ($10{,}816.00$ euros) to repay the loan ($-10{,}612.08$ euros).
   - *Net profit pocketed at $t = 1.5$ years:*
     $$ 10{,}816.00 - 10{,}612.08 = +203.92 \text{ euros} > 0 $$

** Practice Exercise 3: Macaulay Duration, Convexity, and Redington Immunization
*Setting (Style of Problem Set 3, Exercises 3.1 & 3.2, and Section 3.2):*

  $$ V(s) = \sum_{j=1}^T (1+s)^{-j} A_j $$

  $$ D(s) = \frac{\sum_{j=1}^T j (1+s)^{-j} A_j}{V(s)} $$
- The normalized second moment of payment timing is:
  $$ M(s) = \frac{\sum_{j=1}^T j^2 (1+s)^{-j} A_j}{V(s)} $$
- The convexity is:
  $$ C(s) = \frac{V^{\prime\prime}(s)}{V(s)} = \frac{\sum_{j=1}^T j(j+1)(1+s)^{-j-2} A_j}{V(s)} = \frac{M(s) + D(s)}{(1+s)^2} $$

*** Solution to Practice Exercise 3

*Part 1: Macaulay Duration of a Level Annuity*
Define the annuity present value function for unit payment:
$$ f(s) = \sum_{j=1}^T (1+s)^{-j} $$
This is a geometric series with initial term $(1+s)^{-1}$ and common ratio $(1+s)^{-1}$. Using the geometric sum formula:
$$ f(s) = (1+s)^{-1} \frac{1 - (1+s)^{-T}}{1 - (1+s)^{-1}} = \frac{1 - (1+s)^{-T}}{(1+s)[1 - (1+s)^{-1}]} = \frac{1 - (1+s)^{-T}}{s} $$
Differentiate $f(s)$ with respect to $s$:
$$ f\prime(s) = \sum_{j=1}^T (-j)(1+s)^{-j-1} = -(1+s)^{-1} \sum_{j=1}^T j(1+s)^{-j} $$
Rearranging gives:
$$ \sum_{j=1}^T j(1+s)^{-j} = -(1+s) f\prime(s) $$
Because the payments $A_j = A$ are constant, $A$ cancels from both numerator and denominator of $D(s)$:
$$ D(s) = \frac{A \sum_{j=1}^T j(1+s)^{-j}}{A f(s)} = -(1+s) \frac{f\prime(s)}{f(s)} $$
Now compute $f\prime(s)$ by the quotient rule on $f(s) = \frac{1 - (1+s)^{-T}}{s}$:
$$ f\prime(s) = \frac{\frac{d}{ds}[1 - (1+s)^{-T}] \cdot s - [1 - (1+s)^{-T}] \cdot 1}{s^2} = \frac{T(1+s)^{-T-1} s - (1 - (1+s)^{-T})}{s^2} $$
Substituting $f\prime(s)$ and $f(s)$ into the duration formula:
$$ D(s) = -(1+s) \frac{\frac{T(1+s)^{-T-1} s - (1 - (1+s)^{-T})}{s^2}}{\frac{1 - (1+s)^{-T}}{s}} = -(1+s) \left[ \frac{T(1+s)^{-T-1}}{1 - (1+s)^{-T}} - \frac{1}{s} \right] $$
$$ = \frac{1+s}{s} - \frac{T(1+s)^{-T}}{1 - (1+s)^{-T}} = \frac{1+s}{s} - \frac{T}{(1+s)^T - 1} \quad \blacksquare $$

*Part 2: Jensen\prime s Inequality and Duration Derivative*
a) *Proof that $M(s) \ge D(s)^2$:*
Because $(1+s)^{-k} A_k > 0$ and $\sum_{k=1}^T (1+s)^{-k} A_k = V(s)$, the probabilities $p_k = \mathbb{P}(\tau = k) = \frac{(1+s)^{-k} A_k}{V(s)}$ satisfy $p_k > 0$ and $\sum_{k=1}^T p_k = 1$.
Thus, $\tau$ is a well-defined discrete random variable with:
- Expected value:
  $$ \mathbb{E}[\tau] = \sum_{k=1}^T k p_k = \frac{\sum_{k=1}^T k(1+s)^{-k} A_k}{V(s)} = D(s) $$
- Second moment:
  $$ \mathbb{E}[\tau^2] = \sum_{k=1}^T k^2 p_k = \frac{\sum_{k=1}^T k^2(1+s)^{-k} A_k}{V(s)} = M(s) $$
The function $g(x) = x^2$ is strictly convex on $\mathbb{R}$. By Jensen\prime s inequality:
$$ \mathbb{E}[g(\tau)] \ge g(\mathbb{E}[\tau]) \iff \mathbb{E}[\tau^2] \ge (\mathbb{E}[\tau])^2 \iff M(s) \ge D(s)^2 \quad \blacksquare $$
(Equivalently: $\operatorname{Var}(\tau) = M(s) - D(s)^2 \ge 0$).

b) *Derivative $D\prime(s) \le 0$:*
Write $D(s) = \frac{N(s)}{V(s)}$, where $N(s) = \sum_{j=1}^T j(1+s)^{-j} A_j$ and $V(s) = \sum_{j=1}^T (1+s)^{-j} A_j$.
Their derivatives are:
$$ N\prime(s) = -(1+s)^{-1} \sum_{j=1}^T j^2 (1+s)^{-j} A_j = -(1+s)^{-1} V(s) M(s) $$
$$ V\prime(s) = -(1+s)^{-1} \sum_{j=1}^T j (1+s)^{-j} A_j = -(1+s)^{-1} V(s) D(s) $$
Applying the quotient rule:
$$ D\prime(s) = \frac{N\prime(s) V(s) - N(s) V\prime(s)}{[V(s)]^2} = \frac{-(1+s)^{-1} [V(s)]^2 M(s) - V(s) D(s) [-(1+s)^{-1} V(s) D(s)]}{[V(s)]^2} $$
$$ = \frac{1}{1+s} \left[ -M(s) + D(s)^2 \right] = -\frac{M(s) - D(s)^2}{1+s} = -\frac{\operatorname{Var}(\tau)}{1+s} $$
Because $\operatorname{Var}(\tau) \ge 0$ and $1+s > 0$, we have:
$$ D\prime(s) = -\frac{\operatorname{Var}(\tau)}{1+s} \le 0 \quad \text{for all } s > 0 \quad \blacksquare $$
Duration is a non-increasing function of the interest rate (and strictly decreasing whenever payments occur at more than one date).

*Part 3: Redington Immunization Asset-Liability Matching*
a) *Liability Present Value and Duration:*
Since the liability is a single zero-coupon payout of $L = 1{,}000{,}000$ euros at $T_L = 5$ years with $s = 0.05$:
$$ V_L = \frac{1{,}000{,}000}{(1.05)^5} = \frac{1{,}000{,}000}{1.27628156} \approx 783{,}526.17 \text{ euros} $$
The Macaulay duration of any single cash flow is simply its maturity:
$$ D_L = 5.00 \text{ years} $$

b) *First Two Redington Conditions ($V_A = V_L$ and $D_A = D_L$):*
For zero-coupon bonds, durations are $D_1 = 1$ year and $D_2 = 10$ years.
Let $w_1$ be the fraction of total asset wealth invested in Bond 1, and $w_2 = 1 - w_1$ in Bond 2.
The portfolio duration is:
$$ D_A = w_1 D_1 + w_2 D_2 = w_1(1) + (1 - w_1)(10) = 10 - 9w_1 $$
Equating $D_A = D_L = 5$:
$$ 10 - 9w_1 = 5 \implies 9w_1 = 5 \implies w_1 = \frac{5}{9} \approx 55.56\% $$
$$ w_2 = 1 - w_1 = \frac{4}{9} \approx 44.44\% $$
Total capital invested in assets is $V_A = V_L = 783{,}526.17$ euros.
The monetary allocations are:
- Invest in Bond 1: $w_1 V_A = \frac{5}{9} \times 783{,}526.17 \approx 435{,}292.32$ euros.
- Invest in Bond 2: $w_2 V_A = \frac{4}{9} \times 783{,}526.17 \approx 348{,}233.85$ euros.

c) *Convexity Verification ($C_A > C_L$):*
The convexity of a zero-coupon bond of maturity $T$ under yield $s = 0.05$ is:
$$ C(T) = \frac{T(T+1)}{(1+s)^2} = \frac{T(T+1)}{(1.05)^2} = \frac{T(T+1)}{1.1025} $$
- Liability convexity ($T_L = 5$):
  $$ C_L = \frac{5 \times 6}{1.1025} = \frac{30}{1.1025} \approx 27.21 $$
- Bond 1 convexity ($T_1 = 1$):
  $$ C_1 = \frac{1 \times 2}{1.1025} = \frac{2}{1.1025} \approx 1.814 $$
- Bond 2 convexity ($T_2 = 10$):
  $$ C_2 = \frac{10 \times 11}{1.1025} = \frac{110}{1.1025} \approx 99.773 $$
- Portfolio convexity:
  $$ C_A = w_1 C_1 + w_2 C_2 = \frac{5}{9}(1.814) + \frac{4}{9}(99.773) = 1.008 + 44.344 = 45.35 $$
Comparing convexities:
$$ C_A = 45.35 > 27.21 = C_L $$
*Economic Guarantee of Redington Immunization:*
By the second-order Taylor expansion of net portfolio surplus $S(s) = V_A(s) - V_L(s)$:
$$ \Delta S \approx -(V_A D_A - V_L D_L) \Delta s + \frac{1}{2} [V_A C_A - V_L C_L] (\Delta s)^2 $$
Because $V_A = V_L = V$ and $D_A = D_L$, the linear first-order term is exactly zero.
The surplus change simplifies to:
$$ \Delta S \approx \frac{1}{2} V (C_A - C_L) (\Delta s)^2 = \frac{1}{2}(783{,}526)(45.35 - 27.21)(\Delta s)^2 = +7{,}106{,}582 (\Delta s)^2 \ge 0 $$
Because $(\Delta s)^2 > 0$ for *any* shift $\Delta s \ne 0$ (whether rates rise or fall), the assets gain more value than liabilities when rates drop, and lose less value than liabilities when rates rise. The insurer is protected against any small parallel shift in yield! $\blacksquare$

*** Solution to Practice Exercise 4

*Part 1: Formal Definition & Interpretation*
By Definition 5.1 of the lecture notes:
A portfolio $\theta \in \mathbb{R}^N$ is an arbitrage opportunity if:
1. $S(0)\theta \le 0$: The strategy requires no net investment upfront ($S(0)\theta = 0$), or it generates immediate cash in hand today ($S(0)\theta < 0$).
2. $S(1)\theta \ge 0$ a.s.: In every state $\omega_m \in \Omega$ (since $\mathbb{P}(\{\omega_m\}) > 0$), $(S(1)\theta)_m \ge 0$. The investor is completely protected against any loss at time $1$.
3. $\mathbb{P}(S(1)\theta > 0) > 0$: There is at least one state with positive probability where $(S(1)\theta)_m > 0$. The investor has a positive chance of making a gain.
*Economic meaning:* An arbitrage opportunity is a self-financing money pump — guaranteed non-negative wealth with zero risk and a positive probability of profit.

*Part 2: Constructing Arbitrage Portfolio when $p = 20$*
Notice the terminal payoff relation:
$$ S_3(1) = \begin{pmatrix} 20 \\ 30 \\ 40 \end{pmatrix} = 0.5 \begin{pmatrix} 40 \\ 60 \\ 80 \end{pmatrix} = 0.5 S_2(1) $$
Under no arbitrage (Law of One Price / FTAP 1), their initial prices must satisfy:
$$ S_3(0) = 0.5 S_2(0) = 0.5(50) = 25 $$
When $p = 20$, the derivative is strictly underpriced relative to the stock ($20 < 25$).
*Strategy:* Buy 1 unit of Security 3 (cost: $-20$) and sell short $0.5$ units of Security 2 (inflow: $+0.5 \times 50 = +25$).
Portfolio: $\theta = (\theta_1, \theta_2, \theta_3)^T = (0, -0.5, 1)^T$.
1. Initial cash flow:
   $$ S(0)\theta = 0(1) - 0.5(50) + 1(20) = -25 + 20 = -5 < 0 $$
   The investor pockets $+5$ euros cash immediately at time $0$!
2. Terminal payoff in every state $\omega_m$:
   - State $\omega_1$: $-0.5(40) + 1(20) = 0$.
   - State $\omega_2$: $-0.5(60) + 1(30) = 0$.
   - State $\omega_3$: $-0.5(80) + 1(40) = 0$.
Here $S(0)\theta = -5 < 0$ and $S(1)\theta = 0$ a.s., so $\theta$ is a pure arbitrage opportunity under Definition 5.1!

*Part 3: Reinvestment via Remark 5.2*
Pocket the $+5$ euros cash upfront at time $0$ and invest all of it into Security 1 (the bond with rate $i = 10\%$):
$$ \tilde{\theta} = \theta + 5 e_1 = \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ -0.5 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix} + \begin{pmatrix} 5 \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 5 \\ -0.5 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix} $$
1. Initial cost:
   $$ S(0)\tilde{\theta} = 5 S_1(0) - 0.5 S_2(0) + 1 S_3(0) = 5(1) - 25 + 20 = 0 $$
   The strategy requires exactly zero capital today!
2. Terminal payoff at time $1$:
   $$ S(1)\tilde{\theta} = 5 S_1(1) + S(1)\theta = 5(1.10) + \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 5.50 \\ 5.50 \\ 5.50 \end{pmatrix} > 0 $$
   In every state $\omega_m$, the portfolio pays $+5.50$ euros of risk-free cash.
Thus: $S(0)\tilde{\theta} = 0$, $S(1)\tilde{\theta} \ge 0$ a.s., and $\mathbb{P}(S(1)\tilde{\theta} > 0) = 1 > 0$.
This confirms Remark 5.2: an upfront arbitrage with $S(0)\theta < 0$ is completely equivalent to a zero-cost arbitrage with $S(0)\tilde{\theta} = 0$!

*Part 4: Evaluating Portfolio $\eta = (10, -1, 1)^T$*
- Initial cost:
  $$ S(0)\eta = 10(1) - 1(50) + 1(20) = 10 - 50 + 20 = -20 < 0 $$
  (Immediate cash inflow $+20$).
- Terminal payoff at time $1$:
  - State $\omega_1$: $10(1.10) - 1(40) + 1(20) = 11 - 40 + 20 = -9 < 0$.
  - State $\omega_2$: $10(1.10) - 1(60) + 1(30) = 11 - 60 + 30 = -19 < 0$.
  - State $\omega_3$: $10(1.10) - 1(80) + 1(40) = 11 - 80 + 40 = -29 < 0$.
In every state of nature, $S(1)\eta < 0$.
Even though the investor received cash upfront, they face a guaranteed loss at time $1$.
Condition 2 of Definition 5.1 requires $S(1)\theta \ge 0$ a.s., which fails completely here.
Therefore, $\eta$ is NOT an arbitrage opportunity! $\blacksquare$

** Practice Exercise 5: Arbitrage Detection, State Price Vectors, and Constructing Arbitrage Portfolios
*Setting (Style of Problem Set 4, Exercises 4.1, 4.2, 4.4):*
Consider a one-period market with three states of nature: $\Omega = \{\omega_1, \omega_2, \omega_3\}$.
There are three securities traded at time $0$:
- Security 1 (Risk-free Bond): $S_1(0) = 1$, terminal payoff $S_1(1) = (1, 1, 1)^T$ (interest rate $i = 0$).
- Security 2 (Stock A): $S_2(0) = 6$, terminal payoff $S_2(1) = (3, 6, 9)^T$.
- Security 3 (Stock B): $S_3(0) = p$, terminal payoff $S_3(1) = (9, 6, 3)^T$.

*Questions:*
1. State the linear system of equations that a state price vector $\psi = (\psi_1, \psi_2, \psi_3)^T$ must satisfy for this market.
2. Find all strictly positive state price vectors $\psi \gg 0$ as a function of the price $p$.
3. Determine the exact price $p$ for which the market is arbitrage-free.
4. Suppose $p = 10$. Show that the market admits an arbitrage opportunity and construct an explicit portfolio $\theta = (\theta_1, \theta_2, \theta_3)^T$ that achieves an arbitrage.

*** Solution to Practice Exercise 5

*Part 1: The Linear System for State Prices*
By Theorem 5.3 (FTAP 1), the market is arbitrage-free if and only if there exists a vector $\psi = (\psi_1, \psi_2, \psi_3)^T$ with $\psi_1 > 0, \psi_2 > 0, \psi_3 > 0$ such that $S(0) = S(1)^T \psi$:
\begin{equation}
1 = \psi_1 + \psi_2 + \psi_3 \tag{1}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
6 = 3\psi_1 + 6\psi_2 + 9\psi_3 \tag{2}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
p = 9\psi_1 + 6\psi_2 + 3\psi_3 \tag{3}
\end{equation}

*Part 2: Solving for $\psi$*
Divide Equation (2) by 3:
\begin{equation}
2 = \psi_1 + 2\psi_2 + 3\psi_3 \tag{2'}
\end{equation}
Subtract Equation (1) from (2'):
$$ 2 - 1 = (\psi_1 - \psi_1) + (2\psi_2 - \psi_2) + (3\psi_3 - \psi_3) \implies 1 = \psi_2 + 2\psi_3 \implies \psi_2 = 1 - 2\psi_3 $$
Substitute $\psi_2 = 1 - 2\psi_3$ into Equation (1):
$$ \psi_1 + (1 - 2\psi_3) + \psi_3 = 1 \implies \psi_1 - \psi_3 = 0 \implies \psi_1 = \psi_3 $$
For $\psi$ to be strictly positive ($\psi \gg 0$):
- $\psi_3 > 0$
- $\psi_2 = 1 - 2\psi_3 > 0 \implies \psi_3 < 1/2$.
Thus, any $\psi_3 \in (0, 1/2)$ yields a strictly positive state price vector:
$$ \psi = (\psi_3, 1 - 2\psi_3, \psi_3)^T, \quad \psi_3 \in (0, 1/2) $$

Now substitute these into Equation (3) to find the required price $p$:
$$ p = 9\psi_1 + 6\psi_2 + 3\psi_3 = 9\psi_3 + 6(1 - 2\psi_3) + 3\psi_3 = 6 + (9 - 12 + 3)\psi_3 = 6 $$
*Conclusion:* The state price equation requires $p = 6$ exactly. When $p = 6$, there are infinitely many state price vectors parameterized by $\psi_3 \in (0, 1/2)$.

*Part 3: Arbitrage-Free Condition*
The market is arbitrage-free if and only if $p = 6$. For any $p \ne 6$, no positive state price vector exists, so the market admits arbitrage.

*Part 4: Arbitrage Construction when $p = 10$*
Notice that terminal payoffs satisfy:
$$ S_2(1) + S_3(1) = \begin{pmatrix} 3 \\ 6 \\ 9 \end{pmatrix} + \begin{pmatrix} 9 \\ 6 \\ 3 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 12 \\ 12 \\ 12 \end{pmatrix} = 12 S_1(1) $$
Therefore, a portfolio of 1 unit of Stock A and 1 unit of Stock B perfectly replicates 12 units of the Bond in every state.
Under no arbitrage, their prices must satisfy:
$$ S_2(0) + S_3(0) = 12 S_1(0) = 12 $$
When $p = 10$, $S_2(0) + S_3(0) = 6 + 10 = 16 > 12$. The risky stocks are collectively overpriced relative to the bond!

*Arbitrage Strategy:*
1. Sell (short) 1 unit of Stock A and 1 unit of Stock B at time $0$, receiving $+6 + 10 = +16$.
2. Buy 12 units of the Bond at time $0$, costing $-12(1) = -12$.
3. Net cash pocketed at time $0$:
   $$ +16 - 12 = +4 > 0 $$
4. Terminal payoff at time $1$ in every state:
   $$ 12 S_1(1) - S_2(1) - S_3(1) = 12 - (S_2(1) + S_3(1)) = 12 - 12 = 0 $$
The portfolio $\theta = (12, -1, -1)^T$ yields $+4$ riskless cash upfront with identically zero future liabilities. This is a pure arbitrage opportunity! $\blacksquare$

#+begin_quote
*Exam Tip:* Whenever two risky assets sum to a constant multiple of the riskless bond (here $S_2(1) + S_3(1) = 12 S_1(1)$), their initial prices MUST sum to that same constant multiple ($S_2(0) + S_3(0) = 12 S_1(0)$). If they do not, you can immediately construct an arbitrage with zero terminal risk!
#+end_quote

** Practice Exercise 6: Full Mathematical Proof of Theorem 5.3 (FTAP 1)
*Setting (Core Theoretical Proof in Section 5.1.1):*
Consider a one-period market with $N$ traded securities and a finite sample space $\Omega = \{\omega_1, \dots, \omega_M\}$, with $\mathbb{P}(\{\omega_m\}) > 0$ for all $m = 1, \dots, M$.
The price vector at time $0$ is $S(0) \in \mathbb{R}^N$ and the payoff in state $\omega_m$ is $S(1, \omega_m) \in \mathbb{R}^N$.

*Questions:*
1. Formulate the First Fundamental Theorem of Asset Pricing (Theorem 5.3) for this market.
2. Define the mapping $L: \mathbb{R}^N \to \mathbb{R}^{M+1}$ and the image subspace $V = L(\mathbb{R}^N)$ used in the proof.
   Show that the market is arbitrage-free (Definition 5.1) if and only if:
   $$ V \cap \mathbb{R}_+^{M+1} = \{0\} $$
3. State Corollary 5.5 (Variant of Farkas' Lemma for linear subspaces).
   Apply it to $V$ to prove that absence of arbitrage implies the existence of a strictly positive state price vector $\psi \gg 0$ such that $S(0) = S(1)^T \psi$.
4. Prove the converse direction: If there exists a state price vector $\psi \gg 0$ such that $S(0) = S(1)^T \psi$, then the market is arbitrage-free.

*** Solution to Practice Exercise 6

*Part 1: Statement of Theorem 5.3*
#+begin_quote
The one-period market model is *arbitrage-free* if and only if there exists a strictly positive state price vector $\psi = (\psi_1, \dots, \psi_M)^T \gg 0$ ($\psi_m > 0$ for all $m = 1, \dots, M$) such that:
$$ S(0) = S(1)^T \psi \quad \left(\text{i.e. } S_n(0) = \sum_{m=1}^M \psi_m S_n(1, \omega_m) \quad \forall n = 1, \dots, N\right) $$
Equivalently, there exists a strictly positive deflator $\varphi(\omega_m) = \frac{\psi_m}{\mathbb{P}(\{\omega_m\})} > 0$ such that $S(0) = \mathbb{E}[\varphi S(1)]$.
#+end_quote

*Part 2: Geometric Reformulation via Subspace $V$*
Define the mapping $L: \mathbb{R}^N \to \mathbb{R}^{M+1}$ by:
$$ L(\theta) = \begin{pmatrix} -S(0)\theta \\ S(1, \omega_1)\theta \\ \vdots \\ S(1, \omega_M)\theta \end{pmatrix} \in \mathbb{R}^{M+1} $$
Because matrix multiplication is linear, $L$ is a linear mapping, and its image:
$$ V := L(\mathbb{R}^N) = \{ L(\theta) \mid \theta \in \mathbb{R}^N \} $$
is a linear subspace of $\mathbb{R}^{M+1}$.
Now examine what an arbitrage opportunity means for the vector $L(\theta)$:
By Definition 5.1, $\theta$ is an arbitrage opportunity if:
- $S(0)\theta \le 0 \iff -S(0)\theta \ge 0$ (the 0-th component is non-negative),
- $S(1, \omega_m)\theta \ge 0$ for all $m = 1, \dots, M$ (all remaining $M$ components are non-negative),
- With at least one strict inequality: either $-S(0)\theta > 0$ or $S(1, \omega_m)\theta > 0$ for some $m$.
This means $L(\theta)$ is a non-zero vector in the non-negative orthant $\mathbb{R}_+^{M+1}$:
$$ \theta \text{ is an arbitrage opportunity} \iff L(\theta) \in \mathbb{R}_+^{M+1} \setminus \{0\} $$
Therefore:
$$ \text{The market is arbitrage-free} \iff V \cap (\mathbb{R}_+^{M+1} \setminus \{0\}) = \emptyset \iff V \cap \mathbb{R}_+^{M+1} = \{0\} $$

*Part 3: Applying Farkas' Lemma to Derive $\psi \gg 0$*
#+begin_quote
*Corollary 5.5 (Variant of Farkas' Lemma):*
Let $V \subseteq \mathbb{R}^K$ be a linear subspace. If $V \cap \mathbb{R}_+^K = \{0\}$, there exists a strictly positive vector $z = (z_1, \dots, z_K)^T \gg 0$ ($z_j > 0$ for all $j$) such that $z^T y = 0$ for all $y \in V$.
#+end_quote
Here $K = M + 1$. Since the market is arbitrage-free, $V \cap \mathbb{R}_+^{M+1} = \{0\}$.
By Corollary 5.5, there exists a vector $z = (z_0, z_1, \dots, z_M)^T \in \mathbb{R}^{M+1}$ with:
$$ z_0 > 0, \quad z_1 > 0, \quad \dots, \quad z_M > 0 $$
such that $z^T y = 0$ for all $y \in V$.
Since every $y \in V$ is of the form $L(\theta)$ for some $\theta \in \mathbb{R}^N$:
$$ z^T L(\theta) = -z_0(S(0)\theta) + \sum_{m=1}^M z_m(S(1, \omega_m)\theta) = 0 \quad \text{for all } \theta \in \mathbb{R}^N $$
Rearranging:
$$ z_0 S(0)\theta = \left(\sum_{m=1}^M z_m S(1, \omega_m)\right)\theta \quad \text{for all } \theta \in \mathbb{R}^N $$
Because this equality holds for all choices of portfolio $\theta \in \mathbb{R}^N$, the coefficient vectors must be identical:
$$ z_0 S(0) = \sum_{m=1}^M z_m S(1, \omega_m) = S(1)^T \begin{pmatrix} z_1 \\ \vdots \\ z_M \end{pmatrix} $$
Because $z_0 > 0$, divide both sides by $z_0$:
$$ S(0) = \sum_{m=1}^M \frac{z_m}{z_0} S(1, \omega_m) $$
Define the state prices by:
$$ \psi_m := \frac{z_m}{z_0} > 0 \quad \text{for all } m = 1, \dots, M $$
Then $\psi = (\psi_1, \dots, \psi_M)^T \gg 0$ is strictly positive and satisfies:
$$ S(0) = S(1)^T \psi $$
Furthermore, setting $\varphi(\omega_m) = \frac{\psi_m}{\mathbb{P}(\{\omega_m\})} > 0$, we obtain the strictly positive deflator:
$$ S(0) = \mathbb{E}[\varphi S(1)] $$

*Part 4: Proving the Converse Direction $(\impliedby)$*
Assume there exists a state price vector $\psi \in \mathbb{R}^M$ with $\psi \gg 0$ such that $S(0) = S(1)^T \psi$.
Suppose for contradiction that there exists an arbitrage opportunity $\theta \in \mathbb{R}^N$.
By Definition 5.1:
1. $S(0)\theta \le 0$,
2. $(S(1)\theta)_m \ge 0$ for all $m = 1, \dots, M$,
3. There exists at least one state $\omega_{m^*}$ such that $(S(1)\theta)_{m^*} > 0$ with $\mathbb{P}(\{\omega_{m^*}\}) > 0$.
Now evaluate the portfolio cost $S(0)\theta$ using the state price vector:
$$ S(0)\theta = (S(1)^T \psi)^T \theta = \psi^T (S(1)\theta) = \sum_{m=1}^M \psi_m (S(1)\theta)_m $$
Notice that:
- Every $\psi_m > 0$,
- Every $(S(1)\theta)_m \ge 0$,
- For the state $\omega_{m^*}$, $\psi_{m^*} (S(1)\theta)_{m^*} > 0$.
Therefore, the sum is strictly positive:
$$ S(0)\theta = \sum_{m=1}^M \psi_m (S(1)\theta)_m \ge \psi_{m^*} (S(1)\theta)_{m^*} > 0 $$
This proves that $S(0)\theta > 0$.
However, Condition 1 of Definition 5.1 required $S(0)\theta \le 0$.
A number cannot be both strictly positive and non-positive ($S(0)\theta > 0$ and $S(0)\theta \le 0$), a contradiction!
Hence, no such portfolio $\theta$ can exist, and the market is strictly arbitrage-free! $\blacksquare$

** Practice Exercise 7: Two-Period Binomial Tree, Delta-Hedging, and Self-Financing Verification
*Setting (Style of Problem Set 6, Exercises 6.1–6.4):*
Consider a two-period binomial market ($t = 0, 1, 2$).
- Interest rate: $r = 10\%$ per period ($S_1(0) = 1, S_1(1) = 1.10, S_1(2) = 1.21$).
- Stock: Initial price $S_2(0) = 100$.
  At each step, the stock price moves up by factor $u = 1.30$ or down by factor $d = 0.90$.
  (Notice $0.90 < 1.10 < 1.30$).
- Target claim: A European Call option expiring at $t = 2$ with strike price $K = 110$.

*Questions:*
1. Calculate the unique risk-neutral probability $q$.
2. Compute the stock prices and terminal option payoffs at $t = 2$.
3. Using backward induction, compute the option price at time $1$ ($V_u, V_d$) and at time $0$ ($V_0$).
4. Calculate the replicating portfolio $\theta(1) = (\theta_1(1), \theta_2(1))$ held during the first period.
5. Calculate the replicating portfolio $\theta(2)$ held during the second period at node $u$.
6. *Verify explicitly that the self-financing condition holds at $t = 1$ at node $u$:*
   $$ S(1)\theta(2) = S(1)\theta(1) $$

*** Solution to Practice Exercise 7

*Part 1: Risk-Neutral Probability*
By the martingale condition $1+r = q u + (1-q) d$:
$$ q = \frac{1 + r - d}{u - d} = \frac{1.10 - 0.90}{1.30 - 0.90} = \frac{0.20}{0.40} = \frac{1}{2} = 0.50 $$
$$ 1 - q = 0.50 $$

*Part 2: Stock Prices and Terminal Payoffs at $t = 2$*
- At $t = 0$: $S_2(0) = 100$.
- At $t = 1$:
  - Up node $u$: $S_u = 100 \times 1.30 = 130$.
  - Down node $d$: $S_d = 100 \times 0.90 = 90$.
- At $t = 2$:
  - Node $uu$: $S_{uu} = 130 \times 1.30 = 169$. Payoff: $V_{uu} = (169 - 110)^+ = 59$.
  - Node $ud$: $S_{ud} = 130 \times 0.90 = 117$. Payoff: $V_{ud} = (117 - 110)^+ = 7$.
  - Node $dd$: $S_{dd} = 90 \times 0.90 = 81$. Payoff: $V_{dd} = (81 - 110)^+ = 0$.

*Part 3: Backward Induction Valuation*
- At node $u$ ($t = 1$):
  $$ V_u = \frac{1}{1+r}[q V_{uu} + (1-q) V_{ud}] = \frac{1}{1.10}[0.5(59) + 0.5(7)] = \frac{29.5 + 3.5}{1.10} = \frac{33}{1.10} = 30 $$
- At node $d$ ($t = 1$):
  $$ V_d = \frac{1}{1+r}[q V_{ud} + (1-q) V_{dd}] = \frac{1}{1.10}[0.5(7) + 0.5(0)] = \frac{3.5}{1.10} = \frac{35}{11} \approx 3.1818 $$
- At time $0$:
  $$ V_0 = \frac{1}{1+r}[q V_u + (1-q) V_d] = \frac{10}{11}\left[15 + \frac{35}{22}\right] = \frac{1825}{121} \approx 15.0826 $$

*Part 4: Replicating Portfolio in First Period $\theta(1)$*
- Stock holding:
  $$ \theta_2(1) = \frac{V_u - V_d}{S_u - S_d} = \frac{30 - 35/11}{130 - 90} = \frac{295/11}{40} = \frac{59}{88} \approx 0.6705 \text{ shares} $$
- Bond holding:
  $$ \theta_1(1) = \frac{V_0 - \theta_2(1) S_2(0)}{S_1(0)} = \frac{1825}{121} - \frac{1475}{22} = -\frac{12575}{242} \approx -51.9628 $$

*Part 5: Replicating Portfolio in Second Period $\theta(2)$ at Node $u$*
At node $u$ ($S_u = 130$):
- Stock holding for period 2:
  $$ \theta_2^u(2) = \frac{V_{uu} - V_{ud}}{S_{uu} - S_{ud}} = \frac{59 - 7}{169 - 117} = \frac{52}{52} = 1.00 \text{ share} $$
- Bond holding for period 2:
  $$ \theta_1^u(2) = \frac{V_u - \theta_2^u(2) S_u}{S_1(1)} = \frac{30 - (1.00)(130)}{1.10} = \frac{-100}{1.10} = -\frac{1000}{11} \approx -90.9091 $$

*Part 6: Verification of the Self-Financing Condition at Node $u$*
At $t = 1$, the portfolio held over the first period $\theta(1)$ has value:
$$ S(1)\theta(1) = \theta_1(1) S_1(1) + \theta_2(1) S_u = \left(-\frac{12575}{242}\right)(1.10) + \left(\frac{59}{88}\right)(130) $$
$$ = -\frac{12575}{220} + \frac{7670}{88} = -\frac{2515}{44} + \frac{3835}{44} = \frac{1320}{44} = 30 = V_u $$
The value of the rebalanced portfolio $\theta(2)$ at the same time $t = 1$ is:
$$ S(1)\theta(2) = \theta_1^u(2) S_1(1) + \theta_2^u(2) S_u = \left(-\frac{1000}{11}\right)(1.10) + (1.00)(130) = -100 + 130 = 30 = V_u $$
Since:
$$ S(1)\theta(1) = S(1)\theta(2) = 30 $$
*The self-financing condition holds identically:* exactly zero capital is injected or withdrawn when rebalancing from $\theta(1)$ to $\theta(2)$! $\blacksquare$
