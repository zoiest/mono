* The Deterministic Interest Rate Model

** Time Discretization and Compounding Mechanism
Let $\Delta > 0$ denote the length of a single discrete time period (e.g., $\Delta = 1$ year, $\Delta = 1/2$ half-year, or $\Delta = 1/12$ month). The timeline is discretized into points:
$$ 0, \Delta, 2\Delta, \dots, T\Delta $$

The fundamental assumptions of the deterministic interest rate model are:
1. *Constant periodic rate:* During each time period $[(j-1)\Delta, j\Delta)$, $j = 1, 2, \dots$, the annualized interest rate is a constant $i_j > -1$, which is fixed and known at time $0$.
2. *Periodic return formula:* A capital deposit $C$ made at time $(j-1)\Delta$ grows by time $j\Delta$ to:
   $$ C(1 + i_j)^\Delta $$
   Equation (2.1) represents the discrete compounding factor over period length $\Delta$.
3. *Multi-period compounding:* If the deposit is maintained over $n$ consecutive periods $[0, n\Delta)$, the capital accumulates according to the compounding principle:
   $$ C \prod_{k=1}^n (1 + i_k)^\Delta $$

#+begin_quote
*Remark 2.1 (Alternative Compounding Conventions):*
In financial practice, a nominal yearly rate $i$ compounded $m = 1/\Delta$ times per year often uses the linear periodic rate factor $1 + i\Delta = 1 + i/m$.
Under continuous compounding at instantaneous rate $r$, the accumulation factor over time $t$ is $e^{rt}$.
Throughout this course, the standard compounding mechanism is strictly defined by Equation (2.1):
$$ (1 + i_j)^\Delta $$
#+end_quote

* Cash Flows and Arbitrage-Free Valuation

** Definition of Cash Flow
A *cash flow* is a sequence of deterministic cash payments occurring at discrete times $j\Delta$:
$$ B(\Delta), B(2\Delta), \dots, B(T\Delta) $$
where $B(j\Delta) > 0$ denotes a cash inflow (payment received by the holder) and $B(j\Delta) < 0$ denotes a cash outflow (payment made by the holder).

** Theorem 2.1 (Present Value of Cash Flows)
#+begin_quote
*Theorem 2.1 (Arbitrage-Free Price of a Cash Flow):*
Suppose the interest rate market satisfies the assumptions of Section 2.1. Assume further that the market is *arbitrage-free* (no riskless profit can be generated from zero initial wealth).
Then the unique price (present value) of the cash flow $B(\Delta), \dots, B(T\Delta)$ at time $0$, denoted $V(0)$, must be given by:
$$ V(0) = \sum_{j=1}^T \left[ \prod_{k=1}^j (1 + i_k) \right]^{-\Delta} B(j\Delta) \tag{2.4} $$
#+end_quote

*** Complete Mathematical Proof
We prove Theorem 2.1 by contradiction, showing that if the market price $V$ of the cash flow at time $0$ differs from $V(0)$, an investor can construct a self-financing arbitrage strategy that generates guaranteed strictly positive profit with zero net risk.

*Case 1: Suppose $V > V(0)$ (Cash flow is overpriced on the market).*
The investor executes the following strategy at time $0$:
1. *Sell the cash flow* in the market for price $V$, receiving cash $+V$.
2. *Open $T$ synthetic bank deposit accounts:* For each $j = 1, \dots, T$, deposit an amount:
   $$ D_j = \left[ \prod_{k=1}^j (1 + i_k) \right]^{-\Delta} B(j\Delta) $$
   The total capital required to fund these $T$ deposits at time $0$ is:
   $$ \sum_{j=1}^T D_j = V(0) $$
3. *Immediate net cash flow at time $0$:*
   $$ V - V(0) > 0 $$
   The investor securely pockets this positive surplus at time $0$.
4. *Payoffs at times $j\Delta$ ($j = 1, \dots, T$):*
   From account $j$, the capital accumulated at time $j\Delta$ is:
   $$ D_j \prod_{k=1}^j (1 + i_k)^\Delta = B(j\Delta) $$
   The investor withdraws this exact amount $B(j\Delta)$ from account $j$ to fulfill the contractual obligation to the buyer of the cash flow.
5. *Net cash flow at all future times:*
   $$ B(j\Delta) - B(j\Delta) = 0 \quad \text{for all } j = 1, \dots, T $$
Thus, the investor earned a riskless positive profit $V - V(0) > 0$ at time $0$ with zero future liabilities, which is an arbitrage opportunity. This contradicts market arbitrage freedom.

*Case 2: Suppose $V < V(0)$ (Cash flow is underpriced on the market).*
The investor executes the reverse strategy:
1. *Borrow funds to buy the cash flow:* Borrow capital $V$ at time $0$ by issuing $T$ fractional loans:
   $$ L_j = \left[ \prod_{k=1}^j (1 + i_k) \right]^{-\Delta} B(j\Delta) \cdot \frac{V}{V(0)} $$
   so that $\sum_{j=1}^T L_j = V$.
2. *Buy the cash flow* at market price $V$, so net initial investment is $V - V = 0$.
3. *At times $j\Delta$ ($j = 1, \dots, T$):*
   The cash flow delivers payment $B(j\Delta)$. The loan repayment owed on loan $j$ is:
   $$ L_j \prod_{k=1}^j (1 + i_k)^\Delta = B(j\Delta) \frac{V}{V(0)} < B(j\Delta) \quad (\text{since } V < V(0)) $$
4. *Surplus profit at times $j\Delta$:*
   $$ B(j\Delta) - L_j \prod_{k=1}^j (1 + i_k)^\Delta = B(j\Delta) \left(1 - \frac{V}{V(0)}\right) > 0 $$
All future cash flows are non-negative, and at least one is strictly positive, with zero initial outlay. This is an arbitrage opportunity, again contradicting arbitrage freedom.

Therefore, we must have $V = V(0)$, completing the proof. $\blacksquare$

* Intermediate Valuation and Forward Contracts

** Value of Remaining Cash Flow at Time $j\Delta$
At an intermediate time $j\Delta$ ($1 \le j < T$), the remaining cash payments occur at $(j+1)\Delta, \dots, T\Delta$.
The arbitrage-free value of the remaining cash flow at time $j\Delta$, denoted $V(j)$, is obtained by discounting all future payments back to time $j\Delta$:
$$ V(j) = \sum_{k=j+1}^T \left[ \prod_{m=j+1}^k (1 + i_m) \right]^{-\Delta} B(k\Delta) \tag{2.7} $$

** Forward Agreements (Forward Deals)
#+begin_quote
*Definition 2.1 (Forward Contract):*
A forward contract agreed at time $0$ for delivery at time $j\Delta$ is an agreement to purchase the remaining cash flow $B((j+1)\Delta), \dots, B(T\Delta)$ at delivery date $j\Delta$ for a pre-agreed delivery price $W(j)$, with no money changing hands at time $0$ (price of the agreement at time $0$ is $0$).
#+end_quote

By Theorem 2.1 and the Law of One Price:
The value of the forward commitment discounted to time $0$ must equal the present value of the underlying payments:
$$ W(j) \left[ \prod_{k=1}^j (1 + i_k) \right]^{-\Delta} = \sum_{k=j+1}^T \left[ \prod_{m=1}^k (1 + i_m) \right]^{-\Delta} B(k\Delta) $$
Multiplying both sides by $\prod_{k=1}^j (1 + i_k)^\Delta$ yields:
$$ W(j) = \sum_{k=j+1}^T \left[ \prod_{m=j+1}^k (1 + i_m) \right]^{-\Delta} B(k\Delta) = V(j) $$
Thus, the forward price $W(j)$ agreed at time $0$ equals the forward-projected present value $V(j)$.

* Worked Examples from Lecture Notes

** Example 2.1 (Loan Amortization)
An agent borrows principal $L$ at time $0$ and amortizes the debt in two installments: payment $L_1$ at time $\Delta$ and payment $L_2$ at time $2\Delta$.
The borrower's cash flow is:
$$ B(0) = +L, \quad B(\Delta) = -L_1, \quad B(2\Delta) = -L_2 $$
By Theorem 2.1, the present value of repayments must equal the borrowed amount $L$:
$$ L = L_1 (1 + i_1)^{-\Delta} + L_2 [(1 + i_1)(1 + i_2)]^{-\Delta} $$

** Example 2.2 (Replication of Cash Flows)
Consider two basic cash flows available on the market:
- Cash flow I: $B_1(\Delta) = 6, B_1(2\Delta) = 6$, market price $p$.
- Cash flow II: $B_2(\Delta) = 5, B_2(2\Delta) = 8$, market price $q$.

We wish to replicate Cash flow III: $B_3(\Delta) = 0, B_3(2\Delta) = 14$.
We seek portfolio weights $\theta_1, \theta_2$ such that:
$$ \begin{pmatrix} 6 & 5 \\ 6 & 8 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} \theta_1 \\ \theta_2 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 14 \end{pmatrix} $$
From $6\theta_1 + 5\theta_2 = 0 \implies \theta_1 = -\frac{5}{6}\theta_2$.
Substituting into the second equation:
$$ 6\left(-\frac{5}{6}\theta_2\right) + 8\theta_2 = 14 \implies 3\theta_2 = 14 \implies \theta_2 = \frac{14}{3}, \quad \theta_1 = -\frac{35}{9} $$
By the Law of One Price (absence of arbitrage), the price of Cash flow III must be:
$$ \text{Price}(\text{III}) = \theta_1 p + \theta_2 q = -\frac{35}{9} p + \frac{14}{3} q $$
